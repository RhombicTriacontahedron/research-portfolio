# Rent as a Claim on Living Standards: An Eight-Century History and Its Lesson for Housing Policy
Carlos Galindo Escajeda
June 22, 2026

## Abstract

Rent is the oldest income in economics and, today, the form in which the
asset economy presses most directly on household living standards. This
paper reconstructs eight centuries of rent — from direct labour dues, to
a share of the harvest, to money, to a capitalised financial claim — and
argues that beneath the changing form one function has held: rent is an
unearned claim on a necessity in fixed supply, a deduction from the
resources households need to secure shelter, levied by control rather
than by provision. Three results follow for housing policy. First, the
analytical category on which rent policy turns — *economic rent*, the
surplus a factor earns above the cost of bringing it into use — is not a
natural fact but a historical product of the agrarian transition to
commercial farming, which is why it can be captured without discouraging
genuine provision. Second, the capitalisation of a rent stream into an
asset price, $P = R/(r-g)$, is the technical thread linking the
classical theory of land value to the modern asset-pricing of housing;
once rent is capitalised, the binding question becomes who holds the
claim. Third, and centrally for policy, because the binding component of
housing rent is the location value of land, which is fixed in supply,
the incidence of regulating or taxing that rent falls on the capitalised
asset value of owners rather than on the supply of housing to tenants —
a result the recent empirical literature on rent stabilisation and rent
taxation supports once the inelastic land component is separated from
the elastic structure. A reform tradition seeking to capture this
fixed-supply surplus, from the eighteenth-century land tax to today’s
rent stabilisation and proposals to tax rental income as earned income,
recurs at every phase and is alive in current debate.

*Keywords:* rent, economic rent, land value, capitalisation, housing,
affordability, financialisation, incidence

## 1. Introduction

Rent is the oldest income in economics and the least settled. For three
centuries the discipline has not agreed on what it *is* — a price paid
for a productive service, a premium earned from scarcity, or an unearned
claim levied by the ownership of something others need — nor on what, if
anything, should be done about it. That disagreement reflects the fact
that rent has repeatedly changed its form while keeping, this paper
argues, a single underlying function. And it matters now more than at
any point in a century, because the form rent takes today — the price of
housing and the asset value capitalised on top of it — is where the
gains of the modern asset economy bear down most heavily on the living
standards of ordinary households.

The argument is historical and turns on one claim. The *form* of rent
has passed through four broad regimes — direct labour, payment in kind,
money, and capitalised financial claim — and the sequence has a
direction: the claim becomes steadily more abstract, more tradable, and
more removed from anything a tenant can see, until in the contemporary
housing market it appears as a pure asset price with no visible
connection to the household paying it. The *function*, throughout, is
consistent with one description: rent is a deduction from the resources
a household needs to secure and sustain its shelter, levied not by the
provision of anything but by the control of a necessity that is scarce
and, in its binding component, fixed in supply. This is offered not as a
closed theorem but as a reading of the historical and theoretical
record, tested against each period; where a period resists the
continuity — most sharply at the agrarian transition, where the very
category of *economic rent* is born rather than carried forward — the
paper says so, and the claim is the stronger for surviving those breaks.

The contribution is twofold and policy-facing. First, the paper shows
that *economic rent* — the payment to a factor above the minimum needed
to bring it into use — is a historically specific category, produced by
the transition to commercial agriculture and meaningless under the
regime that preceded it; the modern policy distinction between a
*capturable surplus* and a *genuine return to provision* is therefore
internal to the way rent works, not an external imposition on it.
Second, it identifies the capitalisation of a rent stream into an asset
price as the technical thread running from the classical theory of land
value to the contemporary asset-pricing of housing, and draws the
incidence lesson that follows: because the binding part of housing rent
is the location value of land, which cannot be manufactured or moved,
the burden of capturing it falls on the capitalised wealth of owners and
not on the provision of homes. From this the recurring politics of rent
— a reform tradition that returns in every epoch to capture the
fixed-supply surplus at its source — becomes intelligible as a single
argument rather than a series of unrelated episodes.

A word on why the history is necessary to the policy, since the payoff
arrives late. The distinction the second result rests on — between a
capturable surplus and a genuine return to provision — is not available
*a priori*; it had to be produced, historically, by the transition that
first separated rent from profit and wages (Section 3). The argument
therefore cannot begin in the present: the medieval and agrarian
sections are not background colour but the derivation of the very
category — economic rent — on which the contemporary policy lesson
depends. A reader who wants the lesson first may read Sections 6 to 8
and return for the derivation, but the separability those sections rely
on is earned earlier.

A note on method and scope. The paper distinguishes throughout between
claims that are broadly agreed among economic historians, claims that
remain genuinely contested, and the author’s own interpretive synthesis,
and it flags which is which in the prose rather than asserting a uniform
confidence it does not have. Its institutional narrative follows the
English, and latterly British, trajectory — chosen as the case in which
the financialisation of housing rent is most fully developed and best
documented, not as a claim to universal history; comparative paths that
diverged, and the colonial and global circuits of land rent, lie outside
it and would require separate treatment.

## 2. The historical forms of rent

For most of recorded history, rent was paid not in money but in labour.
Under the manorial economy the cultivator worked a fixed number of days
on his own holding and a fixed number on the lord’s land; the obligation
was enforced not by a market but by personal dependence and local
jurisdiction. The structure was transparent. What the cultivator owed
was visible, dated, and counted in days of work, and it was, in effect,
the whole of what he produced above his own subsistence — there being as
yet no profit and no wage for it to be distinguished from.

The history of rent is, in large part, the history of that obligation
becoming progressively more abstract. Labour dues were commuted first
into a share of the harvest, which returned to the cultivator some
control over how he worked, and then into a fixed money payment, which
did more than change the unit of account: a cash obligation forced the
holding into market relations to raise the money, sorted cultivators
into those who could meet it and those who could not, and dissolved a
relation of personal dependence into an impersonal, contractual one. In
England this movement ran across the thirteenth to fifteenth centuries
and was sharply accelerated by the demographic collapse of the Black
Death, which by destroying a large share of the population shifted
bargaining power toward labour and undermined the older servile
arrangements. The mechanisms remain debated among economic historians —
demographic and class-relational accounts weight population, markets,
and seigneurial power differently — and nothing here turns on
adjudicating that debate.

What the earliest regime offers the present argument is a limiting case
in which the nature of rent is impossible to mistake. When rent is paid
in days of labour, the claim that it is a deduction from the resources
the producer needs to live is not an inference; it is the institution in
plain view. Everything the later, financial form of rent conceals — the
household labour behind the payment, the living standard behind the
income — the earliest form displays on its surface. What follows is the
story of that plain relation being progressively concealed — not by any
design of history, but by a sequence of concrete legal, financial, and
technological developments, from money rent to the mortgage and the
securitised asset — until the household labour behind the payment
disappears from view entirely.

## 3. Economic rent as a historical product

The passage from the manorial economy to commercial agriculture is the
most disputed terrain in economic history, and the dispute bears
directly on the concept of rent. On the account most influential in the
modern literature, the divergent fates of European rural societies
turned on their property relations: in England a distinctive outcome
emerged in which landowners lost direct control over labour but kept
ownership of the land, while cultivators lost secure customary tenure
through enclosure. The result was the three-cornered structure —
landowner, commercial tenant-farmer, and wage-labourer — that stands
behind the entire classical theory of distribution.

With this structure, and only with it, rent acquires the meaning it
carries in modern economics. The surplus no longer passes in its
entirety to the holder of the title; it is divided three ways, among
rent, profit, and wages, and the division is settled by competition
among tenant-farmers for the right to farm. Rent becomes a
*competed-down residual*: the advantage a better or better-placed plot
yields over land at the margin of cultivation, which by definition pays
no rent. This is the object Ricardo formalised in 1817 as differential
rent, with the proposition that rent is determined by price rather than
determining it, and does not enter into cost. The historical point,
which the purely theoretical literature tends to obscure, is that
Ricardo’s apparently timeless “law” of rent is the abstraction of a
particular property regime that several preceding centuries had built.
*Economic rent* — the payment to a factor above its supply price — is
not a fact of nature awaiting discovery; it is a category that comes
into existence when, and because, rent is forced to compete with profit
and wages for a share of a divided surplus. (This inference is
conditional on a property-relations reading of the transition; it does
not require that reading to be uniquely correct, only that the
three-cornered structure, however it arose, is what first makes the
surplus divisible.)

The implication for policy is direct and is the analytical hinge of
everything that follows. Every modern instrument that proposes to
capture the *surplus* component of rent while sparing the
*cost-of-provision* component depends on the two being separable — and
that separability is exactly what the transition to commercial
agriculture produced. The contemporary distinction between an economic
rent that can be taxed or capped and a genuine return to building and
maintaining homes is therefore native to the way rent works, not a
regulatory artifice imposed upon it.

## 4. From the land to the home

Two movements organise the long nineteenth century. The first was the
open political contest between landed rent and industrial profit. The
Corn Laws sustained agricultural rents by restricting grain imports;
their repeal in 1846 was the decisive political defeat of the landed
interest by manufacturing, and the classical theory of rent — rent as an
unearned charge and a drag on accumulation — was the intellectual weapon
in that fight. The classical economists were not neutral observers of
rent; they were parties to a distributional contest, and their theory
bears the marks of it.

The second and more lasting movement was the migration of the binding
rent from agricultural land to urban land and, above all, to
working-class housing. As population concentrated in industrial cities,
the rent that pressed hardest on ordinary households was no longer the
farmer’s but the tenant’s, and contemporaries already understood it as
such: the housing question of the nineteenth century was widely analysed
not as a separate misfortune but as a direct charge on wages — a
deduction from the fund out of which working households maintained
themselves. The analytic identification of housing rent with a deduction
from living standards is, in other words, as old as the industrial city
itself.

It is in this period that the technical core of the modern theory is
stated plainly. The classical tradition observed that the price of a
piece of land is simply its rent converted into a capital sum — its
annual rent divided by the rate of interest — and the same proposition,
that an asset is worth the present value of the income stream it
commands, became the foundation of the modern theory of capital and
asset pricing. The capitalisation of rent is the device that turns a
flow of payments into a tradable stock of wealth, and it is the formula
this paper tracks from the nineteenth century to the present.

The reform tradition reaches its nineteenth-century peak here as well.
The argument that rent absorbs the gains of social progress, and that it
should therefore be recovered at its source through a tax on land
values, became a mass political movement under Henry George and left an
institutional mark in Britain in the land-value duties of the 1909
budget. Its logic was already two centuries old — the Physiocrats had
proposed a single tax on the net product of land — and it rested on a
point that is now a commonplace of public economics: because the supply
of land is fixed, a tax on its rent cannot be escaped by reducing
supply, and so falls on the owner without discouraging any productive
activity (the result later formalised as the Henry George Theorem;
Arnott and Stiglitz, 1979). Beneath that efficiency point lies the
argument that makes the word *unearned* precise. The value of a location
is created not by its owner but by the society around it — by
population, infrastructure, employment, and proximity — so the rent it
commands is a private claim on a publicly produced value, and a charge
that recovers that rent takes nothing the owner has made.

## 5. The twentieth century: socialised, then re-privatised

The twentieth century runs the film in both directions, and the reversal
is instructive because it amounts to something close to a natural
experiment on the nature of housing rent.

The first movement was decommodification. Britain’s first rent control,
introduced in 1915 under the pressure of wartime rent strikes, suspended
the free movement of housing rents; the council-housing programme
launched in 1919 removed a large and growing share of the national stock
from the logic of rent-as-asset and administered it instead as a social
service. For half a century an increasing proportion of housing was
treated as a means of securing shelter rather than as a financial claim.

The second movement, which we still inhabit, was re-commodification.
From 1980 the post-war settlement was dismantled: the Right to Buy sold
much of the social stock into private ownership; financial deregulation
and the long shift of bank lending away from productive enterprise and
toward mortgages turned housing into the premier financial asset of the
age; and the buy-to-let mortgage, introduced in the United Kingdom in
1996, completed the transformation by allowing a home to be bought as a
leveraged investment financed against its expected rent rather than the
buyer’s earnings.

The reversal illuminates the paper’s thesis. When the capitalised claim
is suspended — under council housing and rent control — a dwelling
functions as a means of subsistence, a good consumed for the maintenance
of life. When the claim is restored — under the Right to Buy,
securitisation, and buy-to-let — the same dwelling functions as a
capitalised asset, valued for the income stream it commands. The
physical object does not change; the regime governing the claim upon it
does. That a single house can occupy either role, depending entirely on
how its rent is treated, is the historical demonstration of the double
character — necessity and financial claim — that defines the
contemporary housing problem.

## 6. The present: the home as the centre of the rent question

Three features define the present. The first is that housing has become
the centre of gravity of the asset economy. The much-discussed rise in
the share of national income accruing to capital (Piketty, 2014) is, on
the most influential decomposition (Rognlie, 2015), largely a housing
story: net of housing, much of the long-run rise in the capital share
recedes. The magnitude is debated and sensitive to the treatment of
depreciation, but the direction is robust, and the upshot is plain —
modern rentier income is, to a first approximation, housing rent and its
capitalisation. The rent question is no longer a question about
farmland, or even about land in general. It is a question about the
home.

The second feature is the return of land and rent to the centre of
economic analysis after a century in which the marginal-productivity
theory of distribution had dissolved rent’s special status by treating
every factor, land included, as simply paid its marginal product. Recent
work has reconstructed the land–credit feedback at the heart of the
housing economy (Ryan-Collins, Lloyd, and Macfarlane, 2017; Jordà,
Schularick, and Taylor, 2016) and has named the resulting system one
organised around income from the control of scarce assets (Christophers,
2020). The category the twentieth-century mainstream mislaid — unearned
income from the ownership of a necessity in fixed supply — has returned
as the explanatory core of the asset economy.

Within housing scholarship specifically, this recovery has taken the
form of a large literature on the *financialization of housing*: the
transformation of the home from a good consumed for shelter into an
asset traded for yield, integrated into global capital markets through
securitisation and the rise of corporate and buy-to-let landlordism
(Aalbers, 2016; Fields, 2017; Forrest and Hirayama, 2015), and analysed
as a contest over who controls the home and on what terms (Madden and
Marcuse, 2016). The argument of this paper is a complement to that
literature, not a rival to it. Where the financialization literature
documents the *process* by which housing became an asset, the long
history told here isolates the *operation* that does the work — the
capitalisation of a rent stream, $P = R/(r-g)$ — and draws from it a
result that the financialization literature tends to leave implicit:
because the binding component of the capitalised claim is the location
value of land, which is fixed in supply, the incidence of capturing it
falls on owner wealth and not on provision. Capitalisation-to-incidence
is offered here as a unifying test for the design of housing policy
under inelastic land value.

The third feature is the revival, in policy, of the old
counter-tradition, and it is where the historical argument earns its
practical lesson. Statutory rent stabilisation has returned to the
agenda across Europe, and proposals to tax rental income on the same
basis as earned income — on the ground that income from owning a
property should contribute like income from work — have re-entered
serious debate. The better-identified empirical studies of the incidence
of such instruments converge on a finding that confirms the
capitalisation thesis, provided the claim is stated with care. Insofar
as it falls on the *inelastic* component of the asset — the location
value of land, which cannot be withdrawn or remade — the regulation or
taxation of rent is borne by owners through a write-down of the
capitalised asset value rather than by tenants through higher prices;
studies of rent stabilisation (Diamond, McQuade, and Qian, 2019), of
rent decontrol (Autor, Palmer, and Pathak, 2014), and of the wealth
effects of controls (Ahern and Giacoletti, 2026) all show the asset
value moving in this way — though the most-cited of these reads its San
Francisco episode, on balance, as a net cost of control, so what is
drawn on here is its measured incidence, not its net-welfare verdict.
Where supply does respond, it does so on the *elastic* margin — owners
converting, redeveloping, or changing the legal status of the structure
to escape the penalty — and that escape route can raise rents on the
uncontrolled stock, which is precisely why effective designs pair a cap
with the closure of between-tenancy resets and with the acquisition of
exiting stock, shutting the margin through which the charge would
otherwise be avoided. The lesson is one a distributional analyst will
recognise immediately: to act on rent is to act on the capitalised
claim, and the incidence falls where the title is held.

A fourth, fiscal observation completes the picture and connects the
history to the public accounts. Where the private rented sector has
grown and the social sector has shrunk, an increasing share of housing
support paid through the benefit system flows directly to private
landlords as rent — a public subsidy to the capitalised claim, the
magnitude of which is estimated directly in a companion working paper
using two natural experiments in the Local Housing Allowance (Galindo
Escajeda, 2026b). Moderating rents at source, or capturing the
fixed-supply surplus through the tax system, is therefore not only a
transfer between owners and tenants but a reduction in the cost to the
state of underwriting the claim.

## 7. The technical thread: capitalisation, and what the rate stands for

One operation connects every modern treatment of rent to the classical
theory and, through it, to the present: the capitalisation of a rent
stream into a price. For a rent $R$ growing at rate $g$ and discounted
at rate $r$, the price of the asset is

$$ P = \frac{R}{r - g}. $$

Here $R$ is the current rent, $r$ the rate at which future income is
discounted, and $g$ the rate at which the rent is expected to grow. This
is the classical proposition that the price of land is capitalised rent,
with a growth term added; it is the dividend-growth model of finance
(Gordon and Shapiro, 1956); it is the reduced form of the theory of
capital as the present value of an expected income stream (Fisher,
1930); and it is the spine of the modern asset-market approach to house
prices (Poterba, 1984) and the long-run study of housing returns. The
operation is shared across the whole tradition. What the tradition has
never settled is the discount rate $r$ that performs the capitalisation,
and the unsettledness is the place where the disagreement about rent
ultimately lives.

For most of the modern theory the rate is taken as given — posited,
derived from time-preference, or read off market data. Modern
macroeconomics does tie the equilibrium rate to material magnitudes,
since in equilibrium it equals the marginal product of capital and along
a steady path it moves with population growth, technical progress, and
depreciation; but the primitive from which value is built remains
subjective preference, and the magnitudes that enter are the technology
and demography of capital rather than the conditions under which
households sustain themselves. The classical writers, by contrast,
looked for an anchor outside the financial sphere altogether: the
magnitude that ought ultimately to govern the valuation of wealth was,
for them, a material fact about the resources a society needs to
maintain and renew its working population, not a rate of impatience.
Whether that older intuition can be given a contemporary formal
expression is an open question, taken up briefly below; the historical
claim of this paper does not depend on its answer. As a matter of
record, the capitalisation operation is the through-line; what *anchors*
the rate it contains is the unfinished business the history hands
forward.

## 8. The policy lesson, and an open question

Two practical lessons follow from the history, and they are the reason
the long view is worth taking.

The first is that the category on which modern rent policy chiefly rests
— economic rent, the surplus above the cost of provision — is a
historical product and not a natural fact, and that it is precisely the
surplus which can be captured without discouraging provision, because
its binding source is fixed in supply. The second half is the oldest
result in the public economics of land: a charge on a factor in fixed
supply falls on the owner and distorts nothing. The history shows that
the binding component of *housing* rent — the location value of land —
is exactly such a factor, and that the modern instruments built to
capture it, rent stabilisation and the taxation of rental income, are
the latest members of a family of policies two and a half centuries old.

The second is that incidence, not provision, is the right frame for the
housing-rent debate. Because capture falls on the capitalised claim, the
central distributional question is who holds the claim and who bears its
write-down — owners of an inelastic asset, in the main, rather than
tenants through reduced supply, provided the design closes the elastic
escape margins. A policy programme that pairs a cap on rent growth with
the end of between-tenancy resets, the acquisition of exiting stock, and
the taxation of rental income as earned income is, on the reading
offered here, not a departure from sound economics but an application of
its oldest and most secure result to the asset that has become the
centre of the modern rentier economy.

Two qualifications keep this from being a slogan. The incidence result
is conditional on closing the margins through which the charge is
escaped, and those margins are real. A cap on the gross rent of a
dwelling can be borne by tenants not through a higher price but through
the *degradation of the structure*, as owners allow maintenance to lapse
on an asset whose income is now fixed — which is why a credible design
pairs the cap not only with the end of between-tenancy resets and the
acquisition of exiting stock but with enforced minimum standards. That
this requirement is no special burden of regulation is plain from the
unregulated sector’s own record, in which a markedly higher share of
private-rented homes already fails basic decency standards than in the
social stock: under-maintenance is a feature of the asset market the cap
inherits, not a cost the cap creates, and minimum-standards enforcement
is the remedy in either case. The international record bears out this
design-dependence: durable systems such as Vienna’s couple stabilised
rents with large-scale social ownership and maintenance obligations,
while blunter instruments have fared worse — Berlin’s 2020 cap was
struck down on competence grounds, and short, design-light freezes such
as Scotland’s have been blamed, on contested evidence, for prompting
landlord exit, the supply-side evidence on rent controls being genuinely
mixed (Gillespie et al., 2025). Those cases lie outside the British
focus of this paper, but their lesson is the one the history teaches:
the instrument bites on the inelastic claim only to the extent that the
elastic escape routes are shut. Within the British setting the relevant
mechanics are domestic — the acquisition of exiting stock through
existing social and community-housing channels, the closure of
between-tenancy resets under recent tenancy reform, and the treatment of
rental income on the same footing as earned income — and it is there
that the design must finally be judged. The detailed construction of
such a brake for the financialised UK market, including the second-best
case for it and its incidence on capitalised values, is set out in a
companion working paper (Galindo Escajeda, 2026d).

An open question remains, and it is worth stating plainly rather than
burying. The history establishes a regularity — that the gap between
those who own housing and those who rent it tends to widen rather than
close, with the rent flow as the channel through which it does so — but
it does not supply a formal account of why. A model in which housing is
at once a necessity that households must consume and an asset that only
some of them own, and in which the resulting difference in the ability
to accumulate compounds over time, would turn this historical regularity
into a derived result and would put the applied literature on the
housing wealth gap on a firmer theoretical footing. That model is a
project for separate work; it is the question the history sets, not a
result the history delivers.

## 9. Conclusion

Rent began as the most visible of economic relations: a cultivator
walking to the lord’s field for three days in the week. It has become
the most opaque: a figure on a balance sheet, the capitalised value of a
stream of payments whose connection to the household that pays it has
been thoroughly concealed by the financial form. This paper has argued
that the opacity is the achievement of a long historical process, not
the disappearance of the underlying relation. Through labour dues,
payment in kind, money rent, the competed-down rent of commercial
agriculture, the housing rent of the industrial city, and the
financialised housing rent of the present, one function is consistent
with persisting — an unearned claim on the resources households need to
secure their shelter, levied by control of a necessity in fixed supply —
while the form grows ever more abstract and ever more fully capitalised.

The practical payoff is a single, durable lesson for housing policy. The
surplus component of housing rent rests on the location value of land,
which is fixed in supply; capturing it therefore falls on the
capitalised wealth of owners and not on the provision of homes; and the
instruments that do the capturing — land taxation, rent stabilisation,
the social ownership of housing, and the taxation of rental income as
earned income — are not novelties but the latest turn of a wheel that
has been turning since the eighteenth century. The home is where that
wheel now turns, and the question it poses is the same one it has always
posed: who should keep the surplus that control of a necessity throws
off.
