# Reproduction under Capital-Biased Technical Change
Carlos Galindo Escajeda
2026-09-01

# Abstract

Who pays to produce the next generation of workers? The theory of capital income taxation answers a neighbouring question with a long-run zero, the revenue transferred or consumed. If the revenue instead builds the stock that raises the next generation, the owner of capital has his own stake in the tax: the workforce it produces is the workforce he hires. This paper sizes that stake and what capital-biased technical change does to it. Under Cobb–Douglas his preferred rate is strictly positive in closed form, but factor shares cannot move. Under a constant elasticity of substitution an identity rules out that closed form at every elasticity other than one and perfect substitutability, when all revenue is provided in kind. A first-order expansion of his two logged income channels does close, with two effective shares in place of one. His preferred rate then falls strictly in capital’s share, so capital-augmenting progress lowers it at every elasticity above one, leaves it unchanged at one, and raises it below wherever the expansion is well posed. At one parameter point, with the owner held to constant policies, the exact model gives those three signs at elasticities of two, one and one half. Give the owner felicity of any constant curvature and a second channel opens through his patience: progress raises growth, and an owner whose elasticity of intertemporal substitution is below one then values the next workforce less, so where the factors are substitutes the two channels agree at the economy’s stationary state, and Cobb–Douglas silences the question only together with logarithmic felicity. In the expansion, where the factors are substitutes, as capital’s share tends to one his rate tends to zero while the worker’s stays bounded away from zero, so the band of rates both classes want raised, non-empty at every finite technique, empties in that limit: a contested transfer, not a joint investment.

**Keywords.** capital-biased technical change; capital income taxation; elasticity of substitution; reproduction of the workforce; public provision; class conflict

# 1. Introduction

The theory of capital income taxation has a long-run zero at its centre. Judd (1985) proved it for a two-class economy in which capitalists save and workers do not: the optimal tax on capital income goes to zero in the long run wherever the optimum converges, and it does so even for a government that cares about nothing but workers. Chamley (1986) reached the same zero with a representative household. Straub and Werning (2020) questioned that result on its own ground and showed that the zero is not general — in Judd’s own economy a positive long-run rate can be optimal. What none of the three changes is where the money goes. The government transfers the revenue or consumes it, the proceeds do not enter anybody’s production function, and in Judd’s two-class economy the owner of capital meets the tax from one side only. He pays it.

That is the assumption this paper lifts. Suppose the revenue builds something the owner uses. Barro (1990) did exactly that, putting public spending into production as an input and finding an interior positive rate — but his rate is chosen by a single representative household, and with one household there is no second class whose preferred rate could be set against it. Put the two departures together: let the tax fund the stock through which the next generation of workers is raised, and keep two classes. The owner then has an interest of his own in the tax, because the workforce it produces is the workforce he hires. A technology that reproduces labour is an input to his own production, and if the tax is the only instrument that reaches it, he wants the rate strictly above zero. Under the workhorse aggregator that rate can be written down: at the parameter values used below it is 0.40, against the worker’s 0.61, and both are interior.

What makes this more than an arithmetic exercise is the question of what automation does to that rate, and there the workhorse aggregator betrays the question rather than simplifying it. Cobb–Douglas is doing two jobs in the paragraph above, and only one of them is wanted. It is what makes the model solvable, because every transition is then log-linear in the logged stocks and both value functions are affine in them, with coefficients that solve a linear system whose entries are the constant factor shares. It is also what makes the model mute about technical change, because under Cobb–Douglas capital-augmenting progress raises both factor prices and moves no factor share at all. What the model exists to ask — what automation does to capital’s willingness to fund the reproduction of labour — needs the shares to move; moving them means leaving Cobb–Douglas; and leaving Cobb–Douglas removes the property that made the model solvable.

This paper settles that trade-off, and settles it against exact closure. Under a constant elasticity of substitution the owner’s value function is not affine in the logged stocks, and the reason is not that nobody has found the right guess: it is an identity. At the corner where all the revenue is provided in kind rather than paid out in cash, the two income channels the owner reads — his own capital income and the worker’s labour income, each measured against output — share a single curvature in the logged effective capital–labour ratio, which is the capital stock scaled by the technology that augments it, over the workforce. That curvature is a product of strictly positive factors with a coefficient that cannot vanish when the guess works. The product is zero at unit elasticity, zero at perfect substitutability, and zero nowhere between (Proposition 1). Away from the provision corner the obstruction arrives one derivative earlier still: the owner’s preferred rate stops being a number and becomes a function of the state (Proposition 2). The interval that fails is the interval the question lives in, because capital-augmenting progress lowers the labour share only where the factors are substitutes.

What survives is a local expansion, and the choice among expansions is not a matter of taste. Freeze the factor shares at a reference ratio — replace the technology by the Cobb–Douglas function tangent to it — and the model becomes solvable again. But the level of the answer is then wrong by a factor of two at the parameter values used below, because freezing the shares silently replaces the elasticity of the owner’s own income channel by the capital share. Expand the two logged income channels themselves instead of the shares inside them. That expansion is first order in every channel the owner reads, closes in closed form, and returns the Cobb–Douglas answer exactly at unit elasticity. Both classes’ preferred rates then stay strictly between zero and one at every elasticity above one short of perfect substitutability (Proposition 3). It buys that by putting two constants where Cobb–Douglas had one share, and those two constants are where the economics turns out to live. Two exact identities tie them to the factor shares, and they hold exactly rather than to first order: one minus the first is labour’s share divided by the elasticity of substitution, and the second is capital’s share divided by it (Proposition 4).

Four results follow. Capital’s preferred rate carries labour’s share over the elasticity as a factor, falls strictly in capital’s share, and goes to zero as that share goes to one — and it is not proportional to labour’s share, because capital’s share re-enters the denominator, so there is no constant of proportionality to find. Two economies with the same labour share and different elasticities of substitution therefore do not give capital the same interest in funding reproduction. That is a statement about technology which a model with the elasticity pinned at one cannot make at all. Capital-augmenting progress moves both constants at the same rate and both push the rate the same way, so the rate falls at every elasticity above one, does not move at unit elasticity, and rises below it wherever the expansion still describes a well-posed problem, which needs labour’s share not to exceed the elasticity (Proposition 5). The worker’s preferred rate does not follow capital’s down: it stays above a floor at every elasticity, and that floor rises as the factors become better substitutes (Proposition 6). And the two rates do not coincide at any finite elasticity, so the set of rates over which the classes disagree survives with a strictly positive upper end while the set both of them want raised empties.

The zero at the end of that process has a single cause, and saying so unifies two limits that look separate. Capital’s preferred rate is zero exactly when the elasticity of his own income channel in the logged ratio equals one, and that happens in precisely two circumstances: perfect substitutability at any capital share, and a capital share of one at any elasticity. At the first the model closes exactly, his rate is identically zero and the worker’s is one minus the discount factor; at the second the zero is approached and not attained. The zero this paper meets is a different object from the benchmark’s — the owner’s own peak, rather than a government’s optimum — reached along a different road, and the interesting territory is the interior, where the road has not been walked.

The positive results about capital’s preferred rate are exact statements about the expansion, and the exact model is computed rather than solved. With the owner held to a tax rate and a saving rate that do not change with the date, the same comparative static gives the same sign at an elasticity of two, and at every pair of rates he could have chosen rather than only at the ones reported. At the ratio to which the economy settles under those rates, the expansion’s rate is an exact critical point of the exact constant-policy problem, so the two answers agree there and not merely nearly. Above unit elasticity the expansion’s sign survives a moving stationary ratio, which rises strictly with the technique, and survives re-optimisation at that ratio over every elasticity from about 1.001 to a thousand paired with every capital share, at the paper’s other parameter values. How much of the comparative static rests on the owner valuing logarithms is a question the same exact model answers. Give him felicity of constant curvature and technical change reaches his preferred rate through a second channel, his patience: progress raises the rate at which every stock grows, and an owner reluctant to substitute over time values a richer future less, so the rate he would pay for the next workforce falls (Propositions 8 and 9). Where capital and labour are substitutes the two channels push the same way at a given policy whenever his elasticity of intertemporal substitution is at most one, which is where estimates for asset holders lie; only an owner more willing than that to substitute can reverse the sign, and at unit elasticity any such owner does. The curvature above which he has no policy of finite value is set by the economy’s rate of growth, a level the results above leave free (Lemma 2). Throughout, the public stock is how successor labour is reproduced when it is not owned. It enters the owner’s continuation with a strictly positive loading at unit elasticity and at every finite elasticity above it. At perfect substitutability that loading is exactly zero, and so is his preferred rate.[^1]

Three neighbouring literatures bound the claim. Against the zero line (Judd 1985; Chamley 1986), nothing here turns on an optimality argument: the difference is what the tax funds, a stock the owner himself uses in production. To be sure, that a positive long-run rate can be optimal in a two-class economy is not news (Straub and Werning 2020), and neither is a positive rate when the proceeds fund a productive input (Barro 1990). What is claimed is narrower and different — that the owner’s *own* peak is positive, and that the reproduction of his workforce is the channel.

Against the second position, Cobb–Douglas as an innocuous aggregator, unit elasticity turns out to be one of exactly two elasticities at which the model closes (Proposition 1) and, with logarithmic felicity, a point at which technical change moves neither factor share nor the band of rates both classes want raised (Proposition 5). For this question Cobb–Douglas with logarithmic felicity does not simplify the answer; it deletes the question. The case the sign result covers, capital and labour as substitutes, is the one Karabarbounis and Neiman (2014) estimate: an elasticity of substitution above one, through which they attribute about half of the global decline in the labour share since the early 1980s to the falling relative price of investment goods.

Against the third, automation as a factor-price or task-reallocation effect: in the static version of the task-based model of Acemoglu and Restrepo (2018), automation lowers employment and the labour share and the creation of new tasks raises them, but the object there is the allocation of tasks between factors that already exist, and here it is the funding of the reproduction of one of them. Factor prices do not record that the set of rates both classes want raised can empty while output rises. The nearest neighbour is Acemoglu, Gitmez and Shadmehr (2026), where a state acting for the owners of capital redistributes and also sets a strictly positive tax that workers would raise further. There the revenue buys the regime’s survival against revolt rather than an input to production, so that rate rises as automation lowers labour’s share and the set of rates both classes want raised widens. Their set empties too, but at a switch of instrument, and only if the threat of revolt is strong enough: past a critical capital stock, repression, which costs a fixed share of output, becomes cheaper for capital than redistribution, and the state stops taxing to fund workers. Here the set shrinks as capital’s share rises, and it empties only in the limit, through what the tax funds rather than any threat of revolt.

Section 2 puts two results side by side, each of them standard and the two jointly unavailable: that capital-augmenting progress lowers the labour share above unit elasticity, and that under Cobb–Douglas capital’s preferred rate is interior and lies below the worker’s. Section 3 sets out the economy and records the three derivatives of the technology that carry everything after it. Section 4 shows where and why exact closure fails. Section 5 derives the expansion that closes and the two constants it turns on. Section 6 reads the comparative static, as a theorem on the expansion and as a computation on the exact model, where the owner is held to constant policies and the sign is corroborated rather than proved; it then gives the owner felicity of constant curvature, shows that the limit on that curvature is set by the level of growth, and splits the effect of technical change into a channel through distribution and a channel through growth. Section 7 concludes, and says what the results rest on.

# 2. Two benchmark results

The first result is standard. The second is the Cobb–Douglas case of the model of Section 3; it is stated here and proved in Sections 5 and 6. Both are stated first because the paper is about what happens when they are put together.

**(i) The share result.** With production
$$Y \;=\; \bigl[\theta (BK)^{\rho} + (1-\theta) L^{\rho}\bigr]^{1/\rho}, \qquad \rho \le 1,\; \rho \ne 0,\; \sigma = \tfrac{1}{1-\rho},$$
competitive factor payment gives the wage share
$$\frac{wL}{Y} \;=\; \frac{1-\theta}{\theta\,(B\,K/L)^{\rho} + (1-\theta)},$$
whose derivative in $B$, at a fixed capital–labour ratio, is
$$\frac{\partial}{\partial B}\,\frac{wL}{Y} \;=\; -\,\rho\;\frac{\theta(1-\theta)\,(BK/L)^{\rho}}{B\,\bigl[\theta\,(BK/L)^{\rho} + (1-\theta)\bigr]^{2}}$$
and so has the sign of $-\rho$, every other factor being positive. Capital-augmenting progress lowers the wage share when $\sigma>1$, raises it when $\sigma<1$, and leaves it alone at $\sigma=1$. This is standard.

**(ii) Capital’s preferred rate under Cobb–Douglas.** On the generational face of the two-class model used below, both stocks depreciate fully within a generation, both period objectives are logarithmic, and the value functions are affine in the logs. The owner’s and the worker’s payoffs in the tax are then each of the form $M\ln(1-\tau)+N\ln\tau$ plus a constant, hence strictly concave with a unique interior peak at $N/(M+N)$. The peaks, which are the case $a_1=a_2=\alpha$ of Proposition 3 below, are
$$\tau_C \;=\; \frac{\beta^{2}\gamma(1-\alpha)(1-\theta_R)}{1-\beta+\beta\gamma\bigl(1-\theta_R+\alpha\theta_R\bigr)}, \qquad
\tau_W \;=\; \frac{(1-\theta_R)(1-\alpha\beta)}{1-\theta_R+\alpha\theta_R},$$
with $\alpha$ the capital share, $\beta$ the discount factor, $\gamma$ the elasticity of the reproduction technology and $\theta_R$ the weight of the worker’s own income in it. Both are interior, $\tau_C<\tau_W$ (the case $a_1=a_2=\alpha$ of Proposition 6), and the Pareto set in the tax is exactly $[\tau_C,\tau_W]$: every rate below $\tau_C$ is dominated, because *both* classes strictly prefer a larger one. Capital’s own preferred rate falls in $\alpha$ and vanishes as $\alpha\to1$; the worker’s does not, tending to $(1-\theta_R)(1-\beta)>0$.

Result (ii) is a comparative static in a parameter. In a Cobb–Douglas world $\alpha$ is a number chosen by the modeller, not an object technical change can move, so (ii) on its own says nothing about automation. Result (i) supplies an economy in which the share does move — and it is written in a technology under which (ii) has not been derived before. Everything below is about that gap.

# 3. The model

Two classes live through generations in an economy with constant-elasticity production. The owner of capital chooses the instruments, and each class’s preferred rate is read off its own discounted payoff. Both stocks depreciate fully within a generation, and both classes have logarithmic period objectives. What the technology contributes is three derivatives, recorded at the end of the section; one quantity among them carries both the obstruction of Section 4 and the comparative static of Section 6.

Effective labour is the worker stock, $L = N_W$; the object being reproduced is capacity, not headcount. Write $k=\ln K$, $n=\ln N_W$, $g=\ln G$ for the logged capital stock, worker stock and public stock, and
$$x \;\equiv\; \ln B + k - n$$
for the logged *effective* capital–labour ratio. Constant returns give $\ln Y = n + \psi(x)$ with
$$\psi(x) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{\rho}\ln\!\bigl(\theta e^{\rho x} + 1-\theta\bigr), \qquad
\psi'(x) \;=\; s_K(x) \;=\; \frac{\theta e^{\rho x}}{\theta e^{\rho x}+1-\theta}, \qquad s_K+s_L=1 .$$
$s_K$ is capital’s share and $s_L$ labour’s; $\theta$ is the distribution parameter of the technology and is a different object from $\theta_R$, the reproduction weight, throughout.

The owner chooses a saving rate $i$, a tax rate $\tau$ on capital income and a split $\phi$ of the proceeds between the public stock and a direct transfer. With $\kappa_W(x,\tau,\phi) = s_L(x) + (1-\phi)\tau s_K(x)$ the worker’s composite share of output, the transitions are
$$\begin{aligned}
\ln C_C &= \ln(1-i) + \ln(1-\tau) + \ln s_K(x) + n + \psi(x), \\
k' &= \ln i + \ln(1-\tau) + \ln s_K(x) + n + \psi(x), \\
n' &= n + \gamma\theta_R\bigl(\ln \kappa_W(x,\tau,\phi) + \psi(x)\bigr) + \gamma(1-\theta_R)(g-n), \\
g' &= \ln(\phi\tau) + \ln s_K(x) + n + \psi(x),
\end{aligned}$$
with owner felicity $(1-\omega)\ln C_C + \omega k$ and worker felicity $n$. The weight on the stock he holds is what makes saving cost the owner something he values: at $\omega=1$ his payoff is free of $\ln(1-i)$ and strictly increasing in his saving rate through $k'$, so that rate has no interior optimum and its supremum is approached as the rate rises to one rather than attained on the open control set. His felicity is therefore an aggregate of consumption and holdings with an interior weight, the wealth-in-utility form of Kurz (1968) and Zou (1994). Throughout, $\beta,\gamma,\theta,\theta_R,\omega\in(0,1)$ and $\rho\le1$, $\rho\ne0$; the controls satisfy $i,\tau\in(0,1)$ and $\phi\in(0,1]$. Every sign statement below is read on that open box and nowhere else, and a parameter is called *admissible* when it lies in it. The endpoint $\rho=1$ is admissible: it is perfect substitutability, it is one of the two elasticities at which the model closes exactly, and results that need the effective shares of Section 5 to be interior name $\rho<1$ for that reason.

The public stock $G$ is the in-kind input to the reproduction of the workforce: it is built from the taxed proceeds through $g'$, it enters the worker transition through $g-n$, and that is the whole of its definition here. It is a primitive of this model.

Three derivatives of the technology are used repeatedly and are recorded here once:
$$\psi'' = \rho\, s_K s_L, \qquad (\ln s_K)'' = (\ln s_L)'' = -\rho^{2} s_K s_L,$$
from which the two composites that actually enter the transitions satisfy
$$\bigl(\ln s_K + \psi\bigr)' = \rho + (1-\rho)s_K, \qquad
\bigl(\ln s_L + \psi\bigr)' = (1-\rho)s_K,$$
$$\bigl(\ln s_K + \psi\bigr)'' = \bigl(\ln s_L + \psi\bigr)'' = \rho(1-\rho)\, s_K s_L . \tag{3.1}$$
At $\rho\to0$ every line above collapses to the Cobb–Douglas face with $\alpha=\theta$, which is the control every claim below is checked against.

The single quantity $\rho(1-\rho)s_K s_L$ in (3.1) is the whole of the difficulty and the whole of the result. It is the curvature that the two logged income composites share, it is zero at exactly two elasticities, and it reappears in Section 6 as the rate at which the effective shares move under technical change.

# 4. Exact closure fails, and exactly where

At every elasticity strictly between one and infinity the owner’s problem has no affine-in-logs solution, and the reason can be located exactly. Both of the income channels he reads carry the same curvature, and it vanishes at unit elasticity, at perfect substitutability, and at no elasticity between them. Away from the provision corner the difficulty arrives one derivative earlier: his preferred rate stops being a number and becomes a function of the state.

**Proposition 1.** *Set $\phi=1$. Conjecture an affine value function $V_C = v + ak + bn + cg$. Then the Bellman right-hand side is*
$$\text{[affine in }k,n,g\text{]} \;+\; H(x) \;+\; \text{[terms in the controls alone]}, \qquad
H(x) = A\bigl(\ln s_K+\psi\bigr) + C\bigl(\ln s_L+\psi\bigr),$$
*with $A = (1-\omega)+\beta a+\beta c$ and $C = \beta b\gamma\theta_R$. Writing $\Sigma = A + C$,*
$$H''(x) \;=\; \rho(1-\rho)\, s_K(x)\, s_L(x)\, \Sigma, \qquad \Sigma \ne 0 \ \text{ whenever } V_C \text{ solves the Bellman equation}, \qquad\text{and then}\qquad \Sigma \ge 1-\omega > 0 \ \text{ for } \rho\in(0,1] . \tag{4.1}$$
*With the technology extended continuously to $\rho=0$ by its Cobb–Douglas limit, where $\psi\to\theta x$ and $s_K\to\theta$, an affine-in-logs value function therefore satisfies the Bellman equation identically in the states if and only if $\rho(1-\rho)=0$, that is if and only if $\sigma=1$ or $\sigma=\infty$. At $\sigma=\infty$ the right-hand side is a supremum and not a maximum. The owner’s own income channel is then $\ln s_K+\psi = x+\ln\theta$, of unit elasticity in the state (the $a_1$ of (5.1) is one there), so the tax carries no weight in his continuation and his payoff in $\tau$ is strictly decreasing. The optimum is approached as $\tau\downarrow0$ and is not attained on the open control set of Section 3.*

*Proof.* At $\phi=1$ the worker’s composite share is $\kappa_W = s_L(x)$ and carries no control. Substituting the four transitions into $(1-\omega)\ln C_C + \omega k + \beta(v+ak'+bn'+cg')$ and collecting, every occurrence of the state beyond the affine terms $\omega k$, $\bigl[(1-\omega)+\beta a+\beta b+\beta c\bigr]n$ and $\beta b\gamma(1-\theta_R)(g-n)$ enters through the two composites $\ln s_K+\psi$ and $\ln s_L+\psi$, with coefficients $A$ and $C$; the controls $i$ and $\tau$ enter additively and separately. That is the display. Differentiating twice and using (3.1), which gives both composites the *same* second derivative, yields (4.1).

For $\Sigma\ne0$: suppose $\Sigma=A+C=0$. Then $C=-A$ and $H = A\bigl[\ln s_K-\ln s_L\bigr] = A\bigl[\ln\tfrac{\theta}{1-\theta}+\rho x\bigr]$, which is affine in $x$ and hence in $(k,n)$, so the coefficient-matching system exists: $a=\omega+\rho A$, $b=(1-\omega)+\beta a+\beta b\bigl(1-\gamma(1-\theta_R)\bigr)+\beta c-\rho A$, $c=\beta b\gamma(1-\theta_R)$, together with $A=-C=-\beta b\gamma\theta_R$. Substituting $A$ into the second equation gives $b\,\bigl[1-\beta+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)+(1-\rho)\beta\gamma\theta_R\bigr]=0$, and the bracket is strictly positive for every $\rho\le1$; so $b=0$, hence $A=0$, hence $c=0$ and $a=\omega$, hence $A=(1-\omega)+\beta\omega=0$, which is impossible. In words, a vanishing $\Sigma$ would make the non-affine part of the right-hand side disappear, and the linear system that an affine value function would then have to solve has no solution. So $\Sigma\ne0$ at every coefficient vector for which $V_C$ solves the equation, on the whole parameter box, and the argument uses no sign of $a$, $b$ or $c$. On $\rho\in(0,1]$ more is true. Every transition partial is then non-negative — $\partial k'/\partial k=1-(1-\rho)s_L>0$, $\partial n'/\partial n=1-\gamma(1-\theta_R)-\gamma\theta_R(1-\rho)s_K\ge1-\gamma>0$, and the rest are products of non-negative factors — and felicity is non-decreasing in $(k,n)$, so the Bellman operator preserves monotonicity, any affine solution has $a,b,c\ge0$, and $\Sigma\ge1-\omega>0$. That lower bound is what the quantitative statement following the proof uses, and it is stated for $\rho\in(0,1]$ only: for $\rho<0$ the transition $k'$ is decreasing in $k$ wherever $s_K<-\rho/(1-\rho)$ — at $\rho=-1$, $\theta=0.3$ and $x=0$ its partial is exactly $-2/5$ — so the monotonicity route is unavailable there, and the direct argument is what carries the proposition. Since $s_Ks_L\in(0,\tfrac14]$ and $\Sigma\ne0$, $H''\equiv0$ if and only if $\rho(1-\rho)=0$, whose roots are $\rho=0$ and $\rho=1$. Because $x = \ln B + k - n$, $H$ is affine in $(k,n)$ precisely when $H$ is affine in $x$; if it is not, no affine $V_C$ can satisfy the equation identically in $(k,n,g)$, and the coefficient system does not exist. With $\sigma = 1/(1-\rho)$ the two roots are $\sigma=1$ and $\sigma=\infty$. $\square$

Three things about Proposition 1 deserve to be said plainly.

First, it is an obstruction and not a failure of search. The claim is not that no closed form was found; it is that the object whose vanishing closure requires is a product of factors none of which vanishes on the interior. At $\rho=\tfrac12$, $x=0$ and the parameter values used throughout Section 6, the technology factor $\rho(1-\rho)s_Ks_L$ is $0.0525$, so $H''\ge 0.0525\,(1-\omega) = 0.042 > 0$ for *every* admissible coefficient vector — the bound is stated that way because no affine solution exists there, so there is no particular $\Sigma$ to quote. The failure is uniform rather than pointwise: $\rho(1-\rho)s_Ks_L\ge10^{-4}$ at every point of $x\in[-4,4] \times \rho\in[0.1,0.9] \times \theta\in[0.1,0.9]$, so $|H''| \ge 10^{-4}\,\Sigma$ everywhere there.

Second, $\sigma=\infty$ is a genuine second closure point and not an artefact of the limit. At $\rho=1$ factor incomes are linear in the factors, $s_KY = \theta BK$ and $s_LY = (1-\theta)L$, so $\ln s_K + \psi = x + \ln\theta$ and $\ln s_L + \psi = \ln(1-\theta)$ — both affine in $x$, the first non-trivially so. The model is exactly solvable at perfect substitutability for the same reason it is exactly solvable at unit elasticity, and the two are the only such points. The endpoint solution can be written down rather than asserted. At $\rho=1$ the coefficients of (5.1) are exactly $a_1=1$ and $a_2=0$, so the owner’s system of Proposition 3 forces $b\bigl[1-\beta(1-D)\bigr]=0$ with $D=\gamma(1-\theta_R)<1$, hence $b=c=0$ and $a=1/(1-\beta)$: his continuation carries no loading on the public stock at all, his preferred rate is identically zero, and the worker’s system gives $\tau_W=1-\beta$. That is why the supremum in the previous paragraph is not attained — not a defect of the conjecture, but the content of the endpoint.

Third, the interval that fails is the one the paper needs. Capital-augmenting progress lowers the wage share only for $\sigma>1$; the closure points are $\sigma=1$ and $\sigma=\infty$; so $\sigma \in (1,\infty)$ is simultaneously the economically relevant case and the case in which no affine value function exists.

**Proposition 2.** *For $\phi<1$ the obstruction appears one derivative earlier. The cross-partial of the owner’s payoff in the tax and the state is*
$$\frac{\partial^{2} U_C}{\partial\tau\,\partial x} \;=\; \beta b\gamma\theta_R \cdot \frac{\rho\,\theta(1-\phi)(1-\theta)e^{\rho x}}{\bigl[(1-\phi)\tau\theta e^{\rho x} + 1-\theta\bigr]^{2}},$$
*where $U_C$ is the owner’s felicity plus $\beta$ times an affine continuation $v+ak'+bn'+cg'$. The display carries the factor $\rho$ explicitly and is non-zero whenever $\rho\notin\{0,1\}$, $\phi<1$ and $b\ne0$; the condition $\tau>0$ plays no role in it. At $\rho=1$ nothing the owner’s programme reads depends on $n$ — $s_KY=\theta BK$ exactly, so $\ln C_C$, $k'$ and $g'$ are free of $n$ — hence $b=0$ and the cross-partial vanishes identically, which is $\sigma=\infty$ being a closure point once more. Wherever $b\ne0$ the owner’s preferred rate under that continuation is a non-constant function of the state. Whether the exact policy inherits the dependence is not settled here: the exact value function is not affine (Proposition 1), and its curvature could in principle cancel the mixed partial the affine continuation carries.*

The Cobb–Douglas face has the opposite property, and it is what makes that face comfortable: there the state drops out of both payoff functions entirely, so each class’s preferred rate is a constant rather than a function of the state, and the Pareto set in the tax is fixed once the parameters are. Proposition 2 says that this is a fact about $\rho=0$ and not about the model.

The tractability of the Cobb–Douglas face is an exact coincidence at two points of the elasticity line, and a more general technology does not inherit it at some cost in algebra. An economist who wants a moving factor share in this class of models must give up, at every finite elasticity above one, either the affine value function or the exactness, and the next section makes that choice.

# 5. The approximation that closes, and the one that does not

Two first-order approximations are available at a reference ratio $\bar x$, and they are not equally good. It matters which, because they differ by a factor of two in the object the paper is about. The one chosen delivers the closed forms for both preferred rates and the two exact identities that carry the economics of Section 6.

**The frozen-share tangent.** Replace the technology by the Cobb–Douglas function agreeing with it in level and slope at $\bar x$: $\psi \mapsto \psi(\bar x)+\bar s_K(x-\bar x)$ and $s_K\mapsto\bar s_K$. The model becomes the Cobb–Douglas face exactly, with $\alpha := \bar s_K$, and every closed form of Section 2(ii) holds verbatim. This is first order in the logged output aggregator and *zeroth* order in the shares, and the asymmetry is fatal to the level: freezing the share replaces the elasticity of the owner’s own income channel, $(\ln s_K+\psi)' = \rho+(1-\rho)s_K$, by $s_K$, dropping the entire $\rho$ term. At $\rho=\tfrac12$ and $\bar s_K = 0.056$ that is $0.528$ replaced by $0.056$. Measured against the exact model in Section 6, it misstates $\tau_C$ by up to $0.30$, a factor of $2.0$, while getting the direction right. It is reliable for signs only, and the expansion used from here on is a different one.

**The Bellman tangent.** Expand the two logged composites, rather than the shares, to first order about $\bar x$:
$$\ln s_K(x)+\psi(x) \;\mapsto\; \text{const} + a_1(x-\bar x), \qquad \ln s_L(x)+\psi(x) \;\mapsto\; \text{const} + a_2(x-\bar x),$$
$$a_1 \;=\; \rho+(1-\rho)\bar s_K, \qquad a_2 \;=\; (1-\rho)\bar s_K . \tag{5.1}$$
This is uniformly first order in every channel, with neglected curvature $\tfrac12\rho(1-\rho)s_Ks_L(x-\bar x)^{2}$ per channel by (3.1); taking $\bar x$ to be the model’s own stationary ratio sets $x-\bar x=0$ along the path the constant policies induce. For a share tolerance $\varepsilon$ the window is $|x-\bar x|\le 4\varepsilon/|\rho|$, since $|s_K'| = |\rho| s_Ks_L \le |\rho|/4$.

**Proposition 3.** *Under (5.1) the transitions are log-linear again, with two constants in place of the Cobb–Douglas face’s one. Writing $D = \gamma(1-\theta_R)+\gamma\theta_R a_2$, the owner’s and worker’s coefficients solve*
$$\begin{aligned}
a &= \omega + (1-\omega)a_1 + \beta\bigl(a\,a_1 + b\,\gamma\theta_R a_2 + c\,a_1\bigr), &\qquad p &= \beta\bigl(p\,a_1 + q\,\gamma\theta_R a_2 + r\,a_1\bigr),\\
b &= (1-\omega)(1-a_1) + \beta\bigl(a(1-a_1) + b(1-D) + c(1-a_1)\bigr), &\qquad q &= 1 + \beta\bigl(p(1-a_1)+q(1-D)+r(1-a_1)\bigr),\\
c &= \beta b\gamma(1-\theta_R), &\qquad r &= \beta q\gamma(1-\theta_R),
\end{aligned}$$
*two linear systems; and with $\tau_C = \beta c/\bigl[(1-\omega)+\beta a+\beta c\bigr]$ and $\tau_W = r/(p+r)$,*
$$\tau_C \;=\; \frac{\beta^{2}\gamma(1-\theta_R)(1-a_1)}{1-\beta+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)+\beta\gamma\theta_R a_2}, \qquad
\tau_W \;=\; \frac{(1-\theta_R)(1-\beta a_1)}{1-\theta_R+\theta_R a_2}. \tag{5.2}$$
*At $a_1=a_2=\alpha$ both collapse to the Cobb–Douglas expressions of Section 2(ii) identically. For $\rho\in(0,1)$, where $a_1=\rho+(1-\rho)\bar s_K$ and $a_2=(1-\rho)\bar s_K$ both lie in $(0,1)$, both rates are interior:* $\tau_C\in(0,1)$ *because its numerator is positive and*
$$\bigl[1-\beta+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)+\beta\gamma\theta_R a_2\bigr] - \beta^{2}\gamma(1-\theta_R)(1-a_1) \;=\; (1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)\bigl(1-\beta(1-a_1)\bigr)+\beta\gamma\theta_R a_2 \;>\;0,$$
*and* $\tau_W\in(0,1)$ *because* $1-\beta a_1>0$ *and* $\bigl(1-\theta_R+\theta_R a_2\bigr) - (1-\theta_R)(1-\beta a_1) = \theta_R a_2 + \beta a_1(1-\theta_R) > 0$. *The restriction to $\rho\in(0,1)$ is not decorative. At $\rho=1$, $a_1=1$ and $\tau_C=0$ exactly, as the proof of Proposition 4 records. For $\rho<0$ the coefficient $a_1$ can be negative and both brackets can change sign: at $\rho=-3$, $\bar s_K=\tfrac12$, $\beta=\gamma=\tfrac9{10}$ and $\theta_R=\tfrac1{10}$, (5.2) returns $\tau_C=6561/4955>1$ and $\tau_W=171/110>1$.*

*Proof.* Under (5.1) at $\phi=1$ every transition is affine in $(k,n,g)$. Since $x=\ln B+k-n$, the channels $\ln C_C$, $k'$ and $g'$ load $a_1$ on $k$ and $1-a_1$ on $n$, and $n'$ loads $\gamma\theta_R a_2$ on $k$, $1-D$ on $n$ and $\gamma(1-\theta_R)$ on $g$. Substituting $V_C=v+ak+bn+cg$ into $(1-\omega)\ln C_C+\omega k+\beta V_C(k',n',g')$ and matching the coefficients of $k$, $n$ and $g$ gives the owner’s system; the worker’s, with felicity $n$ and continuation $p k+q n+r g$, follows in the same way. The controls enter additively: the owner’s payoff carries $\bigl[(1-\omega)+\beta a\bigr]\ln(1-\tau)$ through $\ln C_C$ and $k'$ and $\beta c\ln\tau$ through $g'$, and the worker’s carries $\beta p\ln(1-\tau)$ and $\beta r\ln\tau$, so each peak is the stated ratio. Solving the owner’s system gives $b=(1-a_1)(1-\omega+\beta\omega)/\bigl[(1-\beta)(1-\beta a_1+\beta D)\bigr]$, $c=\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)\,b$ and $(1-\omega)+\beta a+\beta c=(1-\omega+\beta\omega)(1-\beta+\beta D)/\bigl[(1-\beta)(1-\beta a_1+\beta D)\bigr]$. Their ratio is the first expression in (5.2), since $1-\beta+\beta D$ is its denominator. The worker’s system gives $q=(1-\beta a_1)/\bigl[(1-\beta)(1-\beta a_1+\beta D)\bigr]$, $r=\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)\,q$ and $p+r=\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R+\theta_R a_2)/\bigl[(1-\beta)(1-\beta a_1+\beta D)\bigr]$, whose ratio is the second. Setting $a_1=a_2=\alpha$ returns the expressions of Section 2(ii). The interiority claims are the two displayed inequalities. $\square$

Neither peak depends on how much the owner values the stock he holds. The factor $1-\omega+\beta\omega$ multiplies $b$, $c$ and $(1-\omega)+\beta a+\beta c$ alike, so it cancels in his ratio, and the worker’s system does not contain $\omega$ at all. That weight decides whether he has an interior saving rate at all, and not the rate on capital income he prefers once he has one.

The systems are linear because $a_1$ and $a_2$ are constants once $\bar x$ is fixed — which is precisely what $s_K(x)$ and $s_L(x)$ are not, and precisely why Proposition 1 holds. The spectral condition needed before any coefficient is read off a guess-and-verify solution is satisfied for a reason worth naming rather than asserting: the transition matrix in $(k,n,g)$ has unit row sums for every $(\bar s_K,\gamma,\theta_R)\in(0,1)^{3}$ and, for $\rho\in(0,1]$, is entrywise non-negative as well, so its spectral radius is exactly one by Perron–Frobenius — from the non-negativity, not from the row sums alone — and $\beta\cdot1<1$. The spectrum can also be read directly: the $k$- and $g$-rows of the matrix coincide, so its eigenvalues are exactly $\{0,1,a_1-D\}$, and $|a_1-D|<1$ for $\rho\in(0,1]$. For $\rho<0$ both routes still give $\beta\lvert a_1-D\rvert<1$ wherever the matrix stays non-negative, $a_1\ge0$ and $D\le1$; off that set the condition can fail outright: at the cell just displayed the third eigenvalue is $-199/100$ and $\beta\cdot199/100>1$, so the coefficients that solve the two linear systems there are not discounted sums of anything.

**What is held fixed, and it is not nothing.** Under the approximation at $\phi=1$ the logged composites are affine in $x$, so the two instruments enter the owner’s payoff additively and $\partial^{2}U_C/\partial\ln i\,\partial\ln(1-\tau)=0$: his preferred tax does not depend on his own saving rate, and the intervals in $\tau$ below are intervals in the whole of $\tau$ rather than slices at some fixed $i$. That separability is a property of the approximation and not of the exact model, and it fails by the same obstruction as everything else in Section 4. Both instruments shift the successor ratio one for one, so the next period’s $E(x')=\ln s_K(x')+\psi(x')$ contributes
$$\frac{\partial^{2}E(x')}{\partial\ln i\,\partial\ln(1-\tau)} \;=\; E''(x') \;=\; \rho(1-\rho)\,s_K(x')s_L(x')$$
to the cross-partial, which vanishes at $\rho\in\{0,1\}$ and nowhere else. In the exact model the saving rate and the tax are chosen jointly; under the approximation they are not, and that is one more thing the approximation buys and should be seen to buy.

**Proposition 4.** *Since $1-\rho = 1/\sigma$, the two effective shares of (5.1) satisfy the exact identities*
$$1 - a_1 \;=\; (1-\rho)s_L \;=\; \frac{s_L}{\sigma}, \qquad a_2 \;=\; (1-\rho)s_K \;=\; \frac{s_K}{\sigma},$$
*so that (5.2) reads*
$$\tau_C \;=\; \frac{\beta^{2}\gamma(1-\theta_R)\bigl(s_L/\sigma\bigr)}{1-\beta+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)+\beta\gamma\theta_R\bigl(s_K/\sigma\bigr)}, \qquad
\tau_W \;=\; \frac{(1-\theta_R)\bigl(1-\beta+\beta s_L/\sigma\bigr)}{1-\theta_R+\theta_R\bigl(s_K/\sigma\bigr)} . \tag{5.3}$$
*Consequently $\tau_C$ (i) carries $s_L/\sigma$ as a factor, (ii) is strictly decreasing in the capital share whenever $\sigma<\infty$, and (iii) tends to zero as $s_K\to1$.*

*Proof.* The identities are immediate from (5.1) and $\rho = 1-1/\sigma$. (i) is then the displayed form. For (ii), $a_1$ enters only the numerator, through $-\beta^{2}\gamma(1-\theta_R)a_1$, and $a_2$ only the denominator, through $+\beta\gamma\theta_R a_2$; both are increasing in $s_K$; so both channels push $\tau_C$ down and $\partial\tau_C/\partial s_K<0$ strictly. The strictness needs $\rho<1$: at $\rho=1$ the two shares stop moving with $s_K$ altogether, $a_1\equiv1$, and $\tau_C$ is identically zero rather than decreasing. (iii) At $s_K=1$, $a_1=1$ and the numerator vanishes while the denominator is bounded away from zero. $\square$

**What Proposition 4 does not say, and the distinction is load-bearing.** $\tau_C$ is *not* proportional to labour’s share, and it is not proportional to $s_L/\sigma$ either. Proportionality asserts $\tau_C = \lambda\,(s_L/\sigma)$ with $\lambda$ free of everything the share moves with, and no such $\lambda$ exists: dividing (5.3) through by $s_L/\sigma$ leaves
$$\frac{\tau_C}{s_L/\sigma} \;=\; \frac{\beta^{2}\gamma(1-\theta_R)}{1-\beta+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)+\beta\gamma\theta_R\bigl(s_K/\sigma\bigr)}, \qquad
\frac{\partial}{\partial s_K}\!\left[\frac{\tau_C}{s_L/\sigma}\right] = \frac{-\beta^{3}\gamma^{2}\theta_R(1-\theta_R)(1-\rho)}{\bigl[\,\cdot\,\bigr]^{2}} \;<\;0,$$
because the capital share re-enters through $a_2 = s_K/\sigma$ in the denominator. For the identical reason the rate is not proportional in the Cobb–Douglas case either, and Proposition 4 states the three properties that do hold.

A reader who takes from Section 2(ii) the summary “capital’s preferred rate is proportional to labour’s share” would carry a claim that is false at every elasticity, including the one it was derived at, and would also miss the substantive result: the share enters *divided by $\sigma$*. Two economies with the same labour share and different elasticities of substitution do not give capital the same interest in funding reproduction. That is a statement about technology which the Cobb–Douglas model, where $\sigma$ is fixed at one, cannot make at all.

# 6. What capital-biased technical change does

Capital-augmenting progress moves both effective shares at the same rate, and both of them push capital’s preferred rate in the same direction. What follows reads the sign that results, shows that the worker’s rate does not follow it down, reports what the same comparison gives in the exact model when the owner is held to constant policies, shows that at the model’s own stationary ratio the approximating rate is an exact critical point of the exact constant-policy problem, and then lets the owner’s felicity take any constant curvature, which opens a second channel through his patience.

**Proposition 5.** *$B$ enters only through $x$, so $\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}\ln B = \mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}x$ and, by (3.1), both effective shares move together:*
$$\frac{\mathrm{d}a_1}{\mathrm{d}\ln B} \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{d}a_2}{\mathrm{d}\ln B} \;=\; \rho(1-\rho)\,s_K s_L .$$
*Since $\partial\tau_C/\partial a_1<0$ and $\partial\tau_C/\partial a_2<0$ by the proof of Proposition 4(ii),*
$$\operatorname{sign}\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau_C}{\mathrm{d}\ln B} \;=\; -\operatorname{sign}\bigl[\rho(1-\rho)\bigr],$$
*which is strictly negative for $\sigma\in(1,\infty)$ and zero at $\sigma=1$. For $\sigma<1$ the same expression is strictly positive, and it is the sign of a preferred rate wherever the transition matrix of Proposition 3 is entrywise non-negative, that is wherever $a_1\ge0$ and $D\le1$, the first of which is $s_L\le\sigma$; off that set it can be the sign of the formula (5.2) alone.*

The sign is carried as a theorem and not as a number: it is a product of a factor whose negativity is read off the closed form and a factor whose sign is exactly the sign of $\rho$. Proposition 5 reads the sign of (5.2) for every $a_1<1$, and two further elasticities bear it out: at $\sigma=\tfrac12$ the derivative reverses, and at $\sigma=1$ it is identically zero — as it must be, since Cobb–Douglas shares do not move with $B$ at all. The licence below unit elasticity needs no new argument, and it holds wherever $a_1\ge0$ and $D\le1$. Proposition 3 is stated for $\rho\in(0,1)$, but on the open box its interiority argument uses only $a_1\ge0$ and $a_2>0$. The spectral argument after it and the monotonicity argument after Proposition 6 use only a non-negative transition matrix. All three therefore carry over to $\rho<0$ wherever $a_1\ge0$ and $D\le1$, and the $\rho=-3$ cell of Proposition 3, where the licence fails, lies off that set. At $\sigma=\tfrac12$ and the Section 6 cell, $a_1=-0.4$ is the tangent at $\bar x=0$, where $s_K=\theta$, and that is not where Section 6 evaluates it. At the stationary ratios its computation reaches from $x_0=0$, $a_1$ is $0.41$, $0.14$ and $-0.08$ at $\ln B=-1,0,1$. The matrix is non-negative at the first two; at the third it has a negative entry, and the licence holds on direct check, with $\beta\lvert a_1-D\rvert=0.51<1$ and $a,b,c>0$. Across the three, (5.2) rises with $B$ (0.228, 0.362, 0.488) alongside the exact rate (0.2223, 0.3531, 0.4777), and at the self-consistent stationary ratios the two coincide (0.2254, 0.3569, 0.4807). To be sure, from $x_0=0$ the tangent misses the exact rate by up to $1.0\times10^{-2}$ at $\sigma=\tfrac12$, more than six times its largest miss at $\sigma=2$.

**Proposition 6.** *Let $\rho<1$, so that $a_2 = s_K/\sigma>0$ and $u = 1-a_1 = s_L/\sigma>0$; write $N = 1-\theta_R+\theta_R a_2>0$, so that the denominator of $\tau_C$ in (5.2) is exactly $1-\beta+\beta\gamma N$. Then*
$$\tau_W - \tau_C \;=\; \frac{(1-\theta_R)(1-\beta)\bigl[(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma N+\beta u\bigr]}{N\bigl(1-\beta+\beta\gamma N\bigr)} \;>\;0 \tag{6.1}$$
*at every admissible parameter with $\rho<1$, that is at every finite elasticity. At the admissible endpoint $\rho=1$ the same display holds with $u=0$ and $a_2=0$, where it reads $\tau_W-\tau_C=1-\beta>0$ with $\tau_C$ identically zero. And as $s_K\to1$ under $\sigma>1$,*
$$\tau_C \;\longrightarrow\; 0 \qquad\text{while}\qquad \tau_W \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{(1-\theta_R)(1-\beta)}{1-\theta_R+\theta_R/\sigma} \;>\;0 .$$

*Proof.* Substituting (5.2) and putting the difference over the common denominator $N(1-\beta+\beta\gamma N)$ gives the numerator
$$(1-\theta_R)\bigl[(1-\beta+\beta u)(1-\beta+\beta\gamma N) - \beta^{2}\gamma u N\bigr] = (1-\theta_R)(1-\beta)\bigl[(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma N+\beta u\bigr],$$
the cross terms in $uN$ cancelling exactly. Every factor is strictly positive for $\beta,\theta_R\in(0,1)$, $\gamma>0$ and $a_2>0$, and both denominators are bounded away from zero since $N>1-\theta_R>0$. The limits are Proposition 4(iii) and the substitution $a_1=1$, $a_2 = 1/\sigma$ in (5.2). $\square$

Under the approximation each class’s payoff in the tax is again $M\ln(1-\tau)+P\ln\tau$ plus terms free of $\tau$ — the transitions are log-linear by construction, so the argument of Section 2(ii) goes through verbatim. On $\rho\in(0,1)$ the transition matrix is entrywise non-negative and felicity is non-decreasing in the states, so the approximating Bellman operator preserves monotonicity and gives $a,c\ge0$; hence $M=(1-\omega)+\beta a \ge 1-\omega>0$ for the owner, and $P=\beta c>0$ because Proposition 3 puts $\tau_C = P/(M+P)$ in $(0,1)$ there. The second derivative is $-M/(1-\tau)^{2}-P/\tau^{2}<0$, so the stationary point is a maximum and not merely a critical point. With (6.1) the Pareto set in the tax is therefore $[\tau_C,\tau_W]$, a non-degenerate interval at every parameter with $\rho\in(0,1)$, and $(0,\tau_C)$ is the set of rates both classes strictly prefer to raise. For $\rho<0$ the same argument gives both bounds, and the same non-degenerate interval, wherever the transition matrix stays non-negative, $a_1\ge0$ and $D\le1$; off that set the monotonicity route is closed, and Proposition 3’s interiority or its spectral condition can fail.

Proposition 6 says that it is this second interval, and not the Pareto set, that capital-biased technical change destroys. In the limit there is no rate left on which the two classes agree that more would be better, while a band of rates over which they disagree survives with a strictly positive upper endpoint. The cancellation in the proof is the reason the two statements can be made separately: the term through which the worker’s peak and the owner’s peak are coupled, $\beta^{2}\gamma uN$, drops out of their difference, so the collapse of $\tau_C$ carries no implication for $\tau_W$ at all.

**The zero has a single cause, and the worker’s floor rises with the elasticity.** Capital’s preferred rate is zero exactly when $a_1=1$, since the numerator of (5.2) is $\beta^{2}\gamma(1-\theta_R)(1-a_1)$ and every other factor in it is strictly positive; and since $a_1=\rho+(1-\rho)s_K$, that happens if and only if $\rho=1$ or $s_K=1$. Perfect substitutability at any capital share and a unit capital share at any elasticity are therefore one result and not two limits. The first attains it, where the model closes exactly, the owner’s rate is identically zero and the worker’s is $1-\beta$; the second approaches it, which is Proposition 4(iii). The worker’s floor in that second limit, $(1-\theta_R)(1-\beta)/\bigl(1-\theta_R+\theta_R/\sigma\bigr)$, rises strictly with the elasticity, its denominator falling in $\sigma$ while its numerator is free of $\sigma$: at $\beta=0.9$ and $\theta_R=0.4$ it is $0.060$ at $\sigma=1$, $0.075$ at $\sigma=2$ and $0.086$ at $\sigma=4$. Substitutability widens the distance between the two classes’ preferred rates from both ends.

**Corroboration on the exact model.** The exact model has no closed form, but it does have an exact two-dimensional reduction, and that is enough to compute in it without approximating anything. Write $y = g-n$ and
$$E(x) \;=\; \ln s_K(x)+\psi(x), \qquad \Delta(x,y) \;=\; \gamma\theta_R\bigl(\ln s_L(x)+\psi(x)\bigr) + \gamma(1-\theta_R)y ,$$
so that $n'-n = \Delta(x,y)$. At $\phi=1$ the transitions of Section 3 then give, exactly,
$$x' \;=\; \ln B + \ln i + \ln(1-\tau) + E(x) - \Delta(x,y), \qquad
y' \;=\; \ln\tau + E(x) - \Delta(x,y) . \tag{6.2}$$
**Lemma 1.** *Fix $\rho\in(0,1)$ and let the constant policies range over a compact $\mathcal{K}\subset(0,1)^{2}$. Along (6.2) the difference $x_t-y_t$ is constant from $t=1$, so the reduced dynamics are the scalar map $x_{t+1}=\mathcal{T}(x_t)$ with $\mathcal{T}' = a_1(x)-D(x)$ — the coefficients of (5.1) and of Proposition 3 read at $x$ itself rather than at a fixed reference ratio — and*
$$\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\mathcal{T}'(x)\bigr| \;\le\; \lambda \;\equiv\; \max\bigl\{\,\lvert\rho-\gamma(1-\theta_R)\rvert,\; 1-\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)\,\bigr\} \;<\;1 . \tag{6.3}$$
*Hence $\mathcal{T}$ is a contraction with a unique fixed point reached geometrically from any $x_0$; the policy derivatives $\partial x_t/\partial\vartheta$ are bounded uniformly in $t$; the logged stocks’ policy derivatives grow at most linearly in $t$; and each class’s discounted payoff may be differentiated term by term, the differentiated series being dominated on $\mathcal{K}$ by $C(1+t)\beta^{t}$ and hence uniformly convergent there.*

*Proof.* Subtracting the two lines of (6.2) gives $x'-y' = \ln B+\ln i+\ln(1-\tau)-\ln\tau$, which is free of the state; writing $\kappa$ for that constant and substituting $y=x-\kappa$ leaves $\mathcal{T}(x)=\ln B+\ln i+\ln(1-\tau)+E(x)-\Delta(x,x-\kappa)$, whose derivative is $E'-\gamma\theta_R(\ln s_L+\psi)'-\gamma(1-\theta_R) = a_1(x)-D(x)$ by (3.1). That difference is strictly increasing in $s_K$, with slope $(1-\rho)(1-\gamma\theta_R)>0$, so it is bracketed by its two endpoint values — $\rho-\gamma(1-\theta_R)$ as $s_K\to0$ and $1-\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)$ as $s_K\to1$ — and both lie strictly inside $(-1,1)$ for $\gamma,\theta_R\in(0,1)$ and $\rho\in(0,1)$; that is (6.3), and contraction with geometric convergence follows. For the policy derivatives, $\lvert\partial x_{t+1}/\partial\vartheta\rvert\le\lambda\lvert\partial x_t/\partial\vartheta\rvert+\bar d$, where $\bar d=\bar d(\mathcal{K})<\infty$ bounds the effect of one policy coordinate on one step holding the state fixed — finite because the controls enter the transitions only through $\ln i$, $\ln(1-\tau)$ and $\ln\tau$, each with a bounded derivative on $\mathcal{K}$ — so $\lvert\partial x_t/\partial\vartheta\rvert\le\bar d/(1-\lambda)$ for every $t$. The logged stocks are cumulated: $n_t = n_0+\sum_{s<t}\Delta_s$ and $k_t$ is $n_{t-1}$ plus terms bounded by the same argument, so their policy derivatives are $O(t)$. The period returns are affine in the logged stocks and smooth in $(x,y,i,\tau)$ on the compact set the path visits, so the $t$-th term of the differentiated payoff is bounded on $\mathcal{K}$ by $C(1+t)\beta^{t}$ for a constant $C$ free of $t$, and $\sum_t(1+t)\beta^{t}<\infty$. The Weierstrass M-test then gives uniform convergence of the differentiated series, which licenses the term-by-term differentiation used below. $\square$

The logged stocks themselves have dropped out: every non-linearity enters through $x$ alone, and $(x,y)$ is a sufficient state. Under constant policies (6.2) is a deterministic difference equation, so each class’s payoff is a discounted sum along a single path, computable to machine precision with a geometric tail bound, and the preferred rates can be found by direct maximisation with no approximation anywhere. Payoffs are read from $(x_0,y_0)=(0,0)$, discounting leaving every period beyond the four-hundredth worth less than $10^{-15}$, and the owner maximises over constant $(i,\tau)$ in $[10^{-4},1-10^{-4}]^{2}$. Every maximiser reported is interior with a negative-definite Hessian, and where the global maximisers lie is settled over the whole of that square rather than at the rates the table prints: every global maximiser lies within $7\times10^{-3}$ of the tabulated rates at each of the five values of $B$ — at $\ln B=0$, in $i\in[0.765,0.779]$ and $\tau\in[0.270,0.282]$. The rates below are therefore within $7\times10^{-3}$ of every rate that is best for the owner on constant policies, and not merely the best among the rates computed. The exact $\tau_W$ is the worker’s preferred rate at the owner’s optimal saving rate $i^{*}$, and the frozen-share column is Section 2(ii) at the unrounded $s_K(x^{*})$. Across the five values of $B$ computed, capital’s share at the realised stationary ratio rises with $B$. That is the direction (i) of Section 2 gives at a fixed ratio, and it survives a moving ratio as well, as the bound stated below shows. The exact $\tau_C$ on constant policies falls monotonically across the same five values, as Proposition 5 predicts. All four steps in that fall are ordered over the whole control square, and not only at the rates the table prints: at each of them, every global maximiser at the higher $B$ carries a strictly lower $\tau_C$ than every one at the lower $B$. The first step is the tightest, the two tabulated rates there differing by $1.15\times10^{-2}$, and there the two sets of maximisers stand $3.0\times10^{-3}$ apart. The exact model behaves the same way at those two elasticities: at $\sigma=\tfrac12$ the exact $\tau_C$ *rises* with $B$ (0.2223, 0.3531, 0.4777 at $\ln B=-1,0,1$), and at $\sigma=1$ it does not move at all (0.4012 at every $B$, which is Section 2(ii) at $\alpha=\theta$). The frozen-share column moves in the right direction and is wrong by up to 0.299 across the five rows. The tangent (5.2) is a different matter, and the point at which it is evaluated decides the figure. At the printed column — rates optimised from $x_0=0$, the stationary ratio read off the path they induce — the tangent misses the exact $\tau_C$ by up to $1.6\times10^{-3}$ across the five rows. At the self-consistent stationary ratio, where the rates are re-optimised from the stationary state itself, the two coincide exactly — as critical points, in the sense the next three sentences make precise. Started there, the exact path never leaves $x^{*}$, and neither does the tangent model’s, which agrees with (6.2) in level and slope at $x^{*}$. By Lemma 1 those payoffs may be differentiated term by term, and the gradient with respect to constant policies then depends only on the path and on the slopes of the transitions and of felicity along it; here those slopes are the coefficients of (5.1) at $\bar x=x^{*}$. The exact first-order conditions are therefore the tangent model’s, and (5.2) is an exact critical point of the constant-policy problem. It is a critical point and not a proved global maximiser: the Hessian there is negative definite, which makes it a strict local maximum, and at $\ln B\in\{-1,0,1\}$ no other maximum is found and the two rates coincide at every digit computed — but globality over the control box is not proved — unlike the printed column, where the search over the whole control square reported above locates every global maximiser. Nor is it a solution of the exact economy: it is a statement about one starting state, and from $x_0=0$ the gap is the $1.6\times10^{-3}$ above. The second point is the one the approximation is built at; the first is the one the table prints.

**The stationary ratio moves with the technique, and the response is bounded.** Proposition 5 differentiates holding the ratio fixed, which is the scope of the share result of Section 2(i). The computation above lets the ratio move, and at constant policies the direction survives as a theorem and not as a sample: for $\sigma>1$ the stationary ratio is strictly increasing in $\ln B$ at every admissible parameter. The margin by which the map of Lemma 1 is a contraction bounds the response itself,
$$0 \;<\; \frac{\mathrm{d}x^{*}}{\mathrm{d}\ln B} \;\le\; \frac{1+\gamma(1-\theta_R)}{\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)},$$
at every admissible parameter, and at the values used above that ceiling is $3.25$. Capital’s share is strictly increasing in the ratio by its definition in Section 3, and (5.2) falls strictly in that share for $\sigma\in(1,\infty)$ by the proof of Proposition 4(ii), so the approximating model’s rate falls with $B$ at the moving ratio as well.

Re-optimising the policies at the stationary ratio itself is a further step, and what it puts at issue is the sign of a denominator. The response of the stationary ratio is then $A/\bigl[(1-m')+s_K'(x^{*})J\bigr]$, where $A = 1+\gamma(1-\theta_R)>0$, the direct channel $1-m'$ exceeds $\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)>0$ at every admissible parameter, and $J$ collects the responses of the re-optimised rates, which are strictly negative term by term. A negative $J$ in the denominator does not pull against the direct channel: while the denominator stays positive it makes the stationary ratio respond more to $B$, not less. What it could do is turn the denominator negative, which would take the feedback $s_K'(x^{*})\lvert J\rvert$ exceeding the direct channel $1-m'$. It does not, at the $\beta$, $\gamma$, $\theta_R$ and $\omega$ used above, for every $\rho\in[0.001,0.999]$ — every elasticity from about $1.001$ to $10^{3}$ — paired with every capital share in $[0.001,0.999]$, and at each such pair rather than at a sample drawn from them. So $\mathrm{d}s_K(x^{*})/\mathrm{d}\ln B>0$ survives re-optimisation there, and with it the fall of (5.2) at the moving ratio. That is a statement about the approximating model: the two rates share a critical point at the self-consistent stationary ratio and, as computed above, agree nowhere else.

The mechanism runs through one channel, the share, and the elasticity of substitution decides both whether technical change moves it and by how much it is discounted when it arrives. Automation weakens capital’s interest in funding the reproduction of labour through the rise in capital’s share. The elasticity enters that weakening twice, in the numerator’s $s_L/\sigma$ and again in the denominator’s $s_K/\sigma$, so its size is what (5.3) gives and not the rise in the share divided by $\sigma$ — the same reason the rate is not proportional to $s_L/\sigma$. And the arithmetic has an endpoint at which that interest is zero and the worker’s is not.

The sign just read rests on the owner valuing the logarithm of his aggregate of consumption and holdings, and the same exact model says how far. Give him felicity of constant curvature $\eta>0$ in that aggregate,
$$u_\eta(C_C,K) \;=\; \frac{\bigl(C_C^{1-\omega}K^{\omega}\bigr)^{1-\eta}-1}{1-\eta},$$
which is the felicity of Section 3 at $\eta=1$, and leave the worker’s felicity and every transition unchanged.

**Proposition 7.** *Let $\rho\in(0,1)$ and $\phi=1$, and hold the owner to constant policies with felicity $u_\eta$. (i) Along (6.2) every logged stock grows, after a transient that vanishes geometrically, at the common rate $\mu=\Delta(x^{*},x^{*}-\kappa)$, where $x^{*}$ is the fixed point of Lemma 1 and $\kappa=\ln B+\ln i+\ln(1-\tau)-\ln\tau$; for $\eta\ne1$ the owner’s payoff is finite if and only if $\beta e^{(1-\eta)\mu}<1$. (ii) At $\beta=0.9$, $\gamma=0.5$, $\theta_R=0.4$, $\theta=0.3$, $\omega=0.2$ and $\sigma=2$, $\mu$ is strictly increasing in $i$ and in $\ln B$ and strictly negative at every policy in $[10^{-4},1-10^{-4}]^{2}$ at each value of $B$ in the table. Its supremum there is $-1.0956$, $-0.9880$, $-0.8725$, $-0.7441$ and $-0.5937$ at $\ln B=-1,-0.5,0,0.5,1$, so for $\eta>1$ some constant policy gives the owner a finite payoff if and only if $\eta<\bar\eta(B)\equiv1+\ln(1/\beta)/\lvert\sup\mu\rvert$, which is $1.096$, $1.107$, $1.121$, $1.142$ and $1.177$.*

*Proof.* (i) By Lemma 1, $x_t\to x^{*}$ geometrically and $y_t=x_t-\kappa$ from $t=1$, so $n_{t+1}-n_t=\Delta(x_t,y_t)\to\mu$ geometrically. Each of $\ln C_{C,t}$, $k_t$ and $g_t$ is $n_t$ or $n_{t-1}$ plus a function of the current or previous ratio, so every logged stock grows at $\mu$, and $m_t\equiv(1-\omega)\ln C_{C,t}+\omega k_t=\mu t+c+\varepsilon_t$ with $\varepsilon_t\to0$ geometrically. The payoff is $\sum_t\beta^{t}\bigl(e^{(1-\eta)m_t}-1\bigr)/(1-\eta)$. Its $t$-th term is $\beta^{t}e^{(1-\eta)(\mu t+c)}/(1-\eta)$ times a factor tending to one, less a summable term, so the sum converges if and only if $\beta e^{(1-\eta)\mu}<1$, and otherwise diverges to $-\infty$ for $\eta>1$ and to $+\infty$ for $\eta<1$. (ii) Write $a=\gamma(1-\theta_R)$, $b=\gamma\theta_R$ and $z=e^{x/2}$. At $\sigma=2$, $\psi=2\ln D$ with $D=\theta z+1-\theta$, so $E=\ln\theta+\ln z+\ln D$ and $\ln s_L+\psi=\ln(1-\theta)+\ln D$. Eliminating $y^{*}$ and $\Delta(x^{*},y^{*})$ from the fixed point of (6.2) gives $(1+2a)\ln z^{*}-(1-b)\ln D^{*}=Q$, with $Q=(1+a)\bigl[\ln B+\ln i+\ln(1-\tau)\bigr]+\ln\theta-b\ln(1-\theta)-a\ln\tau$, and
$$\mu \;=\; \frac{b\ln(1-\theta)+a\bigl(\ln\theta+\ln\tau+\ln z^{*}\bigr)+\gamma\ln D^{*}}{1+a}.$$
The left side of the first equation has derivative $(1+2a)-(1-b)s_K>0$ in $\ln z^{*}$, $\mu$ is strictly increasing in $z^{*}$, and $Q$ is strictly increasing in $\ln i$ and in $\ln B$; that is the monotonicity. At the stated point $(1+2a)/(1-b)=2$ exactly, so $z^{*}$ is the positive root of $z^{2}-K\theta z-K(1-\theta)=0$ with $K=e^{Q/(1-b)}$, and $\mu$ is explicit. By the monotonicity in $i$ its supremum over the square is its maximum over $\tau\in[10^{-4},1-10^{-4}]$ at $i=1-10^{-4}$, an explicit continuous function of $\tau$ alone, so it is attained. At the five values of $B$ that maximum equals the five negative numbers stated. For $\eta>1$ the condition of (i) reads $(\eta-1)\lvert\mu\rvert<\ln(1/\beta)$. Since $\mu<0$ and the supremum is attained, some policy satisfies it if and only if $(\eta-1)\lvert\sup\mu\rvert<\ln(1/\beta)$, which is the frontier. $\square$

Numerically, at the same point and with capital’s preferred rate taken, for $\eta\ne1$, at the owner’s interior local maximum rather than over the whole square, the rate falls strictly across the five values of $B$ at $\eta=0.95$, $1.02$, $1.05$, $1.08$ and $1.09$, as at $\eta=1$. At $\eta=0.9$ it rises over the first step, from $\ln B=-1$ to $-0.5$, and falls over the other three. At $\eta=0.85$ it rises over the first two steps, and at $\eta=0.8$ over the first three. At $\eta=0.1,0.2,\dots,0.7$ it rises at every step. Between $\eta=0.9$ and $0.95$ the first step becomes a fall between $\eta=0.9103$ and $\eta=0.9105$.

At this point the sign of the exact model depends on the owner’s logarithmic felicity. Every stock shrinks under every constant policy here, the workforce to between 0.31 and 0.51 of its size each generation at the rates of the table. Curvature other than one turns that shrinkage into discounting: the owner weighs generation $t$ by $\beta^{t}e^{(1-\eta)m_t}$, so on a stationary path his discount factor is in effect $\beta e^{(1-\eta)\mu}$. Because $\mu$ rises with $B$, at a given policy an owner with $\eta<1$ discounts less as capital-augmenting progress proceeds, and one with $\eta>1$ discounts more. In (5.2) a more patient owner prefers a higher rate. With $\eta>1$ his felicity falls without bound as his stocks shrink, and above $\bar\eta(B)$ he has no constant policy of finite value. How fast the stocks shrink, however, is set by the levels in which output and the workforce are measured, and along the family of levels described next nothing in Sections 4 and 5 moves.

**Lemma 2.** *Give production a level $A>0$ and the reproduction technology a level $\Psi>0$, so that $\ln Y=n+\ln A+\psi(x)$ and the worker transition of Section 3 gains $\gamma\theta_R\ln A+\ln\Psi$. Along the family $\ln A=\delta$, $\ln\Psi=(1-\gamma\theta_R)\delta$, $\delta\in\mathbb{R}$, the reduced dynamics (6.2), every factor share and the coefficients of (5.1) are unchanged, and every logged stock gains $\delta t$, so the common growth rate of Proposition 7(i) is $\mu+\delta$. At $\eta=1$ each class’s payoff changes by a constant free of policy, so every preferred rate of Sections 2 to 6 is unchanged. For $\eta\ne1$ the owner’s payoff at $\delta$ is a positive multiple of his payoff at $\delta=0$ with $\beta$ replaced by $\beta e^{(1-\eta)\delta}$, plus a constant free of policy.*

*Proof.* With the two levels, $x'$ and $y'$ in (6.2) each gain $(1-\gamma\theta_R)\ln A-\ln\Psi$, which is zero along the family, and $n'-n$ gains $\gamma\theta_R\ln A+\ln\Psi=\delta$. So $n_t$ gains $\delta t$ from a given $n_0$. Each of $\ln C_{C,t}$ and $k_t$ is $n_t$ or $n_{t-1}$ plus terms in the ratio and the policy, with $\ln A$ entering once more, so $m_t$ gains $\delta t+(1-\omega)\delta$. At $\eta=1$ that adds $\sum_t\beta^t[\delta t+(1-\omega)\delta]$ to the owner’s payoff and $\sum_t\beta^t\delta t$ to the worker’s. For $\eta\ne1$ the owner’s period-$t$ term $\beta^te^{(1-\eta)m_t}/(1-\eta)$ becomes $e^{(1-\eta)(1-\omega)\delta}\bigl(\beta e^{(1-\eta)\delta}\bigr)^te^{(1-\eta)m_t}/(1-\eta)$. $\square$

The frontier of Proposition 7 is therefore a property of the level of growth, which the results of Sections 4 and 5 leave free. As $\delta$ runs over the line, the frontier of Proposition 7(i), read at growth $\mu+\delta$, takes every value above one at the lowest technique, and at every member of the family that grows, every $\eta\ge1$ is admissible. What curvature does to the owner’s choice is the following.

**Proposition 8.** *Let $\rho\in[0,1)$ and $\phi=1$, start the economy at the stationary state $(x^{*},y^{*})$ of (6.2) under a constant policy $\pi^{*}$, and let $\mu^{*}$ be the growth rate of Proposition 7(i) under $\pi^{*}$, with $\tilde\beta\equiv\beta e^{(1-\eta)\mu^{*}}<1$. Then at $\pi^{*}$ the gradient of the owner’s payoff with felicity $u_\eta$ equals $e^{(1-\eta)m_0}$ times the gradient of the payoff of an owner with logarithmic felicity and discount factor $\tilde\beta$. At the self-consistent stationary ratio his preferred rate is therefore a critical point of the constant-policy problem at*
$$\tau_C(\eta) \;=\; \frac{\tilde\beta^{2}\gamma(1-\theta_R)(1-a_1)}{1-\tilde\beta+\tilde\beta\gamma N},\qquad N=1-\theta_R+\theta_R a_2, \tag{6.4}$$
*which is (5.2) with $\beta$ replaced by $\tilde\beta$, evaluated at the growth his own policy induces.*

*Proof.* Under $\pi^{*}$ from $(x^{*},y^{*})$, $m_t=m_0+\mu^{*}t$ for every $t$. The derivative of the owner’s payoff in a policy coordinate $\vartheta$ is $\sum_t\beta^te^{(1-\eta)m_t}\,\partial m_t/\partial\vartheta=e^{(1-\eta)m_0}\sum_t\tilde\beta^{\,t}\,\partial m_t/\partial\vartheta$, and the sequence $\partial m_t/\partial\vartheta$ depends only on the transitions, so it is the same for every felicity. Term-by-term differentiation is licensed as in Lemma 1, whose bound (6.3) holds at $\rho=0$ by the same endpoint argument, the series being dominated by $C(1+t)\tilde\beta^{\,t}$. The second sum is the derivative of $\sum_t\tilde\beta^{\,t}m_t$, the payoff of a logarithmic owner with discount factor $\tilde\beta$. The argument after the table above shows that, at the self-consistent stationary ratio, the first-order conditions of such an owner are those of the tangent model of Section 5, for any discount factor in $(0,1)$; at $\tilde\beta$ they give (6.4). $\square$

On the economy’s own stationary path, then, the first-order conditions of an owner of any curvature are those of a logarithmic owner whose patience has been adjusted by the growth his choice brings about. Every closed form of Section 5 carries over to his critical rate with $\tilde\beta$ in place of $\beta$, and curvature acts on that rate only through the adjustment.

**Proposition 9.** *Let $\rho\in[0,1)$ and hold the policy constant. The stationary ratio and the growth rate rise with the technique,*
$$\frac{\mathrm{d}x^{*}}{\mathrm{d}\ln B}=\frac{1+\gamma(1-\theta_R)}{1-a_1+D}>0,\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\ln B}=\frac{\gamma\bigl[(1-\rho)s_K+\rho(1-\theta_R)\bigr]}{1-a_1+D}>0,$$
*with $D$ as in Proposition 3, and the response of (6.4) is the sum of two terms,*
$$\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau_C}{\mathrm{d}\ln B}=\Bigl(\frac{\partial\tau_C}{\partial a_1}+\frac{\partial\tau_C}{\partial a_2}\Bigr)\rho(1-\rho)s_Ks_L\,\frac{\mathrm{d}x^{*}}{\mathrm{d}\ln B}\;+\;\frac{\partial\tau_C}{\partial\tilde\beta}\,\tilde\beta\,(1-\eta)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\ln B}. \tag{6.5}$$
*(i) For $\sigma\in[1,\infty)$ and $\eta\ge1$, other than $\sigma=\eta=1$, capital-augmenting progress strictly lowers the rate (6.4). (ii) At $\sigma=1$ it lowers it for $\eta>1$ and raises it for $\eta<1$. (iii) For $\sigma\in(1,\infty)$ and $\eta<1$ the two terms have opposite signs.*

*Proof.* At a constant policy the fixed point of Lemma 1 solves $[1+\gamma(1-\theta_R)]x^{*}-E(x^{*})+\gamma\theta_R\bigl(\ln s_L+\psi\bigr)(x^{*})=[1+\gamma(1-\theta_R)]\ln B$ plus terms in the policy, and implicit differentiation gives $\mathrm{d}x^{*}/\mathrm{d}\ln B$ with denominator $1-a_1+D=(1-\rho)s_L+\gamma(1-\theta_R)+\gamma\theta_R(1-\rho)s_K>0$. The growth rate is $\mu=\gamma\theta_R\bigl(\ln s_L+\psi\bigr)(x^{*})+\gamma(1-\theta_R)(x^{*}-\kappa)$ with $\kappa$ rising one for one in $\ln B$, so $\mathrm{d}\mu/\mathrm{d}\ln B=\gamma\theta_Ra_2\,\mathrm{d}x^{*}/\mathrm{d}\ln B+\gamma(1-\theta_R)\bigl(\mathrm{d}x^{*}/\mathrm{d}\ln B-1\bigr)$, which reduces to the display; its numerator is positive for $\rho\in[0,1)$. In (6.4), $\tau_C$ falls strictly in $a_1$ and $a_2$ by the proof of Proposition 4(ii), which uses no property of the discount factor, and rises in $\tilde\beta$:
$$\frac{\partial\tau_C}{\partial\tilde\beta}=\frac{\tilde\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)(1-a_1)\bigl(2-\tilde\beta+\tilde\beta\gamma N\bigr)}{\bigl(1-\tilde\beta+\tilde\beta\gamma N\bigr)^{2}}>0 .$$
Both shares move with $x^{*}$ at rate $\rho(1-\rho)s_Ks_L$ by (3.1), and $\ln\tilde\beta$ moves with $\mu$ at rate $1-\eta$. The chain rule gives (6.5). The first term is negative for $\rho\in(0,1)$ and zero at $\rho=0$; the second has the sign of $1-\eta$. That is (i)–(iii). $\square$

Proposition 9 says that capital-augmenting progress reaches the owner’s interest in funding the reproduction of labour through two channels. The first is distribution: where the factors are substitutes, progress raises capital’s share, and the owner’s income then depends less on the workforce the tax produces. The second is growth: progress raises the rate at which every stock grows, and an owner whose elasticity of intertemporal substitution, $1/\eta$, is below one values a richer future less at the margin, so his effective patience falls and with it the rate he would pay for the next generation of workers. Unit elasticity closes the first channel and logarithmic felicity closes the second, and Cobb–Douglas with logarithmic felicity closes both, which is why that pair answers that automation does nothing to the fiscal coalition. Where both channels are open and the owner’s elasticity of intertemporal substitution is at most one, they push his rate the same way. Estimates of that elasticity place an owner of assets well inside that region: after correcting for selective reporting, Havránek (2015) puts the mean for asset holders at about 0.3 to 0.4 and finds calibrations above 0.8 inconsistent with most of the evidence. An owner more willing than that to substitute over time, $\eta<1$, discounts less as growth rises, so the second channel pulls against the first, and which one prevails depends on the remaining primitives.

**Corollary 1.** *At every curvature at which some constant policy gives the owner a finite payoff, the rate (6.4) lies strictly below the worker’s preferred rate in (5.2), and it tends to zero as capital’s share tends to one.*

*Proof.* (6.4) rises in $\tilde\beta$, and at $\tilde\beta=1$ it equals $(1-\theta_R)(1-a_1)/N$, which falls short of the worker’s $(1-\theta_R)(1-\beta a_1)/N$ by $(1-\theta_R)a_1(1-\beta)/N>0$. Its numerator carries $1-a_1=s_L/\sigma$. $\square$

The gap between the classes therefore survives every curvature at which the owner has a policy of finite value: whatever his attitude to his own future, his critical rate stays below the worker’s and falls to zero with labour’s share.

Numerically, at the cell of Proposition 7(ii) with $\delta=1.6$, so that growth at the owner’s chosen policy lies between 0.17 and 0.66 a generation, capital’s preferred rate on constant policies falls strictly across the five values of $B$ at $\eta=1.5$, $2$, $3$ and $5$; at $\eta=2$ it runs $0.143$, $0.114$, $0.087$, $0.064$ and $0.044$. Under Cobb–Douglas at the same cell it does not move at $\eta=1$ (0.4012 throughout), falls from $0.125$ to $0.073$ at $\eta=2$ at the same levels, and rises from $0.057$ to $0.111$ at $\eta=\tfrac12$ at the cell’s own levels.

# 7. Conclusion

This paper shows that where a tax on capital income funds the technology that produces the workforce, the owner of capital prefers a strictly positive rate of his own at the provision corner — in closed form under Cobb–Douglas, and in the approximating model away from it — and it asks what capital-biased technical change does to that preference once the technology is allowed to move factor shares. Three things follow.

The first is that the default aggregator cannot be used to ask the question. An affine-in-logs value function solves the owner’s problem at the provision corner only at unit elasticity and at perfect substitutability (Proposition 1), and with logarithmic felicity at unit elasticity technical change moves neither factor share nor capital’s preferred rate (Proposition 5). Asked what automation does to the fiscal coalition, a Cobb–Douglas model with logarithmic felicity answers “nothing”, and it gives that answer by construction rather than by finding: unit elasticity closes the channel through distribution and logarithmic felicity closes the channel through growth (Proposition 9).

The second is that where capital and labour are substitutes the answer has a sign, and a mechanism with one channel. Capital’s preferred rate carries labour’s share divided by the elasticity of substitution and falls strictly in capital’s share, so capital-augmenting progress lowers it at every elasticity above one; on constant policies in the exact model it falls as well at $\sigma=2$, where the sign is corroborated rather than proved. Off logarithmic felicity a second channel opens through the owner’s patience, and at a given policy it pushes the same way wherever his elasticity of intertemporal substitution is below one (Proposition 9). The elasticity enters that weakening twice, once in the numerator and once in the denominator, which is why the magnitude is what the closed form says and not the rise in the share divided by the elasticity. The worker’s preferred rate stays bounded away from zero, and its limiting floor rises with the elasticity, so substitutability widens the distance between what the two classes want from both ends.

The third is what that does to the politics of the transfer. The band of rates both classes want raised is non-empty at every finite technique and empties only as capital’s share approaches one, while the band they disagree over survives with a strictly positive upper end. With felicity of any constant curvature, the owner’s critical rate stays below the worker’s and tends to zero as capital’s share tends to one (Corollary 1). A coalition for the public funding of reproduction is therefore a magnitude with a sign attached to a technological parameter, rather than a standing feature of a capitalist economy. Where capital and labour are substitutes, the same technical change that raises output erodes the part of the fiscal question on which the two classes agree without touching the part on which they do not. And if such funding survives automation, it survives as a contested transfer rather than a joint investment, with an argument for it addressed to the owner’s own interest weakening exactly as automation proceeds. That capital needs the working class reproduced is an old proposition in classical political economy. Where that reproduction is publicly funded, the proposition acquires a magnitude, and the magnitude has a sign attached to the elasticity of substitution.

The positive results about capital’s preferred rate are exact statements about the approximating model of Section 5, and the exact model is computed rather than solved. Its sign is read over the whole control square at $\sigma=2$, and at one point in the remaining primitives, with the owner restricted to policies constant over time — the class in which the Cobb–Douglas closed forms live. The worker values the logarithm of the workforce throughout. The owner’s felicity of any constant curvature enters in Section 6, where the two channels are read at the self-consistent stationary ratio on constant policies, with the policy held fixed as the technique moves (Propositions 8 and 9), and the level of growth, which the logarithmic results leave free, bounds his curvature and sets his effective patience (Lemma 2). The closed forms are objects of the provision corner, where all revenue is provided in kind. Away from that corner the preferred rate is a function of the state under any affine continuation that loads the worker stock (Proposition 2). At the self-consistent stationary ratio the approximating rate is a critical point of the exact constant-policy problem and a strict local maximum, the curvature there being negative definite in both instruments. That it is the global one is checked over the control square and not proved, and concavity would not be structural: the two logged income composites are convex in the ratio and enter the owner’s continuation with positive weight. Four extensions would settle what is left. The first is the sign over all state-dependent rather than constant policies, which the two-dimensional reduction of Section 6 makes computable. The second is whether the denominator that carries the re-optimised stationary ratio stays positive at every parameter and not only over the region computed there, and, off logarithmic felicity, once the owner’s effective patience responds to his re-optimised policy as well. The third is a bound over the whole control square at the self-consistent ratio, which the contraction of Lemma 1 keeps finite. The fourth is the sign where the two channels conflict, for an owner more willing to substitute over time than the estimates suggest, over a box of the remaining primitives rather than the one point at which it is read here.

What the paper changes is the reading of a familiar zero. The standing zero for capital income taxation (Judd 1985; Chamley 1986) is a result about a government’s optimum when the revenue leaves production. Put the revenue into the production of the workforce and a second zero appears, this one the owner’s own peak, and it arrives only where his income channel has unit elasticity in the effective ratio — at perfect substitutability, or at a capital share of one. Everywhere else his peak is strictly positive, and technical change is what moves it. The question an argument for social provision has to answer is therefore not whether capital ever has a stake in it, but how much of that stake the current technique has left.
