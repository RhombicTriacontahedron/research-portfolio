# Mortal Labour: Horizon, Old-Age Claims and Who Wants the Next Working Generation Reared
Carlos Galindo Escajeda
2026-09-01

# Abstract

Which class wants the next working generation reared more is decided by
one inequality, and the inequality splits exactly into a term for
horizon and a term for ownership. A working cohort whose only stake in
the next generation is its old-age claim wants a larger levy for rearing
than capital does, everywhere on Cobb–Douglas production and, off it, at
a state stationary under capital’s own policy and at horizons where
capital’s stationary problem is concave, if and only if
$\beta\lambda_C\,[1+s(1-\gamma)]+\beta\lambda_C\,\frac{1-\sigma}{\sigma}\,[s(1-\gamma\theta)+\gamma(1-\theta)(1-s)]<1$,
where $\beta\lambda_C$ is capital’s generational horizon, $s$ the
capital share, $\sigma$ the elasticity of substitution, and $\gamma$ and
$\theta$ describe rearing. The first term is the threshold of a single
planner with capital’s horizon, and no elasticity enters it. The second
is the weight of capital’s claims on profits, and it vanishes only at
unit elasticity.

Workers live two periods, and their old age is a claim on the product of
the generation they rear. Capitalist generations are linked by altruism,
and their capital outlives them. A levy on capital income finances a
public input to rearing. On Cobb–Douglas production a working cohort
that is not altruistic toward its successors prefers the levy that
maximises the next generation’s output, and that levy depends on none of
the preference parameters. Capital’s preferred levy is zero without
altruism and rises with it. There the inequality reads
$\beta\lambda_C(1+\alpha(1-\gamma))<1$, whatever the level of the
pension and however it divides between wages and profits. When the
public input rears the generation after next, the working cohort wants
no levy, and capital wants a positive one exactly when it cares about
its successors. Off unit elasticity the results hold at a state
stationary under capital’s own policy. There, at horizons below a bound
that makes capital’s stationary problem strictly concave, an interior
levy of capital’s that keeps the state stationary must be its
first-order levy, and the ordering turns on a threshold in the capital
share. A bound on capital’s horizon, in terms of the elasticity and the
capital share alone, rules out any reversal whatever the rearing
technology. And for elasticities at most one, where horizon alone
favours labour, only capital’s own claims on profits can reverse the
ordering.

# 1. Introduction

Two literatures answer the question of who wants public money spent on
the next generation, and each supplies half of the answer. In
representative-agent models of productive public spending the
growth-maximising share of the public input exceeds the
welfare-maximising one (surveyed in Misch, Gemmell and Kneller 2013,
p. 11). That gap is a gap in horizon, and there are no classes to assign
it to. In Galor and Moav (2006) a tax funds public schooling and rich
and poor agree on its rate. Their footnote 42 attributes the agreement
to the linear tax and unit elasticity, and states that above unit
elasticity the poor would prefer higher taxes and below it the rich. The
rate they agree on maximises next period’s output per worker. Here only
the working cohort’s preferred levy maximises next period’s output
(Proposition 1); capital’s generally does not. In their model the
classes are divided by what they own, and the elasticity signs the
division. A reader of the first expects classes to disagree about the
next generation only as far as they discount it differently. A reader of
the second expects them to agree at unit elasticity.

This paper shows that the two answers are the two terms of one
inequality. When workers are mortal cohorts whose old age is a claim on
the product of the generation they rear, and capitalists are generations
whose capital outlives them, labour wants the larger levy for rearing,
on Cobb–Douglas production at every state and off it at a state
stationary under capital’s own policy where capital’s stationary problem
is concave, if and only if
$\beta\lambda_C[1+s(1-\gamma)]+\beta\lambda_C\frac{1-\sigma}{\sigma}[s(1-\gamma\theta)+\gamma(1-\theta)(1-s)]<1$
(Propositions 3, 8 and 10). The first term is the planner’s wedge with
one horizon assigned to each class. No elasticity enters it, and on
Cobb–Douglas production it is the whole threshold, so there the classes
disagree through horizon alone, at the elasticity at which Galor and
Moav’s agree. The second term is capital’s claims on profits: its
valuation of the capital share, its accumulation out of it and the base
on which the levy is raised. It has the sign of their footnote, positive
below unit elasticity, where it can make the owner the class that wants
more, and negative above. Each literature therefore omits a term that
the other does not supply. Off unit elasticity the levy compared is
capital’s preferred levy at that state (Proposition 7). That this levy
is also capital’s optimum in its full dynamic programme is not
established.

Marx put the question in two consecutive sentences of chapter 23 of
*Capital*: *“The maintenance and reproduction of the working class is,
and must ever be, a necessary condition to the reproduction of capital.
But the capitalist may safely leave its fulfilment to the labourer’s
instincts of self-preservation and of propagation.”* The first sentence
says whose condition the next working generation is. The second says who
can be left to provide it. Chapter 6 begins from the seller: *“The owner
of labour-power is mortal”*, so subsistence *“must include the means
necessary for the labourer’s substitutes, i.e., his children.”* Chapter
10 gives the buyer’s horizon: *“Après moi le déluge!”*, and *“Capital is
reckless of the health or length of life of the labourer, unless under
compulsion from society.”* This paper takes the question those passages
pose, which class wants the next working generation reared and how much,
and answers it in a model where the workers are mortal cohorts and the
owners of capital are generations whose capital outlives them.

On Cobb–Douglas production the answer has a closed form, and the form
depends on two things: the horizon of each class, and the claims each
class holds on future output. The working cohort’s stake in the next
generation is its old-age claim. That claim is a share of the next
generation’s product, so the cohort’s preferred levy is the one that
maximises that product. It is independent of how the cohort discounts,
how much it values its retirement, how it values consumption while
young, and what share of the pension is paid from wages (Proposition 1).
Capital’s stake is its successors’ income. Its preferred levy is
therefore zero when a capitalist generation does not care about the
next, and rises with the weight it puts on them (Proposition 2). Which
class wants more is decided by one inequality in the generational
discount factor, the capitalist’s altruism, the capital share and the
elasticity of rearing (Proposition 3). Whenever capital’s generational
discount factor, weighted by its altruism, is at most one half, labour’s
selfish old-age claim out-demands capital. It needs no altruism toward
the children, and the sign does not move with the pension institution.
The timing of the return matters as much as its size. When the levy
builds a stock that rears the generation after next, the working cohort,
which will be dead by then, wants none of it, and capital wants it
exactly when it cares about its successors (Proposition 4). The same
horizons decide the form of the fund. For a given total, capital would
split it between cash and public input to maximise the size of the next
generation, while the working cohort, which also consumes out of the
cash, wants more cash (Proposition 5).

There the two preferred levies are, up to their common factor $1-\psi$,
exactly the two public-input shares of a one-agent economy with the same
technologies (Proposition 6). The pensioner’s share maximises
next-period output; capital’s is the share a planner discounting at
$\beta\lambda_C$ chooses. On that face, then, the division is set by
horizon and not by ownership. Off unit elasticity it is not. At a state
stationary under capital’s own policy, an interior levy of capital’s
must be its first-order levy (Proposition 7), and the ordering turns on
a threshold that now involves the stationary capital share (Proposition
8). A bound on capital’s horizon in terms of the elasticity and the
capital share alone guarantees that labour wants the larger levy,
whatever the rearing technology, and the bound is sharp (Proposition 9).
Capital’s own claims on profits are what drive a reversal. They enter
its problem through what it values, what it accumulates and the base on
which the levy is raised. A decider with capital’s horizon and none of
those claims never out-demands the pensioner where horizon alone favours
labour. The one-agent relabelling fails at every elasticity other than
one (Proposition 10).

**Contribution statement.** The class structure is Michl and Foley’s
(2004): capitalist dynasties beside life-cycle workers, which Sasaki
(2021) also embeds. In neither is the rearing of the workforce an
instrument. Here the capitalist’s horizon is a parameter, the workforce
is produced, and the levy that rears it is the object compared. The
pensioner’s stake in the number and productivity of the next generation
is the premise of the pension–fertility literature, where a planner
designs the child-related benefit to internalise the fiscal externality
children create under pay-as-you-go (Meier and Wrede 2005). Here that
stake is set against the stake of the owner being levied. In
representative-agent public-capital models the growth-maximising rate
exceeds the welfare-maximising one (surveyed in Misch, Gemmell and
Kneller 2013, p. 11). Proposition 6 shows that on unit elasticity the
class comparison is that comparison, relabelled, so nothing here
contradicts it in its own model. What that model does not deliver is an
answer to *which class* wants the levy. Off unit elasticity the
relabelling fails, and the gap between the two is exactly capital’s
claims on profits. That finite-lived decision makers under-fund forward
goods relative to an infinitely lived one is Kahn and Lim’s (2001)
finding for representative agents. What is claimed here is the
assignment of the two horizons to two classes, the closed-form threshold
that divides them, its invariance to the pension institution, the timing
reversal, the form of the fund, and, off unit elasticity, the preferred
levy, the horizon bound free of the rearing technology and the necessity
of capital’s claims on profits.

**Whose belief it displaces.** Two readers hold beliefs these results
cost them. The first reads the reproduction of the working class as
capital’s standing interest. On this reading, whatever the state spends
on rearing the next generation is spent in capital’s interest, and
labour’s support for it is solidarity. On the Cobb–Douglas face,
whenever $\beta\lambda_C(1+\alpha(1-\gamma))<1$, and so at every
$\beta\lambda_C\le1/2$, the class that wants the larger levy is labour,
and it wants it selfishly, through a claim on the product of the workers
it helps to rear. The pension institution, funded from wages or from
profits, makes no difference to that ordering. This is not a claim about
distribution. Rada (2012) finds that the pension institution aligns
retirees with capital under full funding and with labour under
pay-as-you-go over the division of income, and the rearing of the next
generation is not an object there. The two results are about different
margins, and the second margin is the one this paper adds. The second
reader, in public finance, places the pensioner’s stake in the next
generation in wage-financed pay-as-you-go. Here it survives when the
pension is a claim on profits, and it is compared with the interest of
the owner whose income is levied, not with a planner’s.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 sets out the
model. Section 3 solves the Cobb–Douglas economy. Section 4 leaves unit
elasticity. Section 5 says what the results do to the question, and
Section 6 places them. Proofs are in Appendix A.

# 2. The model

**Production and time.** Output is $Y_t=AK_t^{\alpha}N_t^{1-\alpha}$,
$0<\alpha<1$, where $N_t$ is the working generation. Capital depreciates
fully, and a period is a generation. The generational discount factor is
$\beta\in(0,1)$.

**Instruments and institutions.** Every instrument is a share of capital
income $\alpha Y_t$. A reproduction levy $\rho_t$ finances a public
input to rearing, $G_t=\rho_t\alpha Y_t$, and a cash transfer $c_t$ goes
to the working cohort. Two institutions carry the pension and are held
fixed: a pension levy $\psi\in[0,1)$ on capital income and a
pay-as-you-go share $\pi\in[0,1)$ of the wage bill. The working cohort’s
income is $W_t=[(1-\pi)(1-\alpha)+\alpha c_t]\,Y_t$. What remains of
capital income, $(1-\rho_t-\psi-c_t)\alpha Y_t$, the capitalist
generation divides between its consumption $C_t$ and next period’s
capital $K_{t+1}$.

**Rearing.** The next working generation is produced by the cohort’s
income and the public input,
$$N_{t+1}=B\,N_t\left(\frac{W_t}{N_t}\right)^{\gamma\theta}\left(\frac{G_t}{N_t}\right)^{\gamma(1-\theta)},\qquad 0<\gamma<1,\;0<\theta<1 .$$
The elasticity $\gamma$ says how strongly resources per child move the
next generation, and $\theta$ how the two inputs share that effect. The
map is a modelling device and is attributed to no one. It is log-linear,
and its value is that every result below is a closed form in its two
parameters. Two timings of the public input are compared. Under
*services* ($S$) the input spent at $t$ rears $N_{t+1}$. Under *durable*
provision ($D$) it builds a stock that rears $N_{t+2}$, entering
$N_{t+2}=BN_{t+1}(W_{t+1}/N_{t+1})^{\gamma\theta}(G_t/N_{t+1})^{\gamma(1-\theta)}$.[^1]

**Workers.** Cohort $t$ works and rears at $t$ and retires at $t+1$. Its
objective is
$$U^W_t=\mu\ln c^y_t+\nu\beta\ln c^o_{t+1}+\eta\ln N_{t+1}+\lambda_W\beta\,U^W_{t+1},\qquad c^y_t=\frac{W_t}{N_t},\quad c^o_{t+1}=\frac{[\psi\alpha+\pi(1-\alpha)]\,Y_{t+1}}{N_t},$$
with $\mu,\eta,\lambda_W\ge0$ and $\nu>0$. The weight $\nu$ is the
cohort’s stake in its retirement, $\eta$ a direct concern for the size
of the next generation, and $\lambda_W$ altruism toward it. The pension
is a share of the next generation’s product. It is divided among the
retiring cohort, whose size $N_t$ was fixed when it was itself reared.

**Capitalists.** Generation $t$ of capitalists has the objective
$$U^C_t=(1-\omega)\ln C_t+\omega\ln K_{t+1}+\lambda_C\beta\,U^C_{t+1},\qquad C_t+K_{t+1}=(1-\rho_t-\psi-c_t)\,\alpha Y_t ,$$
with $\omega\in(0,1)$ and $\lambda_C\in[0,1]$. A capitalist generation
values its own consumption and the capital it leaves. The weight
$\lambda_C$ is its altruism toward the generation that inherits the
capital: $\lambda_C=1$ is the dynasty, and $\lambda_C=0$ a generation
that cares only about itself. Write $L=\beta\lambda_C$ for capital’s
effective horizon. Under logarithmic objectives the capitalist invests a
constant share $i$ of what it retains, so
$K_{t+1}=i\,(1-\rho_t-\psi-c_t)\,\alpha Y_t$.

**Two modelling choices.** The levy is raised on capital income, so it
costs the working cohort nothing today, and the pension is divided by
the retiring cohort’s own size. Together they make the cohort’s stake in
the levy exactly its stake in the next generation’s output, and they are
the model’s statement of the old-age claim: a claim on the product of
the workers the cohort helps to rear. The class structure, with
capitalist dynasties beside life-cycle workers, follows Michl and Foley
(2004).

**What is compared.** In logarithms every transition is linear in the
state, and the instruments enter through terms that do not involve the
state, so the economy is a linear-state game (Dockner et al. 2000,
§7.2). In such a game the value of any constant instrument path is
linear in the log-state with coefficients that do not depend on the
state. Each result compares *preferred instruments*. A class’s preferred
levy is the constant levy that maximises its objective when it sets that
instrument alone, with the other instruments held at their values and
the law of motion taken as it is. Because the objective is linear in the
log-state, the maximiser does not depend on the state. Unless stated
otherwise the levy is compared at the provision corner $c=0$, where the
whole fund is public input.[^2]

# 3. Who wants the next generation reared: the Cobb–Douglas economy

The first two results give each class’s preferred levy under services
timing, at $c=0$, for a cohort with no altruism toward its successors
and no direct concern for their number ($\lambda_W=\eta=0$).

**Proposition 1 (the working cohort’s levy).** *Under services timing,
at $c=0$ and $\lambda_W=\eta=0$, the working cohort’s preferred levy is*
$$\rho_Y=(1-\psi)\,\frac{\gamma(1-\alpha)(1-\theta)}{\alpha+\gamma(1-\alpha)(1-\theta)} .$$
*It maximises the next generation’s output $Y_{t+1}$ over $\rho$ and
does not depend on $\beta$, $\nu$, $\mu$, $\omega$ or $\pi$. At $\nu=0$
the cohort is indifferent to the levy. A direct concern for the next
generation’s size raises the preferred levy:
$\partial\rho_Y/\partial\eta>0$ at $\lambda_W=0$.*

The cohort’s retirement claim is its whole stake in the levy. It wants
the levy that makes the next generation’s product largest, because that
product is what it will be paid from. How patient it is, how much it
values old age relative to youth, and what share of the pension is paid
from wages change the weight or the level of that stake, not its
maximiser. A concern for the next generation’s numbers only adds to it.

**Proposition 2 (capital’s levy).** *Under services timing and at $c=0$,
capital’s preferred levy is*
$$\rho_C=(1-\psi)\,\frac{\beta\lambda_C\,\gamma(1-\alpha)(1-\theta)}{1-\beta\lambda_C+\beta\lambda_C\,\gamma(1-\theta+\alpha\theta)} .$$
*It does not depend on $\omega$, it is zero at $\lambda_C=0$, and it is
strictly increasing in $\lambda_C$.*

Capital pays for the levy out of its own income today and gains from it
only through its successors, who inherit the capital and employ the
generation it rears. A capitalist generation that does not care about
its successors wants no levy at all. This is the sense in which capital
can leave the reproduction of the working class to others. It is a
statement about capital’s horizon, and it follows from the model’s
primitives rather than being assumed.

**Proposition 3 (which class wants more).** *Under services timing, at
$c=0$ and $\lambda_W=\eta=0$,*
$$\rho_Y-\rho_C=(1-\psi)\,\frac{\gamma(1-\alpha)(1-\theta)\,\bigl[1-\beta\lambda_C\,(1+\alpha(1-\gamma))\bigr]}{\bigl[\alpha+\gamma(1-\alpha)(1-\theta)\bigr]\bigl[1-\beta\lambda_C+\beta\lambda_C\gamma(1-\theta+\alpha\theta)\bigr]} ,$$
*so labour’s preferred levy exceeds capital’s if and only if
$\beta\lambda_C(1+\alpha(1-\gamma))<1$. The sign is the same at every
pension level $\psi$ and every pay-as-you-go share $\pi$. Both signs
occur on the parameter space.*

**Corollary (generational discounting).** *If $\beta\lambda_C\le1/2$,
labour’s preferred levy exceeds capital’s at every
$(\alpha,\gamma,\theta)$. The bound is sharp: for every
$\beta\lambda_C>1/2$ capital’s exceeds labour’s at some
$(\alpha,\gamma)\in(0,1)^2$.*

The threshold has an economic reading. The cohort’s claim reaches
exactly one generation ahead, and it is paid from that generation’s
whole product. Capital’s reaches as far as its altruism carries it,
discounted once for every generation, and it is paid from the capital
income of each. With a thirty-year generation, $\beta\le1/2$ whenever
the annual discount factor is at most $2^{-1/30}\approx0.977$, and
altruism below one lowers $\beta\lambda_C$ further; there the pensioner
wants the larger levy. Capital wants the larger levy only when its
altruism and its patience across generations together exceed
$1/(1+\alpha(1-\gamma))$, a bound that is lower the larger the capital
share and the smaller the elasticity of rearing. The pension institution
does not enter the sign. A pension paid from profits and one paid from
wages scale both levies by the same factor, and neither moves the
comparison. Nor does the pension’s level: both levies carry the factor
$1-\psi$, so the ranking holds at every $\psi\in[0,1)$, including the
high levels toward which the retired cohort would push it (Proposition
5).

**Proposition 4 (timing).** *Under durable provision, with
$\lambda_W=0$, the working cohort’s preferred levy is zero for every
$\mu$, $\nu>0$ and $\eta$. Capital’s preferred levy is positive if and
only if $\lambda_C>0$, it is strictly increasing in $\lambda_C$, and it
equals*
$$\rho^D_C=(1-\psi)\,\frac{\beta^2\lambda_C^2\,\gamma(1-\alpha)(1-\theta)}{1-\beta\lambda_C+\beta\lambda_C\gamma(1-\theta+\alpha\theta)}=\beta\lambda_C\,\rho_C .$$

The ordering reverses with the timing of the return. A stock that rears
the generation after next pays nothing to a cohort whose pension is
drawn from the next generation. The cohort bears its cost through a
smaller capital stock and gains nothing. Capital gains through its
successors’ successors, because its capital outlives it. For the part of
reproduction whose return arrives after the working cohort has retired,
capital’s interest dominates at every horizon at which it cares about
its successors at all. Under durable provision capital’s levy is its
services levy discounted once more.

**Proposition 5 (the form of the fund).** *Under services timing with
$\psi=0$, fix the total fund $\tau=\rho+c$. For $\lambda_C\in(0,1]$
capital’s objective is strictly concave in the cash share
$c\in[0,\tau)$, so its preferred split is unique. Where it is interior
it is*
$$c_C=\frac{\theta\alpha\tau-(1-\theta)(1-\pi)(1-\alpha)}{\alpha},$$
*and otherwise it is the corner $c=0$. It does not depend on
$\lambda_C$. At $\mu=0$ it coincides with the working cohort’s. For
$\mu>0$ the cohort’s objective is concave in $c$, and its marginal value
of cash at $c_C$ is
$\alpha\mu/\bigl(\theta[(1-\alpha)(1-\pi)+\alpha\tau]\bigr)>0$, so the
cohort wants more cash than capital wherever capital’s split is
interior. At $\lambda_C=0$ capital is indifferent across splits, and the
split above is the limit of its preferred split as $\lambda_C\to0^+$. A
retired cohort’s ($\lambda_W=0$) objective is strictly increasing in the
pension levy $\psi$, so it wants $\psi$ as high as the institution
allows.*

For a given fund, capital’s split is the one that rears the largest next
generation: it equates the marginal effect of cash on the next
generation with that of the public input. The working cohort agrees
about rearing, but it also consumes out of its income, so it wants more
of the fund as cash. The division of the fund between the family and the
state is thus contested over the cohort’s own consumption, not over the
next generation. About the next generation the two classes agree.

**Proposition 6 (the one-agent identity).** *On Cobb–Douglas production,
$\rho_Y$ and $\rho_C$ are, up to their common factor $1-\psi$, exactly
the public-input shares of a one-agent economy with the same production
and rearing technologies: $\rho_Y$ is the share that maximises
next-period output, and $\rho_C$ is the share chosen by a planner who
discounts at $\beta\lambda_C$.*

Proposition 6 says what the class comparison is on Cobb–Douglas
production. The division between labour and capital is the
representative-agent wedge between a growth-maximising and a
welfare-maximising public-input share, with each horizon assigned to a
class. On this face the division is set by horizon, not by ownership.
Section 4 shows that this identity is special to unit elasticity, and
says what replaces it.

# 4. Off unit elasticity

Let output per worker be
$y(k)=A[\alpha k^{\varepsilon}+1-\alpha]^{1/\varepsilon}$ with $k=K/N$,
elasticity of substitution $\sigma>0$ and
$\varepsilon=(\sigma-1)/\sigma$. The capital share is
$s_K(k)=\alpha k^{\varepsilon}/(\alpha k^{\varepsilon}+1-\alpha)$ and
the wage share $s_L=1-s_K$. The levy $r$ is a share of capital income,
services timing is kept, and the cash transfer is at its corner:
$$\frac{N'}{N}=B\,(s_Ly)^{\gamma\theta}(r\,s_Ky)^{\gamma(1-\theta)},\qquad \frac{K'}{N}=i\,(1-r)\,s_Ky,\qquad k'=\frac{K'}{N'} .$$
The pension is a fixed share $\bar s$ of the next generation’s output,
financed by a flat contribution on both factor incomes. The contribution
rescales $i$ and $B$ and leaves every sign below unchanged. A capitalist
generation with horizon $L=\beta\lambda_C$ has value $\ln N/(1-L)+v(k)$,
where
$$v(k)=\max_r\;\ln(1-r)+\ln s_K(k)+\ln y(k)+\frac{L}{1-L}\ln\frac{N'}{N}+L\,v(k') .$$
The working cohort, with $\lambda_W=\eta=0$, maximises
$\ln(N'/N)+\ln y(k')$, the part of its objective that the levy moves.
The comparison is made at a state $k^*$ that is stationary under
capital’s own policy, with stationary capital share $s=s_K(k^*)$. Write
$p=\gamma(1-\theta)$ and
$$G=\gamma\bigl[s(1-\theta\varepsilon)+(1-\theta)\varepsilon(1-s)\bigr],\qquad M=s+\varepsilon(1-s)-G,\qquad T=1+\frac{s}{\sigma}-G .$$
At $\sigma=1$ the share is $s=\alpha$, $G=\gamma\alpha$ and
$T=1+\alpha(1-\gamma)$, the threshold of Proposition 3.

**Proposition 7 (capital’s preferred levy).** *Let $\lambda_C>0$ and let
$k^*$ be stationary under capital’s own policy, with $1-L+LG>0$ and
$1-LM>0$, and value the continuation at its marginal value at $k^*$ in
logarithms. Then, under that valuation, capital’s objective in the levy
is exactly*
$$J(r)=a\ln(1-r)+b\ln r+\text{const},\qquad a=\frac{1-L+LG}{(1-L)(1-LM)},\qquad b=\frac{pL(1-s)(1-\varepsilon)}{(1-L)(1-LM)} ,$$
*with $a,b>0$. It is strictly concave on $(0,1)$ and tends to $-\infty$
at both ends, so its first-order root $r_C$, with
$r_C/(1-r_C)=b/a=pL(1-s)(1-\varepsilon)/(1-L+LG)$, is its unique
maximiser, and an interior levy of capital’s that keeps the state at
$k^*$, where capital’s value is differentiable, equals $r_C$. Call $r_C$
capital’s stationary preferred levy.*

The levy enters the next generation only through $p\ln r$ and the
capital stock only through $\ln(1-r)-p\ln r$. Once the continuation is
valued at the stationary state, capital faces a two-term logarithmic
trade-off between capital left and workers reared. That valuation is the
one the stationary state itself imposes: an interior levy that keeps the
state at $k^*$, where capital’s value is differentiable, satisfies the
same first-order condition, so it is $r_C$. The trade-off has one
interior optimum and no corner competitor, so capital’s stationary
preferred levy is well defined at every elasticity where the two
hypotheses hold. What is not established is that it is capital’s optimum
in its full programme: the valuation fixes the slope of the continuation
at $k^*$, not its curvature away from it. The hypotheses restrict the
horizon. They hold whenever $\sigma\ge1$ and $L\le1/2$, and whenever
$\sigma=1/2$ and $L\le1/3$.

**Proposition 8 (the ordering off unit elasticity).** *Let
$\lambda_C>0$. At $k^*$ stationary under capital’s own policy, with
$1-L+LG>0$ and $1-LM>0$, the working cohort’s objective is single-peaked
in $r$, its derivative at $r_C$ has the sign of $p(1-s)/s-r_C/(1-r_C)$,
and*
$$\frac{p(1-s)}{s}-\frac{r_C}{1-r_C}=\frac{p(1-s)\,(1-LT)}{s\,(1-L+LG)} .$$
*So labour’s preferred levy exceeds capital’s stationary preferred levy
$r_C$ if and only if $LT<1$. In particular, labour’s exceeds capital’s
at every $s,\gamma,\theta\in(0,1)$ (i) if $\sigma\ge1$ and $L\le1/2$,
and (ii) if $\sigma=1/2$ and $L\le1/3$. (iii) At $\sigma=1/2$ and
$L=\gamma=\theta=1/2$, both hypotheses hold at every $s$. There $r_C$
exceeds labour’s preferred levy exactly when $s>3/4$.*

Off unit elasticity the stationary capital share enters the threshold,
and a higher share raises it at every elasticity, since
$\partial T/\partial s=(1-\gamma)/\sigma>0$. The effect is larger the
stronger the complementarity. With strong complements ($\sigma=1/2$) and
a horizon of one half, a stationary share above three quarters makes the
owner the class that wants the larger levy. With substitutes, a horizon
of one half never suffices. What decides the division is still the
horizon, joined now by the share of output that the owner’s claims
command.

**Proposition 9 (a horizon bound free of the rearing technology).**
*$LT\le1$ at every $(\gamma,\theta)\in[0,1]^2$ if and only if
$L\le\min\bigl(\sigma,\;\sigma/(\sigma+s)\bigr)$, and $LT<1$ there when
the inequality is strict. Wherever the hypotheses of Proposition 8 hold,
labour’s preferred levy is then at least $r_C$, and strictly above it
under the strict inequality. Neither branch of the bound can be dropped.
If $L(\sigma+s)>\sigma$, $r_C$ exceeds labour’s preferred levy at some
$\gamma$ near zero with both hypotheses strict. If
$\sigma<L<\sigma/(1-s)$, it does so near $(\gamma,\theta)=(1,0)$. At
$\sigma=1$ the bound reads $\beta\lambda_C(1+\alpha)\le1$, the condition
under which Proposition 3’s threshold holds for every $\gamma$.*

The bound involves only capital’s horizon, the elasticity of
substitution and the capital share. It gives the patience capital must
exceed before any rearing technology can make it the class that wants
the next generation reared more. The share branch binds when
$\sigma+s>1$, and the elasticity branch when $\sigma+s<1$, that is, with
strong complements and a small capital share.

**Proposition 10 (capital’s claims on profits are necessary).**
*Capital’s claims on profits move its problem through three channels:
its valuation ($\ln s_K$ in its flow), its accumulation (it invests out
of $s_Ky$) and the levy base (the levy is a share of capital income).
Consider a decider with capital’s horizon $L$ and none of the three,
compared at a state stationary under its own policy. (i) If $\sigma\le1$
and $L(1+s(1-\gamma))<1$, it never wants a larger levy than the working
cohort. (ii) At every $\sigma$, if $L\le1/2$, it never wants a larger
levy than the working cohort. (iii) Capital’s threshold differs from
that of the one-agent planner of Proposition 6, for whom the factor
shares move nothing, by*
$$T_{\text{capital}}-T_{\text{planner}}=\frac{1-\sigma}{\sigma}\,\bigl[s(1-\gamma\theta)+p(1-s)\bigr],$$
*whose bracket is positive. So the class comparison is a relabelled
one-agent comparison only at $\sigma=1$.*

Proposition 10 locates the reversal of Proposition 8. With substitution
at most unit, horizon alone favours labour wherever
$L(1+s(1-\gamma))<1$, and there only capital’s own claims on profits can
make the owner the class that wants the larger levy. At $\sigma<1$ those
claims raise capital’s threshold by a positive amount. At $\sigma=1$
they are invisible, which is why Proposition 6 holds there and only
there. “Horizon, not ownership” is the Cobb–Douglas statement. Off unit
elasticity the statement is this: where horizon alone favours labour and
substitution is at most unit, only capital’s ownership can reverse the
ordering.

# 5. What the results do to the question

The question posed by chapter 23 was whose condition the next working
generation is, and who can be left to provide it. On Cobb–Douglas
production the answer divides by the timing of the return. For
reproduction whose return arrives within the working cohort’s lifetime,
labour wants it financed through its claim on the next generation’s
product, and it wants it without any altruism toward the children it
rears. Whenever $\beta\lambda_C\le1/2$ it wants more than capital does.
That ordering survives at every elasticity of substitution of at least
one, and it can fail with strong complements: at an elasticity of one
half, with $\beta\lambda_C=\gamma=\theta=1/2$, it reverses once the
stationary capital share exceeds three quarters (Proposition 8). For
reproduction whose return arrives after the cohort has died, a cohort
without altruism toward its successors wants none of it, and capital
wants it exactly when it cares about its successors, and more the more
it cares. A capitalist generation that does not care about the next
wants no levy at all. What the owner leaves to others is the part of
reproduction whose return it will not see.

Two things do not decide the division. The pension institution does not:
a claim on wages and a claim on profits scale both levies alike. Nor, on
Cobb–Douglas, does ownership as such: there the classes differ only by
horizon, exactly as a growth-maximising and a welfare-maximising share
differ in a one-agent economy. Ownership returns off unit elasticity,
and in a precise place. Where horizon alone favours labour and
substitution is at most unit, capital’s claims on profits are the only
channel through which the owner can come to want more of the next
generation than the pensioner does. Those claims are its valuation of
the capital share, its accumulation out of it, and the base on which the
levy is raised.

# 6. Placement, by one primitive each

**The class structure.** Capitalist dynasties beside life-cycle workers
are Michl and Foley’s (2004), where the labour force accommodates
accumulation at a given wage and the rearing of the workforce is not an
instrument. Sasaki (2021) embeds the same pair. Here the capitalist
horizon is a parameter and the reproduction levy is the object.

**The pensioner’s stake.** In the pension–fertility literature the
child-related benefit is designed by a planner to internalise the fiscal
externality children create under pay-as-you-go (Meier and Wrede 2005).
That stake is the literature’s premise, and Proposition 1 inherits it.
What is added is its comparison with the owner being levied, and its
survival when the pension is a claim on profits.

**The pension institution and class alignment.** Rada (2012) finds that
the pension institution aligns retirees with capital under full funding
and with labour under pay-as-you-go over the division of income.
Proposition 3 is about a different margin, the rearing of the next
generation, and on that margin the institution does not move the
ordering.

**Forward goods and horizons.** Finite-lived decision makers under-fund
forward goods relative to an infinitely lived one in Kahn and Lim
(2001), for representative agents. Proposition 4 assigns the two
horizons to two classes, and finds that the finite-lived class wants
none of a good whose return falls after its lifetime.

**Public-input arithmetic.** The wedge between growth-maximising and
welfare-maximising public-capital shares is the representative-agent
result (surveyed in Misch, Gemmell and Kneller 2013, p. 11). Proposition
6 shows the class comparison is that wedge on unit elasticity, and
Proposition 10 measures the distance from it off unit elasticity.

**Capital taxes that finance public investment.** With dynastic
capitalists beside life-cycle workers, a capital tax that finances
productive public investment is Pareto-improving at low rates, and
workers are reported to prefer higher rates than capitalists (Mattauch,
Klenert, Stiglitz and Edenhofer 2018, p. 3). There the public input
raises productivity, and the ordering is reported as a property of the
class pair. Here the input rears the workforce, and the ordering is
conditional: labour wants the larger levy exactly when the inequality of
the introduction holds, on Cobb–Douglas production exactly when
$\beta\lambda_C(1+\alpha(1-\gamma))<1$, so a capitalist generation
patient and altruistic enough wants more than the pensioner does.

**The elasticity of substitution.** In Galor and Moav (2006) a tax on
intergenerational transfers funds public schooling, which enters
production, and rich and poor agree on its rate. Their footnote 42
attributes the agreement to the linear tax and unit elasticity, and
states that above unit elasticity the poor would prefer higher taxes and
below it the rich. Here the levy funds rearing, and the classes disagree
already at unit elasticity (Proposition 3). Below unit elasticity the
elasticity does not sign the ranking by itself: at $\sigma=1/2$ labour’s
preferred levy exceeds capital’s whenever $L\le1/3$, and capital’s
stationary preferred levy exceeds labour’s once the stationary capital
share passes three quarters at $L=\gamma=\theta=1/2$ (Proposition 8).

# Appendix A. Proofs

Throughout, $L=\beta\lambda_C$, and logarithms of the state are written
$\kappa=\ln K$, $n=\ln N$. Under services timing,
$\ln N_{t+1}=\text{const}+\gamma\alpha\,\kappa_t+(1-\gamma\alpha)\,n_t+\gamma\theta\ln w_t+\gamma(1-\theta)\ln\rho_t$,
where $w_t=(1-\pi)(1-\alpha)+\alpha c_t$. Also
$\ln K_{t+1}=\text{const}+\ln(1-\rho_t-\psi-c_t)+\alpha\kappa_t+(1-\alpha)n_t$.

## A.1 Proposition 1.

At $\lambda_W=\eta=0$ the levy enters the cohort’s objective only
through $\nu\beta\ln c^o_{t+1}$. With $c=0$, $w$ does not depend on
$\rho$, and $\ln c^o_{t+1}=\text{const}+\ln Y_{t+1}$. Here
$\ln Y_{t+1}=\text{const}+\alpha\ln(1-\rho-\psi)+(1-\alpha)\gamma(1-\theta)\ln\rho$
in $\rho$, so the cohort’s objective in $\rho$ is $\nu\beta$ times the
log of next-generation output. The first-order condition
$\alpha/(1-\rho-\psi)=(1-\alpha)\gamma(1-\theta)/\rho$ gives $\rho_Y$,
which is the unique maximiser of a strictly concave function and
contains none of $\beta,\nu,\mu,\omega,\pi$. At $\nu=0$ the derivative
is identically zero. With $\eta>0$ the objective gains
$\eta\gamma(1-\theta)\ln\rho$, which adds to the coefficient on
$\ln\rho$. Differentiating the resulting closed form gives
$\partial\rho_Y/\partial\eta$ equal to a positive constant times
$\alpha\beta\gamma\nu(1-\theta)$ over a square, which is positive.

## A.2 Proposition 2.

Capital’s value under a constant instrument path is
$a\kappa+bn+\text{const}$. Both $C_t$ and $K_{t+1}$ are proportional to
$(1-\rho-\psi-c)\alpha Y_t$, so the flow contributes
$\ln(1-\rho-\psi-c)+\alpha\kappa+(1-\alpha)n$. Matching coefficients,
$$a=\alpha+L(a\alpha+b\gamma\alpha),\qquad b=(1-\alpha)+L\bigl(a(1-\alpha)+b(1-\gamma\alpha)\bigr).$$
Adding the two gives $a+b=1/(1-L)$, and the second gives
$b/(1+La)=(1-\alpha)/(1-L+L\gamma\alpha)$. At $c=0$ capital’s objective
in $\rho$ is $(1+La)\ln(1-\rho-\psi)+Lb\gamma(1-\theta)\ln\rho$. Its
first-order condition gives odds
$\rho_C/(1-\psi-\rho_C)=L\gamma(1-\alpha)(1-\theta)/(1-L+L\gamma\alpha)$,
which is the stated closed form. It contains no $\omega$, and the
objective is strictly concave. At $\lambda_C=0$ the derivative is
$-1/(1-\rho-\psi)<0$ everywhere, so the preferred levy is zero. The
derivative in $\lambda_C$ is $\beta\gamma(1-\alpha)(1-\theta)(1-\psi)$
over the square of the denominator, which is positive because the
denominator $1-L+L\gamma(1-\theta+\alpha\theta)$ is positive for
$L\in[0,1)$.

## A.3 Proposition 3 and its Corollary.

The odds of the two levies over $1-\psi-\rho$ are
$x_Y=\gamma(1-\alpha)(1-\theta)/\alpha$ and
$x_C=L\gamma(1-\alpha)(1-\theta)/(1-L+L\gamma\alpha)$. Hence $x_Y>x_C$
if and only if $1-L+L\gamma\alpha>L\alpha$, that is
$L(1+\alpha(1-\gamma))<1$. The displayed difference is the exact
rational-function identity obtained by subtracting the two closed forms.
Both levies carry the factor $1-\psi$ and neither contains $\pi$, which
gives the invariance. Both signs occur: take $\lambda_C=1$ and $\beta$
close to one, and the threshold fails because $1+\alpha(1-\gamma)>1$.
For the corollary, $1+\alpha(1-\gamma)<2$ on the box, so $L\le1/2$ gives
$L(1+\alpha(1-\gamma))<1$. For sharpness, let $L>1/2$ and put
$m=1/(2L)\in(0,1)$, $\gamma=(1-m)/2$ and $\alpha=2m/(1+m)$. Both lie in
$(0,1)$, $\alpha(1-\gamma)=m$, and $L(1+\alpha(1-\gamma))=L+1/2>1$.

## A.4 Proposition 4.

Under durable provision $\rho_t$ rears $N_{t+2}$. The cohort’s objective
at $\lambda_W=0$ reads $Y_{t+1}$, $N_{t+1}$ and $c^y_t$, none of which
depends on $\rho_t$ except through $K_{t+1}$. Its derivative in $\rho$
is therefore $-\nu\beta\alpha/(1-\rho-\psi)<0$ for every $\mu$ and
$\eta$, and the preferred levy is zero. For capital the coefficient
system of A.2 gains a third equation for the public stock,
$g=Lb\gamma(1-\theta)$, and the transition coefficients become
$(\gamma\theta\alpha,\;1-\gamma+\gamma\theta(1-\alpha),\;\gamma(1-\theta))$
on $(\kappa,n,\ln G)$. Capital’s objective in $\rho$ is
$(1+La)\ln(1-\rho-\psi)+Lg\ln\rho$, so its odds over $1-\psi-\rho$ are
$Lg/(1+La)$. Solving the system gives the stated closed form, with the
factor $1-\psi$ at every $\psi$, whose numerator vanishes exactly at
$\lambda_C=0$. Its denominator is that of Proposition 2, which gives
$\rho^D_C=L\rho_C$. Its derivative in $L$ is
$(1-\psi)\gamma(1-\alpha)(1-\theta)\,L\,(2-L+L\gamma(1-\theta+\alpha\theta))$
over the square of that denominator, which is positive for $L\in(0,1)$.

## A.5 Proposition 5.

With $\psi=0$ and $\rho=\tau-c$, capital’s objective in $c$ is
$(1+La)\ln(1-\tau)+Lb\,\gamma[\theta\ln w+(1-\theta)\ln(\tau-c)]+\text{const}$.
For $\lambda_C>0$, $Lb>0$ (A.2), so capital maximises
$\theta\ln w+(1-\theta)\ln(\tau-c)$. The condition
$\theta\alpha/w=(1-\theta)/(\tau-c)$ gives $c_C$, which contains no
$\lambda_C$. At $\lambda_C=0$ the objective does not depend on $c$, and
the limit of $c_C$ as $\lambda_C\to0^+$ is $c_C$ itself. The cohort’s
objective in $c$ is $\kappa_w\ln w+\kappa_\rho\ln(\tau-c)+\text{const}$,
with nonnegative coefficients, so it is concave. At $\mu=0$ the ratio
$\kappa_w/\kappa_\rho$ equals $\theta/(1-\theta)$, and the two splits
coincide. For $\mu>0$ its derivative at $c_C$ equals $\alpha\mu/w(c_C)$,
and $w(c_C)=\theta[(1-\alpha)(1-\pi)+\alpha\tau]$, which gives the
stated value. Since $(1-\alpha)(1-\pi)+\alpha\tau>0$ the derivative is
positive. At $\lambda_C=0$ every split is preferred by capital, so the
ordering is stated for the selection that is continuous in $\lambda_C$.
For the retired,
$\partial J_O/\partial\psi=\alpha\nu/(\alpha\psi+\pi(1-\alpha))>0$.

## A.6 Proposition 6.

Take a one-agent economy with the production function of Section 2 and
the rearing map
$N_{t+1}=BN_t^{1-\gamma}W_t^{\gamma\theta}G_t^{\gamma(1-\theta)}$, in
which $W_t$ is a fixed share of output and the public input
$G_t=x_t\alpha Y_t$ is financed out of the return to capital. The agent
consumes and invests fixed shares of the return net of the public input,
so its flow is $\ln(1-x_t)+\ln Y_t$ and
$\ln K_{t+1}=\ln(1-x_t)+\ln Y_t$, each up to a constant. These are
capital’s flow and accumulation in A.2 at $\psi=0$, and the rearing map
is the one in the preamble to this appendix. The share that maximises
next-period log output,
$\alpha\ln(1-x)+(1-\alpha)\gamma(1-\theta)\ln x$, is $\rho_Y/(1-\psi)$
(A.1). With discount factor $L$ the agent’s value solves the coefficient
system of A.2, so its preferred share is $\rho_C/(1-\psi)$. Both
identifications are exact rational-function identities in
$(\alpha,\gamma,\theta,L)$.

## A.7 Proposition 7.

At a stationary point of capital’s policy, the envelope condition in
$u=\ln k$ gives the marginal value $V^*=(E+LG/(1-L))/(1-LM)$ with
$E=s+\varepsilon(1-s)$. It uses
$\mathrm d\ln s_K/\mathrm du=\varepsilon(1-s)$,
$\mathrm d\ln y/\mathrm du=s$, and $\mathrm d\ln(N'/N)/\mathrm du=G$,
and it is finite under $1-LM>0$. In $r$ the next generation moves only
through $p\ln r$, and $\ln k'=\ln(1-r)-p\ln r+\text{terms free of }r$.
Valuing the continuation at $V^*$, capital’s objective is
$(1+LV^*)\ln(1-r)+pL\bigl(\tfrac1{1-L}-V^*\bigr)\ln r+\text{const}$.
Substituting $V^*$ gives $1+LV^*=(1-L+LG)/((1-L)(1-LM))=a$. The same
substitution, with $1-E=(1-s)(1-\varepsilon)$, gives
$pL(\tfrac1{1-L}-V^*)=pL(1-s)(1-\varepsilon)/((1-L)(1-LM))=b$. Under the
two hypotheses $a>0$. Also $b>0$, because $p>0$, $s<1$ and
$\varepsilon<1$. A function $a\ln(1-r)+b\ln r$ with $a,b>0$ is strictly
concave on $(0,1)$ and tends to $-\infty$ at both ends, so its
stationary point $r/(1-r)=b/a$ is the unique maximiser. The valuation is
the one the stationary state imposes. If capital’s levy $r^*$ at $k^*$
is interior and keeps the state at $k^*$, and capital’s value is
differentiable at $k^*$, the envelope theorem makes $v'(u^*)$ the $V^*$
above, so the first-order condition of capital’s own problem at $r^*$ is
$J'(r^*)=0$, and $r^*=r_C$. The hypotheses hold in the two stated cases.
For $\sigma\ge1$, $\varepsilon\in[0,1)$ gives $G\ge0$ and
$M\le s+\varepsilon(1-s)<1$, so $1-L+LG\ge1-L>0$ and $1-LM>0$. For
$\sigma=1/2$, $\varepsilon=-1$ gives
$G=\gamma[s(1+\theta)-(1-\theta)(1-s)]>-1$ and $M=2s-1-G<s$, so
$L\le1/3$ gives $1-L+LG>1-2L>0$ and $1-LM>0$.

## A.8 Proposition 8.

In $r$, the cohort’s objective moves through $p\ln r$ in $\ln(N'/N)$ and
through $\ln k'=\ln(1-r)-p\ln r+\text{const}$ in $\ln y(k')$, so its
derivative is $p(1-s')/r-s'/(1-r)$, with $s'=s_K(k')$. Its first-order
condition is therefore $\ln x+\ln(s'/(1-s'))=\ln p$, with $x=r/(1-r)$.
The left side has derivative
$(1-\varepsilon p)/r+(1-\varepsilon)/(1-r)>0$ in $r$, so the condition
has one root and the objective is single-peaked. At $r_C$ the next state
is $k^*$, so $s'=s$, and the derivative there is
$(1+x_C)(s/x_C)\bigl(p(1-s)/s-x_C\bigr)$, where $x_C=r_C/(1-r_C)$. The
displayed identity follows from $x_C=pL(1-s)(1-\varepsilon)/(1-L+LG)$
and $s(1-\varepsilon)=s/\sigma$. The sign of $r_Y-r_C$ is that of $1-LT$
because $s(1-L+LG)>0$. For (i), $\varepsilon\in[0,1)$ gives $G\ge0$ and
$s/\sigma\le s$, so $T\le1+s<2$, and $L\le1/2$ gives $LT<1$. For (ii),
$\varepsilon=-1$ gives $G>-(1-s)$, so $T=1+2s-G<2+s<3$, and $L\le1/3$
gives $LT<1$. In both cases the hypotheses hold by A.7. For (iii), at
$\varepsilon=-1$ and $L=\gamma=\theta=1/2$ one has $G=s-1/4$, $M=s-3/4$
and $T=5/4+s$. So $1-L+LG=3/8+s/2>0$, $1-LM=1-(s-3/4)/2>0$, and $LT>1$
if and only if $s>3/4$.

## A.9 Proposition 9.

For fixed $\theta$, $T$ is affine in $\gamma$, and for fixed $\gamma$ it
is affine in $\theta$. Its maximum over $[0,1]^2$ is therefore attained
at a corner. The corner values are $T(0,\theta)=1+s/\sigma$,
$T(1,0)=1/\sigma$ and $T(1,1)=1$. Hence
$\sup T=\max(1+s/\sigma,1/\sigma)$, and $L\sup T\le1$ if and only if
$L\le\sigma/(\sigma+s)$ and $L\le\sigma$. For sharpness, at $\gamma=0$
one has $\sigma T=\sigma+s$, $\sigma(1-L+LG)=\sigma(1-L)>0$ and
$\sigma(1-LM)=\sigma(1-L)+L(1-s)>0$. So $L(\sigma+s)>\sigma$ gives
$LT>1$ with both hypotheses strict, and by continuity also at some
$\gamma>0$. At $(\gamma,\theta)=(1,0)$, $\sigma T=1$,
$\sigma(1-LM)=\sigma$ and $\sigma(1-L+LG)=\sigma-L(1-s)$. So
$\sigma<L<\sigma/(1-s)$ gives $LT>1$ with both hypotheses strict, and
hence nearby in the open box. A reversal with both hypotheses strict
also exists in the band that the two branches leave open, where
$L>\sigma$ and $L(\sigma+s)\le\sigma$ but $L\ge\sigma/(1-s)$. At the
rational point $(\sigma,s,L,\gamma,\theta)=(1/4,1/4,9/20,1/2,1/3)$,
which lies in that band, $T=5/2$, $LT=9/8>1$, $1-L+LG=13/40>0$ and
$1-LM=67/40>0$. So the bound cannot be relaxed on that part of the
boundary either. At $\sigma=1$, $\min(1,1/(1+s))=1/(1+\alpha)$.

## A.10 Proposition 10.

Attach a marker to each channel through which a factor share enters the
decider’s problem: $f$ on valuation, $a$ on accumulation, $c$ on the
levy base, and $w$ on labour’s wage base in rearing. The marked
primitives are the flow $\ln(1-r)+f\ln s_K+\ln y$, the rearing law
$\ln(N'/N)=\ln B+\gamma\theta(w\ln s_L+\ln y)+\gamma(1-\theta)(\ln r+c\ln s_K+\ln y)$,
and the accumulation law
$\ln k'=\ln i+\ln(1-r)+a\ln s_K+\ln y-\ln(N'/N)$; at $f=a=c=w=1$ they
are capital’s problem of Section 4, and at $f=a=c=w=0$ no factor share
enters any of them. Repeating the derivation of A.7 and A.8 with the
markers in the primitives gives the same sign identity with denominator
$D=1-L+LG+L(1-L)(f-a)\varepsilon(1-s)$ and threshold
$$T=1+s(1-\gamma)+e\bigl[f(1-L)+a\bigl(s-(1-L)\bigr)+c\,p(1-s)-w\gamma\theta s\bigr],\qquad e=\frac{1-\sigma}{\sigma}.$$
At $f=a=c=w=1$ this is capital’s $T$, and at $f=a=c=w=0$ it is the
one-agent planner’s. For (i), set $f=a=c=0$. With $\sigma\le1$, $e\ge0$,
and so $T=1+s(1-\gamma)-e\,w\gamma\theta s\le1+s(1-\gamma)$ for
$w\in\{0,1\}$. Hence $L(1+s(1-\gamma))<1$ implies $LT<1$. For both (i)
and (ii), $f=a=c=0$ gives the decider
$G=\gamma s(1-w\theta\varepsilon)$, which is positive at every
$\varepsilon<1$ because $1-w\theta\varepsilon>0$ there. So $D=1-L+LG>0$,
and $M=s-G<1$ gives $1-LM>0$, so the decider’s stationary valuation is
finite at every $\sigma$. For (ii),
$T=1+s(1-\gamma)+\varepsilon w\gamma\theta s<1+s(1-\gamma)+\gamma\theta s\le1+s<2$
at every $\varepsilon<1$, so $L\le1/2$ gives $LT<1$. For (iii),
subtracting gives $e[s(1-\gamma\theta)+p(1-s)]$. The bracket is positive
on the unit box because $s(1-\gamma\theta)>0$ and $p(1-s)\ge0$. The
difference therefore vanishes if and only if $\sigma=1$.
