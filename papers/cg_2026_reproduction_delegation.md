# Cash or Kind When the Household Decides the Split
Carlos Galindo Escajeda
2026-09-01

# Abstract

Much of what it costs to rear the next working generation is paid by
someone who does not do the rearing, and whoever pays chooses a form at
the same cost: cash into the household’s budget, or a public input
provided directly. This paper derives that choice from the payer’s
position rather than from its tastes: the payer hires the cohort the
fund rears, and what a household counts as strictly necessary is
inherited from its own past consumption. Where the standard of necessity
is absent and the two rearing inputs combine at unit elasticity, the
household’s control of the split changes nothing: the payer’s preferred
split is the one it would choose if it set the split itself. Once the
standard is inherited, the two forms separate: at unit elasticity and to
first order in the habituation rates, the standard raises the payer’s
preferred cash share at every horizon. Which way patience then pulls is
decided by a comparison the payer’s own nature does not settle: a longer
horizon moves the fund toward the public input exactly when public
provision raises the future standard by less than unspent cash does. A
payer able to bind itself at the outset faces no such ambiguity: it
moves toward cash whenever its own provision leaves a mark at all.
Patience therefore has a determinate direction only under commitment;
elsewhere it points wherever the two habituation rates send it, and
those rates are an empirical matter.

**Keywords:** transfers in cash and in kind; reproduction of labour;
habit formation; subsistence standard; public provision of private
goods; overlapping generations.

**JEL:** H42; H23; J13; D91.

# 1. Introduction

Rearing the next working generation is expensive, and most of the bill
is not paid by the households that do it. Employers pay through the wage
bill and the levies on it; the state pays through childcare, schooling
and child health. Anyone in that position has a choice that is separate
from how much to spend: whether to hand the household money and leave it
to decide how much of that money reaches the children, or to provide the
service itself as a public input. The two cost the same. Which is
better?

Economics has a good answer to that question, and it rests on two
premises worth naming. In the literature on transfers in cash and in
kind the payer is a donor, and the choice follows from what the donor
values and what the recipient can do. A donor who thinks the recipient
will spend too little on the children prefers to give in kind; a donor
who cannot see who is poor uses the in-kind form to sort; and provision
in kind crowds private spending on the same good out, or in. Peltzman
(1973) opened the crowding question, Epple and Romano (1996) and Singh
and Thomas (2000) worked out the sorting and the top-up, and Currie and
Gahvari (2008) survey both the arguments and the evidence. In each case
the donor’s valuation of how the recipient spends is a primitive —
paternalism, or a screening technology — and what the recipient counts
as necessary stays fixed while the transfer arrives.

Put an employer in the donor’s place. It hires what the fund rears, so
its valuation of the household’s spending is not a taste: it follows
from the technology that turns that spending into the next workforce and
from the horizon over which it collects the return. Then let what the
household counts as strictly necessary next period be inherited from
what it consumed this period. Money that passes through the household’s
own budget raises what the household consumes, and so raises what its
successors will count as strictly necessary; a public input provided
directly need not leave that mark.[^1] That standards of necessity move
with resources is Easterlin’s (1976) reading of American fertility,
where couples weigh what they aspire to against what they have; that the
standard is inherited from the parents’ consumption is de la Croix’s
(1996) formalisation in an overlapping-generations economy. A
subsistence floor fixed in the utility function leaves the question
untouched, because what is at issue is what the household’s own spending
does to its successors’ floor. Neither antecedent has a transfer in it,
and neither has a payer with a choice of form.

An employer with a horizon, a choice of form, and a standard of
necessity that the household’s own spending writes into: put the three
together and the form of the fund — the total the payer pays, in
whatever mixture of the two — becomes a question about the payer’s
horizon, because the mark that cash leaves is a cost the payer meets
only later. This paper shows that the horizon alone does not settle it.
What settles it is the horizon together with two habituation rates — the
rate at which the household’s own consumption raises what the next
cohort will count as necessary, and the rate at which the payer’s
provision does. The first result is that the obvious suspect is
innocent. The payer and the household disagree about the split: the
household eats out of the same budget it rears from, so it wants more of
the fund in cash than the payer does. That disagreement decides nothing.
With no inherited standard and with the two rearing inputs combining at
unit elasticity, the textbook Cobb–Douglas case, the household spends a
constant fraction of whatever it is handed, and that fraction drops out
of the payer’s comparison. The payer’s preferred cash share is then
exactly the share it would pick if it set the split itself — the same
number, and one that depends on neither party’s preferences. Away from
unit elasticity the household’s discretion is a relabelling: it is
equivalent to changing the weight the rearing technology puts on the
household’s own spending, upward where the two inputs substitute easily
and downward where they do not. Where they substitute less easily than
at unit elasticity, the relabelling is the whole of it. The preferred
cash share moves with the size of the fund, and with the share of the
wage bill paid to the retired, by the same formulas as in an economy
with no discretion at all; the fund below which the payer gives no cash
takes the same form; and the household still values cash at the margin.
Where they substitute more easily, the household’s response can put a
trough in the payer’s objective between the corner and an interior peak,
and a growing fund can tip the payer from a positive cash share to none.

The second result is that the payer’s patience does not settle the form
either. Switch the inherited standard on, and the two forms stop being
two names for the same money. The standard owed today can be met only
out of cash, because the public input reaches the cohort only through
rearing that has already been paid for out of a smaller purse. At unit
elasticity in rearing, and to first order in the two habituation rates,
that raises the payer’s preferred cash share whatever its horizon. The
comparison does not turn on which way the horizon points: at every
horizon an inherited standard makes the payer want more of the fund in
cash than it would under a fixed standard. Where the public input leaves
no mark of its own, the fund below which the payer gives no cash falls
as well, so the standard buys cash where none was given before. But cash
also raises the standard the next cohort will inherit, at a rate given
by how fast the household’s own consumption habituates the standard
times its propensity to consume out of what it has above the standard;
and the public input that the cash displaced would itself have raised
that standard, at its own rate. The payer weighs the net of those two at
its own discount factor. So a longer horizon moves the fund toward the
public input exactly when public provision habituates the standard less
than the unspent cash does, and toward cash when it habituates more. At
equality the payer’s horizon drops out of the form of the fund
altogether.

The third result says what that threshold belongs to: a payer that
re-optimises. A payer bound at the outset to a constant cash share,
starting from a standard of zero, faces a standard that scales with the
very money that pays it: what the household is asked to owe and the
money that pays it then move together, the household’s own habituation
drops out of the comparison, and the only mark left is the one the
payer’s own provision makes. Such a payer’s patience moves the fund
toward cash whenever its provision habituates at all, and there is no
threshold. Timing is not what does this: a payer choosing at the very
first date, before anything has been inherited, weighs the same two
rates in the same order as the re-optimiser. Finally, if the household’s
own weight on the generation it rears were private, no rule that varied
smoothly with the household’s report would draw a truthful one except a
rule that pays the household its own preferred cash share, which is more
cash than the payer wants; under the payer’s own rule a household asked
to report understates, because understating is rewarded in cash.

Three literatures bear on this, and each is missing one of the three
ingredients; which one is missing is what tells a reader what the
intersection costs. Miller and Neanidis (2015) compare cash with in-kind
transfers to families in an overlapping-generations economy with
fertility and child rearing. Cash raises fertility and welfare where
parents value numbers, while provision in kind does better where they
value quality. What carries the past forward in their economy is
children’s health, and their decider is a welfarist state, so a reader
who holds that paper has no payer with a horizon and no standard of
necessity, and hence no way to ask the question here. Aronsson and Schöb
(2022) show that habituation changes the Pareto-efficient provision rule
for a public good exactly when private and public consumption habituate
at different rates. That is the comparison the threshold below makes, so
a reader who holds their result already has the two rates; what such a
reader does not have is a payer choosing a form at a given cost, or a
private good whose habituation competes with a second use of the same
budget. Habit formation in optimal taxation (Koehne and Kuhn 2015; Guo
and Krause 2011; Tuomala and Tenhunen 2013) carries the standard but
offers no in-kind instrument. Public provision of private goods carries
the instrument without an own-past standard, whether provision is
Pareto-improving through relative consumption (König and Lausen 2017) or
through sorting with a private top-up (Epple and Romano 1996).

The nearest rival is the family of arguments in which the form of a
transfer matters because the payer cannot commit. If tomorrow’s transfer
will respond to today’s saving, in-kind provision that cannot be resold
does work that cash cannot (Bruce and Waldman 1991, for a parent and for
a public programme alike; Chatterjee and Dey 2025 in a
Samaritan’s-dilemma setting; Currie and Gahvari 2008 survey the family).
The force in this paper’s ratchet is the same force: money handed over
today raises what has to be handed over tomorrow. Its origin is not.
Here nothing is promised and nothing is renegotiated; the household’s
own past consumption sets the floor its successors inherit, and the
payer holds no promise it would like to break. The two accounts also
part company on an observable. A commitment story has no place for the
rate at which the payer’s own provision habituates the standard, and in
this model that rate is what decides the direction the horizon pushes
in.

Section 2 sets out the economy: a payer who hires the generation the
fund rears, a household that splits a budget it does not set between its
own consumption and rearing, a rearing technology with a private and a
public input, and a standard of necessity that the next cohort inherits.
Section 3 works out the benchmark in which the standard is fixed, first
at unit elasticity in rearing and then for any elasticity of
substitution. It shows that the household’s discretion moves numbers
without deciding anything. Section 4 switches the standard on. It
derives the household’s rule and the stationary standard, then the
payer’s preferred form and the threshold that signs the effect of its
horizon. It also asks what commitment does to that threshold, and how
far the answer depends on the payer’s own preferences over consumption.
Section 5 asks what could be done if the household’s weight on its
successors were private, and shows that a rule responding smoothly to
the household’s report is truthful only if it pays the household its own
preferred cash share. Section 6 draws the answer together. Proofs are in
Appendix A.

# 2. The model

This section sets out the economy and fixes what is compared: two
preferred forms of one fund, at a given cost to the payer.

**Production and time.** Output is $Y_t=AK_t^{\alpha}N_t^{1-\alpha}$
with $0<\alpha<1$, where $N_t$ is the working cohort. Capital
depreciates fully, a period is a generation, and the generational
discount factor is $\beta\in(0,1)$. Production is Cobb–Douglas
throughout.

**Instruments and institutions.** Every instrument is a share of capital
income $\alpha Y_t$. A levy $\rho_t$ finances a public input to rearing,
$G_t=\rho_t\alpha Y_t$, and a cash transfer $c_t$ is paid into the
working cohort’s budget. A pay-as-you-go share $\pi\in[0,1)$ of the wage
bill is paid to the retired and is held fixed. The cohort’s budget is
therefore $$W_t=\bigl[(1-\pi)(1-\alpha)+\alpha c_t\bigr]Y_t ,$$ and what
the owners of capital retain is $(1-\rho_t-c_t)\alpha Y_t$. Write
$D=(1-\pi)(1-\alpha)$ for the cohort’s wage income as a share of output,
and measure every quantity as a share of date-$t$ output: $w=D+\alpha c$
for the cohort’s budget and $g=\alpha(\tau-c)$ for the public input,
where $\tau=\rho+c$ is the fund. At a given fund the two shares sum to a
constant, $w+g=D+\alpha\tau$, which is what makes the two forms cost the
payer the same.

**Rearing.** The next working cohort is produced by the household’s own
spending on rearing, $R_t$, and the public input:
$$N_{t+1}=B\,N_t^{1-\gamma}\bigl[\theta R_t^{\varepsilon}+(1-\theta)G_t^{\varepsilon}\bigr]^{\gamma/\varepsilon},\qquad \varepsilon=\frac{\varsigma-1}{\varsigma},\quad \varsigma>0,\quad 0<\gamma<1,\quad 0<\theta<1 ,$$
where $\varsigma$ is the elasticity of substitution between the two
inputs. At $\varsigma=1$ this is
$N_{t+1}=B\,N_t(R_t/N_t)^{\gamma\theta}(G_t/N_t)^{\gamma(1-\theta)}$,
the textbook form in which a private and a public input combine into the
next generation’s human capital (Glomm and Ravikumar 1992). The
elasticity $\gamma$ says how strongly resources per child move the next
cohort and $\theta$ how the two inputs share that effect. The map is a
modelling device and is attributed to no one.

**The household.** Cohort $t$ receives $W_t$, spends $R_t$ on rearing
and consumes the rest. Necessary consumption per head, $\bar X_t$, is
met before anything else, so the cohort’s felicity is
$$\mu\ln\bigl(W_t-R_t-\bar X_tN_t\bigr)+\eta\ln N_{t+1},\qquad \mu>0,\ \eta>0 ,$$
defined where $W_t-R_t>\bar X_tN_t$. The weight $\eta$ is the cohort’s
own valuation of the size of the generation it rears, as in the
fertility literature that follows Barro and Becker (1989). Write
$k=\eta\gamma$. The payer observes $c_t$, $\rho_t$ and $N_{t+1}$, and
can make no instrument depend on $R_t$: the split is the household’s.

**The standard of necessity.** What the next cohort counts as strictly
necessary is inherited from what this one consumed and from what it
received in kind:
$$\bar X_{t+1}=\frac{\kappa\,(W_t-R_t)+\kappa_G\,G_t}{N_t},\qquad \kappa\in[0,1),\quad \kappa_G\ge 0,\quad \bar X_0=0 .$$
At $\kappa=\kappa_G=0$ the standard is absent and the economy is the
benchmark of Section 3. The coefficient $\kappa$ is the rate at which
the household’s own consumption habituates the standard and $\kappa_G$
the rate at which the public input does. Each cohort’s felicity has no
term in the standard its successors will inherit, so the household does
not weigh its own effect on that standard. Write $q_t=\bar X_tN_t/Y_t$
for the standard as a share of output.

**The payer.** Generation $t$ of the owners of capital has the objective
$$U^C_t=(1-\omega)\ln C_t+\omega\ln K_{t+1}+\lambda_C\beta\,U^C_{t+1},\qquad C_t+K_{t+1}=(1-\tau)\alpha Y_t ,$$
with $\omega\in(0,1)$ and $\lambda_C\in(0,1]$. The weight $\lambda_C$ is
the generation’s valuation of the one that inherits its capital:
$\lambda_C=1$ is the dynasty, and as $\lambda_C$ falls toward zero the
generation comes to care only about itself. Write $L=\beta\lambda_C$ for
the payer’s horizon, which is the only way $\beta$ and $\lambda_C$ enter
below. Under logarithmic objectives the generation invests a constant
share of what it retains, so its flow at a given fund is $\ln Y_t$ up to
a constant, and its value is $\sum_{j\ge0}L^j\ln Y_{t+j}$.

**What is compared.** Without the standard ($\kappa=\kappa_G=0$), every
transition is linear in the log-state and the instruments enter through
terms that do not involve it, so the economy is a linear-state game
(Dockner, Jørgensen, Long and Sorger 2000, §7.2) and the value of any
constant instrument path is linear in the log-state with coefficients
independent of it. With the standard on, the standard and the
instruments share one logarithm in the rearing law, and the payer’s
continuation is the first-order expansion of that game’s value in the
two habituation rates. Each result compares *preferred forms*: at a
given fund $\tau$, the cash share $c$ that maximises a class’s objective
when it sets that share alone, with the rest of the economy’s laws as
they are. The payer chooses at a date with the standard already owed,
later cash shares at the stationary policy, on the path along which
output per head is constant. Without the standard that path is the
economy’s steady state: under a constant policy, log output per head
follows $\ln y_{t+1}=\text{const}+\alpha(1-\gamma)\ln y_t$ with
$\alpha(1-\gamma)\in(0,1)$, so the path is reached from any initial
capital. This one-shot deviation is the payer’s Markov first-order
condition, because the next generation of owners maximises exactly the
continuation this one values. Results on the standard are stated to
first order in the two habituation rates $(\kappa,\kappa_G)$ at zero.

# 3. The benchmark: discretion when the standard is fixed

This section sets $\kappa=\kappa_G=0$ and asks what the household’s
discretion over the split does to the payer’s preferred form. The
comparison throughout is with the economy in which the household’s
rearing share is held at its own equilibrium value, so that the payer
faces the same numbers but no response.

**Proposition 1 (the fixed-standard benchmark, unit elasticity).** *At
$\varsigma=1$ and $\kappa=\kappa_G=0$ the household spends a constant
share of its budget on rearing,*
$$\frac{R_t}{W_t}=s\equiv\frac{k\theta}{\mu+k\theta},\qquad k=\eta\gamma ,$$
*which depends on neither the cash transfer nor the levy. The payer’s
preferred cash share is unique, and where it is interior it is*
$$c_C=\frac{\theta\alpha\tau-(1-\theta)D}{\alpha},$$ *and otherwise the
corner $c=0$; it is interior if and only if
$\tau>\tau_F\equiv(1-\theta)D/(\theta\alpha)$, and it does not depend on
$\eta$, $\mu$, $\gamma$, $\omega$ or $L$. In the economy with the
rearing share held at $s$ the payer’s preferred cash share is the same
number. At $c_C$ the household’s budget is
$w_C\equiv\theta(D+\alpha\tau)$, the public input is
$(1-\theta)w_C/\theta$, and the household’s marginal valuation of cash
is $\alpha\mu/w_C>0$, so the household wants more of the fund in cash
than the payer does.*

The payer’s own valuation of the next cohort cancels from its choice of
form, and so does everything about the household except the technology’s
weights. At a given fund the payer wants the split that rears the
largest next cohort, and it equates the marginal effect of the
household’s spending with that of the public input. Because the
household spends a constant fraction of whatever it is handed, the
constant drops out of that comparison: the payer’s preferred form is the
one it would choose if it set the split itself. The household agrees
about the rearing but also eats, so it wants more cash than the payer.
The contested margin is the cohort’s own consumption, not the next
generation. The corner has a reading too: below a fund of $\tau_F$ the
wage already carries so much of the private input that the payer puts
the whole fund into the public one.

**Proposition 2 (the discretion relabels the technology).** *At
$\kappa=\kappa_G=0$ and any $\varsigma>0$, let $x=R_t/G_t$ and let
$\phi=\theta x^{\varepsilon}/(\theta x^{\varepsilon}+1-\theta)$ be the
share of the rearing bracket carried by the household’s spending. The
household’s rearing share solves $s=k\phi/(\mu+k\phi)$, and
$1-\varepsilon(1-s)(1-\phi)>0$, so the cash share is strictly increasing
in $x$. At every interior critical point of the payer’s problem the
ratio of private to public rearing satisfies*
$$x^{1/\varsigma}=\frac{\theta_{\text{eff}}}{1-\theta_{\text{eff}}}\,s,\qquad \frac{\theta_{\text{eff}}}{1-\theta_{\text{eff}}}=\frac{\theta}{(1-\theta)\bigl[1-\varepsilon(1-s)\bigr]},\qquad \theta_{\text{eff}}\in(0,1),$$
*which is the condition of the economy with the rearing share held at
$s$, with $\theta$ replaced by $\theta_{\text{eff}}$. Hence $x$ exceeds
its held-share value by the factor $[1-\varepsilon(1-s)]^{-\varsigma}$,
which is above one exactly when $\varsigma>1$. The payer’s preferred
cash share is at least the held-share economy’s for $\varsigma>1$ and at
most it for $\varsigma<1$, strictly wherever either is interior; the
sign of the effect of the rearing share on the ratio of private to
public rearing is the sign of $\varsigma-1$ in both; and at every
interior critical point the household’s marginal valuation of cash is
$k\alpha(1-\phi)(1-s)/(\varsigma g s)>0$. For $\varsigma\in(0,1]$ the
payer’s problem has at most one interior critical point, which is then
its maximum. There the interior condition fixes $R=w/g$ independently of
$\tau$ and $\pi$, and where the preferred cash share is interior it
satisfies $\partial c^*/\partial\tau=R/(1+R)>0$ and
$\partial c^*/\partial\pi=(1-\alpha)/[\alpha(1+R)]>0$, the held-share
economy’s formulas; it is interior if and only if $\tau>D/(\alpha R)$,
the held-share economy’s form, with the location of that threshold
scaled by $[1-\varepsilon(1-s)]^{\varsigma}$.*

Away from unit elasticity the household’s rearing share does move with
the policy, because the share responds to the ratio of the two inputs.
At every candidate for the payer’s choice, what the discretion does can
be had by rewriting the technology’s weight on the household’s own
spending: a higher elasticity of substitution makes the household’s
response amplify cash, which the payer answers by giving more of it, and
a lower elasticity makes the response damp cash, which the payer answers
by giving less. Where the two inputs are harder to substitute than at
unit elasticity, that is all the discretion does. The payer’s problem
keeps a single peak, and every comparison the economy without discretion
makes comes back: the preferred share rises with the fund and with the
pay-as-you-go share by that economy’s own formulas; the fund below which
the payer gives no cash takes its form, with the location scaled by the
relabelling factor; and the household still values cash at the margin.
Where they are easier to substitute, the amplification can put a trough
in the payer’s objective between the corner and an interior peak. A
larger fund can then tip the payer from a positive cash share to none
(Appendix A.4 gives an example). A reader who wanted the household’s
choice to matter would have to ask what else the choice touches, and the
answer is the standard of necessity.

# 4. An inherited standard of necessity

Switching the standard on gives the payer’s problem a second margin:
what the household consumes today sets what its successors will count as
strictly necessary. What follows derives the household’s rule and the
stationary standard, then the payer’s preferred form, the threshold that
signs the effect of the payer’s horizon, and what changes if the payer
can bind itself. Results here are at unit elasticity in rearing and to
first order in the two habituation rates.

**Proposition 3 (the household and the stationary standard).** *At
$\varsigma=1$, for any $\kappa\in[0,1)$ and $\kappa_G\ge0$, the
household meets the standard first and then splits what is left in the
same proportion as before:*
$$R_t=s\bigl(W_t-\bar X_tN_t\bigr),\qquad s=\frac{k\theta}{\mu+k\theta} .$$
*Along a path on which output per head is constant the standard settles
at* $$q=\frac{\kappa(1-s)w+\kappa_G\,\alpha\rho}{1-\kappa s},$$ *which
lies below the household’s budget, $q<w$, if and only if
$\kappa_G\,\alpha\rho<(1-\kappa)w$, and so at every $\kappa$ when
$\kappa_G=0$; where the inequality fails the household cannot meet the
standard on such a path. On such a path the household’s rearing share is
$R_t/W_t=s(1-\kappa)/(1-\kappa s)$ at $\kappa_G=0$, which depends on
neither the cash transfer nor the levy. In the economy with the rearing
share held at its own stationary value the payer’s preferred cash share
is $c_C$ for every such $\kappa$ and $\kappa_G$ and every $L$, $\eta$
and $\mu$.*

The standard costs the household a fixed amount and then leaves its
behaviour unchanged, because logarithmic felicity over what is left
splits that remainder in the old proportion. On a path with constant
output per head the standard settles below the household’s budget
exactly when what the public input adds to it falls short of the part of
that budget the household’s own habituation leaves; this holds at every
rate of the household’s own habituation when the public input leaves no
mark, and where it fails no such path carries the standard at all. Two
further consequences matter. The first is that the standard reaches the
next cohort only through rearing that has already been paid for out of a
smaller purse, so the whole of it must be met out of the household’s
budget: the public input cannot pay it. The second is the benchmark.
Hold the rearing share at a number and the standard touches nothing the
payer values, so the payer’s preferred form is what it was in
Proposition 1, at every habituation rate and every horizon. Whatever the
standard does to the payer, it does through the household’s discretion.

**Proposition 4 (the payer’s form, and the threshold on its horizon).**
*At $\varsigma=1$, to first order in $(\kappa,\kappa_G)$ at zero, on the
path of Proposition 3 and with the standard owed at the decision date,
the payer’s preferred cash share, where it is interior, is*
$$c^*=c_C+\frac{(1-\theta)w_C}{\alpha}\Bigl[\kappa(1-L)(1-s)+\kappa_G\frac{(1-\theta)+\theta L}{\theta}\Bigr] ,$$
*which strictly exceeds $c_C$ whenever $L<1$ and
$(\kappa,\kappa_G)\neq(0,0)$, against $c_C$ itself in the held-share
economy. Its horizon effect is*
$$\frac{\partial c^*}{\partial L}=\frac{(1-\theta)w_C}{\alpha}\bigl[\kappa_G-\kappa(1-s)\bigr],\qquad 1-s=\frac{\mu}{\mu+\eta\gamma\theta},$$
*where $1-s$ is the household’s marginal propensity to consume out of
what it has above the standard; so a longer horizon moves the fund
toward the public input if and only if $\kappa_G<(1-s)\kappa$, toward
cash if and only if $\kappa_G>(1-s)\kappa$, and not at all at equality.
At $\kappa_G=0$ the cross-derivatives
$\partial^2c^*/\partial\kappa\,\partial L$ and
$\partial^2c^*/\partial\kappa\,\partial\eta$ are both strictly negative,
against zero in the held-share economy, and the corner moves to
$\bar\tau=\tau_F\bigl[1-\kappa(1-L)(1-s)\bigr]$.*

The standard enters the payer’s problem twice. What the household owes
today can be met only out of the money it holds, so a given fund rears
less the more of it arrives as a public input, and that raises the
payer’s preferred cash share whatever its horizon. The second entry is
tomorrow’s standard, and cash moves it two ways. The consumption that
unspent cash buys raises it, at the rate $\kappa$ times the household’s
propensity to consume out of what it has above the standard, and the
public input that the cash displaces would have raised it, at the rate
$\kappa_G$. The payer counts the net change at its own discount factor.
Where the first rate is the larger, cash rears less tomorrow and a
patient payer holds back; where the second is the larger, cash rears
more tomorrow and a patient payer adds cash. Together the two entries
raise the preferred cash share above $c_C$ at every horizon, and the
horizon scales the second alone. Where the public input leaves no mark
of its own, the same term moves the corner: the fund below which the
payer gives no cash falls in proportion, so an inherited standard buys
cash at funds that would otherwise have gone entirely into the public
input. At $\kappa_G=(1-s)\kappa$ the two rates are equal and the payer’s
horizon drops out of the form of the fund.

This is what the model answers to the question it was built for. The
form of the fund is not decided by the technology of rearing, because
the technology’s weight absorbs the household’s discretion wherever the
payer’s choice is interior (Proposition 2); and it is not decided by the
payer’s horizon either, because the same horizon points either way
across the threshold. It is decided by the horizon together with the two
habituation rates. So the reading that takes socialised provision of the
conditions of reproduction to follow from a payer’s long view loses its
direction: the same long view asks for cash wherever the payer’s own
provision habituates the standard faster than the household’s unspent
money does. A reader who wants the direction back must measure
$\kappa_G$ against $(1-s)\kappa$, and the model says nothing about which
side of that an economy sits on.

**Proposition 5 (commitment and the first date).** *At $\varsigma=1$ and
to first order in $(\kappa,\kappa_G)$ at zero, starting from
$\bar X_0=0$:*

*(i) a payer bound at the outset to a constant cash share prefers
$c^*=c_C+\kappa_G L(1-\theta)w_C/(\theta\alpha)$, with no term in
$\kappa$ and horizon effect $\kappa_G(1-\theta)w_C/(\theta\alpha)\ge0$,
which is zero exactly at $\kappa_G=0$;*

*(ii) a payer choosing at the first date alone, before any standard has
accumulated, prefers
$c^*=c_C+L(1-\theta)w_C\bigl[\kappa_G-\kappa(1-s)\bigr]/\alpha$, whose
horizon effect is the one in Proposition 4.*

Commitment removes the ratchet from the comparison. A payer who fixes
its cash share once and for all faces a standard that scales with the
money out of which it is paid, so the household’s consumption
habituation washes out of the logarithm and the only mark left is the
one the payer’s own provision makes. Such a payer’s horizon therefore
moves the fund toward cash whenever its provision habituates at all, and
there is no threshold. So the threshold of Proposition 4 belongs to a
payer who re-optimises, and what it prices is the obligation that this
period’s generosity leaves for the next one to meet. The timing of the
decision does not disturb it: a payer choosing at the very first date,
when nothing has yet been inherited, weighs the same two rates in the
same order.

**Remark 1 (the payer’s own preferences).** *Let the payer’s felicity
have constant relative risk aversion $\sigma>0$ in place of the
logarithm, in the two-period problem
$(1-\omega)u(C)+\omega u(K')+L\,u(Y')$ with
$Y'=A(K')^{\alpha}(N')^{1-\alpha}$. The payer’s marginal valuation of
the next cohort is $L(1-\alpha)(Y')^{1-\sigma}/N'$. It is free of the
capital the payer leaves, and hence of $\omega$, if and only if
$\sigma=1$; otherwise its elasticity in that capital is
$\alpha(1-\sigma)$.*

With logarithmic felicity the payer’s valuation of the next cohort is
free of the capital it leaves, which is why its horizon $L$ alone weighs
tomorrow’s ratchet against today’s standard in Proposition 4. Away from
the logarithm that valuation moves with the capital left behind: a payer
whose felicity is more concave than the logarithm values the next cohort
less the more capital it leaves for that cohort to work with, so the
weight it puts on the dates after its decision is no longer $L$ alone.
The results above are the logarithmic case, in which the two margins
separate.

# 5. A private weight on successors

Proposition 4 makes the payer’s preferred form depend on the household’s
own valuation of the generation it rears. This section asks what the
payer could do if only the household knew that valuation. A rule that
responds smoothly to the household’s report draws a truthful report only
if it pays the household its own preferred cash share, which the payer
does not want.

**Proposition 6 (a report of the household’s weight).** *At
$\varsigma=1$ and any standard $0\le q<D$ owed at the date, the
household’s indirect payoff in the cash share is strictly concave, and
its peak is*
$$c_H=\frac{(\mu+k\theta)\alpha\tau-k(1-\theta)(D-q)}{\alpha(\mu+k)}<\tau ,$$
*interior if and only if $(\mu+k\theta)\alpha\tau>k(1-\theta)(D-q)$ and
the corner $c=0$ otherwise, with $\partial c_H/\partial\eta<0$; at $q=0$
it exceeds the payer’s split by
$\mu(1-\theta)(\alpha\tau+D)/\bigl(\alpha(\mu+k)\bigr)>0$. Strict
concavity in the cash share holds at every $\varsigma>0$. Consequently,
on any interval of weights on which $c_H$ is interior, a differentiable
rule $c(\hat\eta)$ under which the household’s own report
$\hat\eta=\eta$ maximises its payoff at every $\eta$ is either constant
in the report or equal to $c_H(\eta)$ at every $\eta$ in it. The payer’s
rule of Proposition 4 is neither: it is strictly decreasing in $\eta$
for $\kappa>0$, and the household’s marginal valuation of cash at it is
strictly positive for $(\kappa,\kappa_G)$ small enough, so a household
asked to report $\eta$ understates it.*

The household’s payoff is strictly concave in the cash share at every
elasticity of substitution in rearing, not only at unit elasticity, so
its peak is the only candidate a rule has to beat. That peak lies above
the payer’s for the reason Proposition 1 gave: the household eats out of
the same budget. The gap between the two is what makes reporting
profitable. Because the payer’s preferred cash share falls in the
household’s weight on its successors, a household that reports a smaller
weight is paid more in cash, and since it values cash at the margin it
takes the trade. Truthfulness would need the rule to sit exactly at the
household’s own peak, which the payer does not want, or to ignore the
report, which makes the report idle. So the private-information version
of the question has no answer in this class of rules, and the payer’s
problem is the one Section 4 solved, with the household’s weight taken
as it is.

# 6. Conclusion

An employer that funds the rearing of the generation it will hire, and
that must leave the split to the household, faces a choice of form. The
literature on transfers in cash and in kind answers that choice from the
donor’s tastes. Answered instead from the employer’s position — it hires
what the fund rears, so its valuation of the household’s spending is
derived rather than assumed — the answer is a threshold rather than a
direction.

The mechanism is a standard of necessity that the next cohort inherits
from this one’s consumption. Where that standard is absent, the
household’s control of the split decides nothing: the payer’s preferred
form is what it would be if the payer set the split itself, exactly so
at unit elasticity in rearing and, wherever the payer’s choice is
interior, up to a relabelling of the technology’s weight away from it.
Where the standard is present, cash does two things at once. It is the
only source out of which the standard already owed can be met, which
pushes the payer toward cash; and the part of it the household keeps
raises the standard its successors will inherit, while the public input
it displaces would have raised that standard too, so the payer weighs
the net change at its own discount factor. Together they leave the payer
wanting more cash than under a fixed standard at every horizon. A long
horizon strengthens the second effect alone, and whether that moves the
fund toward the public input or toward cash depends on which of the two
habituation rates is the larger.

That displaces the premise the donor-side literature shares with its
critics: that once we know what the payer values and how patient it is,
the form of the transfer follows. Here it does not. A payer’s patience
asks for provision in kind in one economy and for cash in another, with
the same technology, the same instruments and the same preferences,
separated only by two habituation rates. The functionalist reading,
under which socialised provision of the conditions of reproduction
expresses a far-sighted employer’s interest, is therefore a claim about
those rates rather than about the employer’s horizon. It can be had only
where public provision habituates the standard less than the cash
households do not spend on rearing. A payer that can bind itself at the
outset faces no such ambiguity, and that is the one case in which
patience has a direction: the case is institutional rather than
temperamental, and it points toward cash whenever the payer’s own
provision leaves a mark at all.

The results hold where they are proved: at unit elasticity of
substitution in rearing for everything involving the standard, to first
order in the two habituation rates around zero, and on the path along
which output per head is constant, which without the standard is the
economy’s steady state. Off unit elasticity the comparative statics of
the benchmark hold where the two rearing inputs are harder to substitute
than at unit elasticity. Where they are easier, the payer’s objective
can have two peaks, and which of them is higher can change with the size
of the fund. Extending the results would take two further steps. A
second order in the habituation rates would say how far from the
threshold the comparison survives, and a rearing technology off unit
elasticity would let the relabelling of Proposition 2 interact with the
standard rather than sit beside it. That the payer here is the owner of
capital, with a generational horizon and a claim on the output of the
cohort the fund rears, is the institution written into Section 2. Any
principal with the same horizon and the same claim faces the same
comparison. The horizon that decides the form is one payer’s; where
several fund the same rearing, the composition of the fund would have to
be settled between them.

What the threshold leaves is an empirical question with an address. It
asks how much of what a household comes to count as strictly necessary
is set by what it consumed out of its own budget, and how much by what
it was given in kind. Those two rates decide which way a re-optimising
payer’s patience moves the fund, and until they are measured that
patience predicts nothing about the direction.

# Appendix A. Proofs

Throughout, lower-case letters are shares of date-$t$ output:
$w=D+\alpha c$, $g=\alpha(\tau-c)$, $r=R_t/Y_t$, $q=\bar X_tN_t/Y_t$,
with $D=(1-\pi)(1-\alpha)$ and $w+g=D+\alpha\tau$ at a given fund
$\tau$. Write $k=\eta\gamma$ and $\varepsilon=(\varsigma-1)/\varsigma$.

## A.1 The payer’s value and what it maximises at a given fund

**Lemma A.1.** *Under the objective of Section 2 the payer’s flow at a
given fund is $\ln Y_t$ up to a constant, its value under a constant
policy is $a\ln K_t+b\ln N_t$ up to a constant with*
$$a=\frac{\alpha\bigl[1-L(1-\gamma)\bigr]}{(1-L)\bigl[1-L\alpha(1-\gamma)\bigr]},\qquad b=\frac{1-\alpha}{(1-L)\bigl[1-L\alpha(1-\gamma)\bigr]},$$
*so that $a+b=1/(1-L)$, $b/(1+La)=(1-\alpha)/(1-L+L\gamma\alpha)$ and
$Lb>0$ for $L\in(0,1)$. At a given fund the cash share enters the
payer’s value only through $\ln N_{t+1}$, and only through*
$$h(c,q)=\gamma\Bigl[\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\bigl(\theta\,r(c,q)^{\varepsilon}+(1-\theta)g(c)^{\varepsilon}\bigr)\Bigr],$$
*with $h(c,q)=\gamma[\theta\ln r+(1-\theta)\ln g]$ at $\varsigma=1$.
Hence the payer maximises the size of the next cohort given the
household’s response.*

*Proof.* With $C_t+K_{t+1}=M_t$ and felicity
$(1-\omega)\ln C_t+\omega\ln K_{t+1}$, the optimum is
$C_t=(1-\omega)M_t$ and $K_{t+1}=\omega M_t$, so the flow is $\ln M_t$
up to a constant, and $M_t=(1-\tau)\alpha Y_t$ gives $\ln Y_t$ up to a
constant at a given fund. Summing, $U^C_t=\sum_{j\ge0}L^j\ln Y_{t+j}$ up
to a constant.

Take logarithms of the two laws of motion. Production gives
$\ln Y_t=\ln A+\alpha\ln K_t+(1-\alpha)\ln N_t$ and
$\ln K_{t+1}=\ln\bigl(\omega(1-\tau)\alpha\bigr)+\ln Y_t$. For rearing,
both $R_t$ and $G_t$ are shares of $Y_t$, and the bracket is homogeneous
of degree $\varepsilon$, so
$$\tfrac{\gamma}{\varepsilon}\ln\bigl[\theta R_t^{\varepsilon}+(1-\theta)G_t^{\varepsilon}\bigr]=\gamma\ln Y_t+\tfrac{\gamma}{\varepsilon}\ln\bigl[\theta r^{\varepsilon}+(1-\theta)g^{\varepsilon}\bigr],$$
whence
$\ln N_{t+1}=\gamma\alpha\ln K_t+(1-\gamma\alpha)\ln N_t+h(c,q)+\text{const}$.
Posit $V=a\ln K+b\ln N+\text{const}$ and match coefficients in
$V=\ln Y+LV(K',N')$:
$$a=\alpha+L(a\alpha+b\gamma\alpha),\qquad b=(1-\alpha)+L\bigl(a(1-\alpha)+b(1-\gamma\alpha)\bigr).$$
Adding the two gives $a+b=1+L(a+b)$, so $a+b=1/(1-L)$. The second
equation gives $b\bigl[1-L(1-\gamma\alpha)\bigr]=(1-\alpha)(1+La)$, that
is $b=(1-\alpha)(1+La)/(1-L+L\gamma\alpha)$, which is the stated ratio.
Substituting it into the first,
$a=\alpha(1+La)\bigl[1-L(1-\gamma)\bigr]/(1-L+L\gamma\alpha)$, and
solving the resulting linear equation for $a$ — its coefficient is
$1-L\alpha[1-L(1-\gamma)]/(1-L+L\gamma\alpha)=(1-L)[1-L\alpha(1-\gamma)]/(1-L+L\gamma\alpha)$
— gives the displayed $a$; then $b=1/(1-L)-a$ gives the displayed $b$.
Both denominators are positive for $L\in(0,1)$, $\alpha\in(0,1)$ and
$\gamma\in(0,1)$, since $L\alpha(1-\gamma)<1$; and $1-\alpha>0$, so
$b>0$ and $Lb>0$.

At a given fund $\tau$, $K_{t+1}$ does not depend on $c_t$, and $K_t$,
$N_t$ are predetermined, so $\ln Y_t$ does not depend on $c_t$ either.
The whole effect of $c_t$ on dates $t+1$ and later therefore runs
through $N_{t+1}$, with
$\partial\ln N_{t+1}/\partial c=\partial h/\partial c$; the payer’s
derivative is $Lb\,\partial h/\partial c$, and $Lb>0$. $\square$

## A.2 The household

**Lemma A.2.** *At $\varsigma=1$ the household’s rule is
$R_t=s(W_t-\bar X_tN_t)$ with $s=k\theta/(\mu+k\theta)$. For any
$\varsigma>0$ its problem is strictly concave in $R_t$ and its rule
satisfies $s=k\phi/(\mu+k\phi)$, where
$\phi=\theta x^{\varepsilon}/(\theta x^{\varepsilon}+1-\theta)$ and
$x=R_t/G_t$.*

*Proof.* Write the felicity in shares as
$\mu\ln(w-q-r)+k\,\varepsilon^{-1}\ln[\theta r^{\varepsilon}+(1-\theta)g^{\varepsilon}]$,
dropping terms free of $r$. Differentiating the bracket,
$$\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\ \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\bigl[\theta r^{\varepsilon}+(1-\theta)g^{\varepsilon}\bigr]=\frac{\theta r^{\varepsilon-1}}{\theta r^{\varepsilon}+(1-\theta)g^{\varepsilon}}=\frac{\phi}{r},$$
so the first-order condition is $\mu/(w-q-r)=k\phi/r$. At $\varsigma=1$,
$\phi=\theta$ and the condition is $\mu r=k\theta(w-q-r)$, that is
$r=s(w-q)$ with $s=k\theta/(\mu+k\theta)$; in levels,
$R_t=s(W_t-\bar X_tN_t)$. For general $\varsigma$, write $r=s(w-q)$ and
the condition becomes $\mu s=k\phi(1-s)$, that is $s=k\phi/(\mu+k\phi)$.
For concavity, differentiate again: the first term contributes
$-\mu/(w-q-r)^2<0$ and the second
$\phi\bigl[\varepsilon(1-\phi)-1\bigr]/r^2$, where
$\varepsilon(1-\phi)-1<0$ because $\varepsilon<1$ and $\phi\in(0,1)$.
$\square$

## A.3 Proof of Proposition 1

At $\varsigma=1$ and $q=0$, Lemma A.2 gives $r=sw$ with $s$ constant,
which depends on neither $c$ nor $\rho$. By Lemma A.1 the payer
maximises $h(c,0)=\gamma[\theta\ln(sw)+(1-\theta)\ln g]$, and the
constant $s$ enters additively, so it drops out of the maximiser. The
first-order condition is $\theta\alpha/w=(1-\theta)\alpha/g$, that is
$\theta g=(1-\theta)w$; substituting $w=D+\alpha c$ and
$g=\alpha(\tau-c)$ and solving gives
$\alpha c=\theta\alpha\tau-(1-\theta)D$, the displayed $c_C$. The
objective is strictly concave in $c$ on $[0,\tau)$, since
$\partial^2h/\partial c^2=-\gamma[\theta\alpha^2/w^2+(1-\theta)/(\tau-c)^2]<0$,
so the maximiser is unique and is the corner $c=0$ when $c_C\le0$. Now
$c_C<\tau$ always, because
$c_C-\tau=-(1-\theta)(\alpha\tau+D)/\alpha<0$; and $c_C>0$ if and only
if $\theta\alpha\tau>(1-\theta)D$, that is $\tau>\tau_F$. Nothing in
$c_C$ involves $\eta$, $\mu$, $\gamma$, $\omega$ or $L$.

In the held-share economy the payer maximises
$\gamma[\theta\ln(\bar sw)+(1-\theta)\ln g]$ with $\bar s$ a number,
which has the same maximiser; at $\bar s=s$ the two economies therefore
choose the same cash share.

At $c_C$, $w=D+\theta\alpha\tau-(1-\theta)D=\theta(D+\alpha\tau)=w_C$
and
$g=\alpha\tau-\theta\alpha\tau+(1-\theta)D=(1-\theta)(D+\alpha\tau)=(1-\theta)w_C/\theta$.
The household’s indirect payoff at $q=0$ is
$\mu\ln\bigl((1-s)w\bigr)+k\theta\ln(sw)+k(1-\theta)\ln g$, that is
$(\mu+k\theta)\ln w+k(1-\theta)\ln g$ up to a constant, so its
derivative in $c$ is $(\mu+k\theta)\alpha/w-k(1-\theta)\alpha/g$.
Evaluated at $c_C$, the second term is
$k(1-\theta)\alpha\theta/\bigl((1-\theta)w_C\bigr)=k\theta\alpha/w_C$
and the sum is $\alpha\mu/w_C>0$. $\square$

## A.4 Proof of Proposition 2

At $q=0$ put $T=w+g=D+\alpha\tau$, a constant at a given fund, and
parametrise by $x=r/g$. From $r=sw$, $w/g=x/s$, so $g=Ts/(s+x)$ and
$w=Tx/(s+x)$. By Lemma A.1 the payer maximises, up to constants,
$$H=\ln s-\ln(s+x)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\bigl(\theta X+1-\theta\bigr),\qquad X=x^{\varepsilon},$$
because
$\varepsilon^{-1}\ln[\theta r^{\varepsilon}+(1-\theta)g^{\varepsilon}]=\ln g+\varepsilon^{-1}\ln(\theta X+1-\theta)$.
Along the household’s rule, $s$ depends on $x$ through $\phi$;
differentiating $s=k\phi/(\mu+k\phi)$ and
$\phi=\theta X/(\theta X+1-\theta)$ gives
$x\,\mathrm{d}s/\mathrm{d}x=s\varepsilon(1-s)(1-\phi)$. Substituting,
$$x(s+x)\frac{\mathrm{d}H}{\mathrm{d}x}=\phi s-x(1-\phi)\bigl[1-\varepsilon(1-s)\bigr] .$$
Since $\varepsilon<1$ and $s,\phi\in(0,1)$, the factor
$1-\varepsilon(1-s)$ is positive, and so is
$1-\varepsilon(1-s)(1-\phi)$; the latter gives
$\mathrm{d}w/\mathrm{d}x=Ts\bigl[1-\varepsilon(1-s)(1-\phi)\bigr]/(s+x)^2>0$,
so $c=(w-D)/\alpha$ is strictly increasing in $x$.

Setting the derivative to zero and dividing by $x(1-\phi)>0$, and using
$\phi/(1-\phi)=\theta X/(1-\theta)$, the interior condition is
$$\frac{\theta\,sX}{(1-\theta)x}=1-\varepsilon(1-s)\iff x^{1-\varepsilon}=\frac{\theta s}{(1-\theta)\bigl[1-\varepsilon(1-s)\bigr]} ,$$
and $1-\varepsilon=1/\varsigma$ gives the displayed form with
$\theta_{\text{eff}}$. In the held-share economy the derivative is
$\phi\bar s-x(1-\phi)$, whose zero is
$x^{1-\varepsilon}=\theta\bar s/(1-\theta)$: the same condition with
$\theta$ in place of $\theta_{\text{eff}}$, so at $\bar s=s$ the ratio
of the two solutions is $[1-\varepsilon(1-s)]^{-\varsigma}$. That factor
exceeds one exactly when $\varepsilon(1-s)>0$, that is when
$\varsigma>1$; with $c$ strictly increasing in $x$, the ordering of the
two preferred cash shares follows wherever the payer’s is interior. At
the corner the two coincide. At $\bar s=s$ the held-share derivative
$\phi s-x(1-\phi)$ lies below $\phi s-x(1-\phi)[1-\varepsilon(1-s)]$
when $\varepsilon>0$ and above it when $\varepsilon<0$. So for
$\varsigma>1$, if the payer’s objective does not rise at the corner,
neither does the held-share economy’s, which then chooses the corner
too; for $\varsigma<1$ the same holds with the two economies exchanged.
Since $\theta_{\text{eff}}/(1-\theta_{\text{eff}})>0$,
$\theta_{\text{eff}}\in(0,1)$.

In the held-share economy the payer’s objective in $c$ is strictly
concave, so its critical point is unique: with $p\in(0,1)$ the bracket
share, $A=\alpha/w$ and $B=\alpha/g$, its curvature is proportional to
$\varepsilon p(1-p)(A+B)^2-\bigl(pA^2+(1-p)B^2\bigr)=-\bigl(pA-(1-p)B\bigr)^2-(1-\varepsilon)p(1-p)(A+B)^2<0$,
and $1-\varepsilon=1/\varsigma>0$. With discretion the curvature also
carries the response of $s$ to $x$, and uniqueness needs its own
argument. For $\varsigma\in(0,1]$ write the interior condition as
$x^{1-\varepsilon}=\Phi(x)$ with
$\Phi(x)=\theta s(x)/\bigl((1-\theta)[1-\varepsilon(1-s(x))]\bigr)$. The
left side rises strictly from zero to infinity, since
$1-\varepsilon=1/\varsigma>0$. The right side is positive and
non-increasing, because
$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\frac{s}{1-\varepsilon(1-s)}=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{[1-\varepsilon(1-s)]^2}>0$
while $x\,\mathrm{d}s/\mathrm{d}x=s\varepsilon(1-s)(1-\phi)\le0$ for
$\varepsilon\le0$; and it is bounded above by $\theta/(1-\theta)$,
because $1-\varepsilon(1-s)\ge1$ there. So the two sides cross exactly
once, at $x^*$. Dividing $x(s+x)\,\mathrm{d}H/\mathrm{d}x$ by
$x(1-\phi)[1-\varepsilon(1-s)]>0$ gives $\Phi(x)x^{\varepsilon-1}-1$,
which has the sign of $\Phi(x)-x^{1-\varepsilon}$, so $H$ rises below
$x^*$ and falls above it. Since $c$ is strictly increasing in $x$, the
payer’s preferred cash share is $c(x^*)$ when that is positive and the
corner $c=0$ otherwise.

For the comparative statics, write $R=w/g$ at an interior critical
point. The interior condition involves neither $\tau$ nor $\pi$, and so
neither does $R$. For $\varsigma\in(0,1]$, at $x^*$, $w=TR/(1+R)$ and
$c^*=\bigl[(D+\alpha\tau)R/(1+R)-D\bigr]/\alpha$ where it is interior.
Hence $\partial c^*/\partial\tau=R/(1+R)>0$ and, using
$\partial D/\partial\pi=-(1-\alpha)$,
$\partial c^*/\partial\pi=(1-\alpha)/\bigl(\alpha(1+R)\bigr)>0$; both
formulas are the held-share economy’s with its own $R$. Also $c^*>0$ if
and only if $R\alpha\tau>D$, that is $\tau>D/(\alpha R)$; the threshold
is inversely proportional to $R$, and $R$ scales by
$[1-\varepsilon(1-s)]^{-\varsigma}$, so the threshold scales by
$[1-\varepsilon(1-s)]^{\varsigma}$. At every interior critical point and
every $\varsigma>0$, from $x^{1-\varepsilon}$ above,
$\ln R=\ln x-\ln s=(\varsigma-1)\ln s-\varsigma\ln[1-\varepsilon(1-s)]+\text{const}$,
whose derivative in $s$ is
$\varsigma\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)(1-s)/\bigl(s[1-\varepsilon(1-s)]\bigr)$;
in the held-share economy $\ln R=(\varsigma-1)\ln\bar s+\text{const}$.
Both have the sign of $\varsigma-1$, because $1-\varepsilon>0$.

For the ordering, the household’s value derivative is, by the envelope
theorem, $\mu\alpha/\bigl(w(1-s)\bigr)-k\alpha(1-\phi)/g$; using the
household’s condition $\mu=k\phi(1-s)/s$ and $w=xg/s$ this is
$(k\alpha/g)(\phi/x-(1-\phi))$. At the payer’s critical point
$\phi/x=(1-\phi)[1-\varepsilon(1-s)]/s$, so it equals
$k\alpha(1-\phi)(1-s)(1-\varepsilon)/(gs)=k\alpha(1-\phi)(1-s)/(\varsigma gs)>0$.

For $\varsigma>1$ the single-crossing argument fails, because $s$, and
with it $\Phi$, rises with $x$, and $\Phi$ can cross $x^{1-\varepsilon}$
more than once. At $\theta=0.3$, $\varsigma=2.5$, $k=1$ and $\mu=0.1$,
$x(s+x)\,\mathrm{d}H/\mathrm{d}x$ is negative at $x=0.0005$, positive at
$x=0.005$ and negative at $x=0.1$, so $H$ has an interior local minimum
below an interior local maximum. Numerically, with $\alpha=0.9$ and
$\pi=0.9947$ added, the payer’s preferred cash share is about $0.020$ at
$\tau=0.285$ and the corner $c=0$ at $\tau=0.315$: the corner’s value
falls short of the interior peak’s at the smaller fund and exceeds it at
the larger. $\square$

## A.5 Proof of Proposition 3

The law of the standard does not enter the household’s problem, so Lemma
A.2 applies with $q>0$ and gives $R_t=s(W_t-\bar X_tN_t)$ at
$\varsigma=1$. Then $W_t-R_t=(1-s)W_t+s\bar X_tN_t$, so in shares
$$q_{t+1}=\Bigl[\kappa\bigl((1-s)w_t+sq_t\bigr)+\kappa_G\,\alpha\rho_t\Bigr]\frac{y_t}{y_{t+1}},\qquad y_t=Y_t/N_t .$$
On a path with $y$ constant the ratio is one, and the stationary
solution of $q=\kappa[(1-s)w+sq]+\kappa_G\alpha\rho$ is the displayed
$q$. Since $1-\kappa s>0$, it lies below $w$ if and only if
$\kappa(1-s)w+\kappa_G\alpha\rho<(1-\kappa s)w$, that is
$\kappa_G\alpha\rho<(1-\kappa)w$; this is the condition $w-q>0$ under
which the household’s felicity is defined on the path and its rearing
$s(w-q)$ is positive. The rearing share is $r/w=s(w-q)/w$; at
$\kappa_G=0$, $q=\kappa(1-s)w/(1-\kappa s)$ is proportional to $w$, so
$$\frac{r}{w}=s\Bigl(1-\frac{\kappa(1-s)}{1-\kappa s}\Bigr)=\frac{s(1-\kappa)}{1-\kappa s},$$
free of $c$ and $\rho$. In the held-share economy $r=\bar sw$ whatever
the standard, so the payer maximises
$\gamma[\theta\ln(\bar sw)+(1-\theta)\ln g]$, whose maximiser is $c_C$
by the argument of A.3; the standard appears nowhere in it, hence at
every $\kappa$, $\kappa_G$, $L$, $\eta$ and $\mu$ the held-share
economy’s preferred cash share is $c_C$. $\square$

## A.6 Proof of Proposition 4

Write $Q(c)=\kappa(1-s)w(c)+\kappa_G\alpha(\tau-c)$, the first-order
stationary standard produced by a constant cash share $c$: along any ray
in $(\kappa,\kappa_G)$ the term $\kappa sq$ in Proposition 3 is second
order, so $q=Q(c)$ to first order. Near $(\kappa,\kappa_G)=(0,0)$ the
inequality of Proposition 3 holds, since there it reads $0<w$. Note
$$Q'(c)=\alpha\bigl[\kappa(1-s)-\kappa_G\bigr],$$ so cash raises
tomorrow’s standard through the household’s consumption and lowers it
through the public input it displaces.

By Lemma A.1 the payer’s objective in $c$ is $Lb\,h(c,q)$ plus
continuation terms, with
$h(c,q)=\gamma[\theta\ln(w(c)-q)+(1-\theta)\ln(\tau-c)]$ up to a
constant at $\varsigma=1$. Expanding in $q$ around zero,
$h(c,q)=h(c,0)-\gamma\theta q/w(c)+O(q^2)$. Three dates carry a
first-order term. The standard owed at the decision date is $Q(\bar c)$
for the stationary cash share $\bar c$, and it enters through
$-\gamma\theta Q(\bar c)/w(c)$. The standard at $t+1$ is $Q(c)$ times
the ratio of output per head, and it lowers $\ln N_{t+2}$ by
$\gamma\theta Q(c)/w(\bar c)$ times that ratio, valued at $L$. Later
dates carry $Q(\bar c)/w(\bar c)$ times a smooth function of the
deviation $\Delta=h(c,0)-h(\bar c,0)$. Collecting, the first-order part
of the objective is
$$-Lb\gamma\theta\Bigl[\frac{Q(\bar c)}{w(c)}+L\frac{Q(c)}{w(\bar c)}\rho(\Delta)+\frac{Q(\bar c)}{w(\bar c)}\Gamma(\Delta)\Bigr],$$
with $\rho$ and $\Gamma$ smooth and $\rho(0)=1$. Here
$\rho(\Delta)=e^{\alpha\Delta}$ is the ratio at $t+1$, and $\Gamma$ is a
sum over the later dates that may be differentiated term by term. The
two laws of motion in the proof of Lemma A.1 give
$\ln y_{t+1}=\text{const}+\alpha(1-\gamma)\ln y_t-\alpha h(c_t,q_t)$, so
at zeroth order a deviation at the decision date moves $\ln y_{t+j}$ by
$-\alpha[\alpha(1-\gamma)]^{j-1}\Delta$, and date $t+j$ contributes
$L^j$ times a fixed smooth function of the ratio of output per head at
that date. On a neighbourhood of $c_C$ each such term and its first two
derivatives in $c$ are therefore bounded by $ML^j$, with $M$ independent
of $j$; since $L<1$, the Weierstrass M-test makes the differentiated
series converge uniformly, and $\Gamma$ is twice continuously
differentiable. The same bound, with each date’s standard at most a
fixed multiple of $|(\kappa,\kappa_G)|$, makes the remainders $O(q^2)$
summed over all dates of second order in $(\kappa,\kappa_G)$. Because
$\Delta$ and its derivative vanish at $c=\bar c$ when $\bar c$ maximises
$h(\cdot,0)$, the terms in $\rho$ and $\Gamma$ contribute only through
$Q'(c)$: at $c=\bar c=c_C$ the derivative of the bracket is
$-Q(c_C)\alpha/w_C^2+LQ'(c_C)/w_C$.

The shift of the maximiser is
$-\,\partial_c(\text{first-order part})/\partial^2_c(\text{zeroth-order part})$
at $c_C$. The zeroth-order part is $Lb\,h(c,0)$, whose second derivative
at $c_C$ is, using $\tau-c_C=(1-\theta)w_C/(\theta\alpha)$,
$$-Lb\gamma\alpha^2\Bigl[\frac{\theta}{w_C^2}+\frac{\theta^2}{(1-\theta)w_C^2}\Bigr]=-\frac{Lb\gamma\alpha^2\theta}{(1-\theta)w_C^2} .$$
Dividing,
$$\mathrm{d}c^*=\frac{1-\theta}{\alpha}\Bigl[Q(c_C)-\frac{L\,w_C\,Q'(c_C)}{\alpha}\Bigr] .$$
Now $Q(c_C)=\kappa(1-s)w_C+\kappa_G(1-\theta)w_C/\theta$, because
$\alpha(\tau-c_C)=(1-\theta)w_C/\theta$. Substituting $Q(c_C)$ and $Q'$
gives
$$\mathrm{d}c^*=\frac{(1-\theta)w_C}{\alpha}\Bigl[\kappa(1-s)(1-L)+\kappa_G\frac{(1-\theta)+\theta L}{\theta}\Bigr],$$
which is the displayed $c^*-c_C$. Both coefficients are strictly
positive for $L<1$, so $c^*>c_C$ whenever $(\kappa,\kappa_G)\neq(0,0)$,
while the held-share economy stays at $c_C$ by Proposition 3.

Differentiating in $L$, the two coefficients give
$(1-\theta)w_C\bigl[\kappa_G-\kappa(1-s)\bigr]/\alpha$, which equals
$-(1-\theta)w_CQ'/\alpha^2$. It is negative exactly when
$\kappa_G<(1-s)\kappa$, positive exactly when $\kappa_G>(1-s)\kappa$,
and zero at equality; and
$1-s=\mu/(\mu+k\theta)=\mu/(\mu+\eta\gamma\theta)$. Differentiating it
once more,
$\partial^2c^*/\partial\kappa\,\partial L=-(1-\theta)(1-s)w_C/\alpha<0$
and $\partial^2c^*/\partial\kappa_G\,\partial L=(1-\theta)w_C/\alpha>0$.
At $\kappa_G=0$ the shift is $\kappa(1-L)(1-\theta)(1-s)w_C/\alpha$,
which is strictly decreasing in $k=\eta\gamma$ because
$1-s=\mu/(\mu+k\theta)$ is, so
$\partial^2c^*/\partial\kappa\,\partial\eta<0$; in the held-share
economy every one of these derivatives is zero.

For the corner at $\kappa_G=0$, the threshold $\bar\tau$ solves
$c^*(\bar\tau)=0$. At $\tau=\tau_F$ we have $c_C=0$ and
$D+\alpha\tau_F=D/\theta$, so $w_C=D$ and the shift is
$\kappa(1-L)(1-\theta)(1-s)D/\alpha$. Since
$\partial c_C/\partial\tau=\theta$, to first order
$$\bar\tau-\tau_F=-\frac{\kappa(1-L)(1-\theta)(1-s)D}{\alpha\theta}=-\kappa(1-L)(1-s)\,\tau_F ,$$
which is the displayed $\bar\tau$. $\square$

## A.7 Proof of Proposition 5

Both parts use the decomposition of A.6, with the dates that carry a
first-order term changed.

1)  A payer bound at the outset to a constant cash share, from
    $\bar X_0=0$, owes no standard at the date it chooses, so the term
    $Q(\bar c)/w(c)$ is absent; every later date carries $Q(c)/w(c)$
    times the discounted sum of the output-per-head ratios, which on the
    stationary path is $L/(1-L)$. The first-order part is then
    $-Lb\gamma\theta\,Q(c)H(\Delta)/w(c)$ with $H(0)=L/(1-L)$, and $H$
    is twice continuously differentiable by the bound in A.6, since
    under a constant deviation the logarithm of each ratio of output per
    head again moves by $[\alpha(1-\gamma)]^{j-1}$ times a fixed
    multiple of $\Delta$; its derivative at $c_C$ is
    $\bigl[Q'(c_C)-Q(c_C)\alpha/w_C\bigr]L/\bigl((1-L)w_C\bigr)$; the
    zeroth-order part is now $Lb\,h(c,0)/(1-L)$, so its second
    derivative carries the same factor $1/(1-L)$, which cancels.
    Dividing as in A.6,
    $$\mathrm{d}c^*=\frac{(1-\theta)L}{\alpha}\Bigl[Q(c_C)-\frac{w_CQ'(c_C)}{\alpha}\Bigr] ,$$
    and substituting
    $Q(c_C)=\kappa(1-s)w_C+\kappa_G(1-\theta)w_C/\theta$ and
    $Q'(c_C)/\alpha=\kappa(1-s)-\kappa_G$ the terms in $\kappa$ cancel
    exactly, leaving
    $$\mathrm{d}c^*=\frac{(1-\theta)L\,w_C}{\alpha}\Bigl[\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}+1\Bigr]\kappa_G=\frac{\kappa_GL(1-\theta)w_C}{\theta\alpha},$$
    whose derivative in $L$ is
    $\kappa_G(1-\theta)w_C/(\theta\alpha)\ge0$, zero exactly at
    $\kappa_G=0$. The cancellation is the ratchet’s: with the standard
    paid out of the same cash that produced it,
    $\ln(w-q)=\ln w+\ln(1-\kappa(1-s))$ to first order, and the second
    term does not involve $c$.

2)  A payer choosing at the first date alone, from $\bar X_0=0$, owes no
    standard at that date either, but its later cash shares are the
    stationary ones, so only the date-$(t+1)$ term responds to its
    choice: the first-order part is $-Lb\gamma\theta LQ(c)/w(\bar c)$,
    whose derivative at $c_C$ is $-Lb\gamma\theta LQ'(c_C)/w_C$.
    Dividing by the same second derivative as in A.6,
    $$\mathrm{d}c^*=-\frac{L(1-\theta)w_CQ'(c_C)}{\alpha^2}=\frac{L(1-\theta)w_C}{\alpha}\bigl[\kappa_G-\kappa(1-s)\bigr],$$
    whose derivative in $L$ is the expression in Proposition 4.
    $\square$

## A.8 Proof of Proposition 6

At $\varsigma=1$, substituting the household’s rule $r=s(w-q)$ into its
felicity gives, up to a constant,
$$V_H(c)=(\mu+k\theta)\ln\bigl(w(c)-q\bigr)+k(1-\theta)\ln g(c),$$ so
$V_H'(c)=(\mu+k\theta)\alpha/(w-q)-k(1-\theta)\alpha/g$ and
$V_H''(c)=-(\mu+k\theta)\alpha^2/(w-q)^2-k(1-\theta)\alpha^2/g^2<0$.
Setting $V_H'=0$ and using $w=D+\alpha c$, $g=\alpha(\tau-c)$,
$$(\mu+k\theta)\alpha(\tau-c)=k(1-\theta)(D-q+\alpha c)\iff \alpha c(\mu+k)=(\mu+k\theta)\alpha\tau-k(1-\theta)(D-q),$$
which is the displayed $c_H$, using $(\mu+k\theta)+k(1-\theta)=\mu+k$.
It is below $\tau$ because
$c_H-\tau=-k(1-\theta)(\alpha\tau+D-q)/\bigl(\alpha(\mu+k)\bigr)<0$,
with $k>0$ and $q<D$, and it is positive exactly under the stated
inequality; outside that set $V_H$ is decreasing on $[0,\tau)$ and the
peak is $c=0$. Differentiating in $k$,
$$\frac{\partial c_H}{\partial k}=-\frac{\mu(1-\theta)(\alpha\tau+D-q)}{\alpha(\mu+k)^2}<0 ,$$
so $\partial c_H/\partial\eta=\gamma\,\partial c_H/\partial k<0$. At
$q=0$, $c_H-c_C=\mu(1-\theta)(\alpha\tau+D)/\bigl(\alpha(\mu+k)\bigr)>0$
by direct subtraction.

For every $\varsigma>0$, let
$J(r,c)=\mu\ln\bigl(w(c)-q-r\bigr)+\gamma\ln f(r,g(c))$ with $f$ the
constant-elasticity aggregator
$[\theta r^{\varepsilon}+(1-\theta)g^{\varepsilon}]^{1/\varepsilon}$.
Then $f$ is concave and positively homogeneous of degree one for
$\varepsilon<1$, so $\ln f$ is concave, and $(r,c)\mapsto(r,g(c))$ is
affine, so the second term is concave in $(r,c)$; the first is concave
as the logarithm of an affine function. Along any direction
$(\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}c)\neq0$ either
$\alpha\,\mathrm{d}c-\mathrm{d}r\neq0$, and the first term is strictly
concave along it, or $\mathrm{d}r=\alpha\,\mathrm{d}c$ and hence
$\mathrm{d}g=-\alpha\,\mathrm{d}c$, a direction off the ray through
$(r,g)$, along which $\ln f$ is strictly concave. So $J$ is strictly
concave in $(r,c)$, and $V_H(c)=\max_rJ(r,c)$ is strictly concave in
$c$. The peak is interior at the upper end because
$\partial\ln f/\partial g\to\infty$ as $g\to0$ for every
$\varepsilon<1$.

Now let $c(\cdot)$ be differentiable and suppose $\hat\eta=\eta$
maximises $V_H\bigl(c(\hat\eta)\bigr)$, where $V_H$ is evaluated at the
household’s true $\eta$, for every $\eta$. The first-order condition is
$V_H'\bigl(c(\eta)\bigr)c'(\eta)=0$ at every $\eta$, so at each $\eta$
either $c'(\eta)=0$ or $c(\eta)$ is the peak $c_H(\eta)$. On an interval
$I$ of weights on which $c_H$ is interior, $c_H'<0$, and the dichotomy
holds on the whole of $I$. Let $E\subseteq I$ be the set on which
$c=c_H$, which is closed in $I$ because both are continuous. At a point
of $E$ that is a limit of other points of $E$, taking the difference
quotient along those points gives $c'=c_H'\neq0$. At every other point
of $I$, $c'=0$: off $E$ by the condition above, and at an isolated point
of $E$ because $c'=0$ on each side of it nearby, so $c$ is constant
across it. So the set $F$ of limit points of $E$, on which $c'\neq0$, is
closed, and its complement in $I$, on which $c'=0$, is open. If both
were non-empty, some component of the complement would have an endpoint
$\eta_1\in F$ inside $I$; $c$ is constant on that component, so its
one-sided derivative at $\eta_1$ is zero, against $c'(\eta_1)\neq0$.
Hence either $F$ is empty and $c$ is constant on $I$, or $F=I$ and
$c=c_H$ throughout $I$. The payer’s rule of Proposition 4 at $\kappa>0$
is
$c^*=c_C+\kappa(1-L)(1-\theta)(1-s)w_C/\alpha+O(\kappa\kappa_G,\kappa^2)$,
and $1-s=\mu/(\mu+\eta\gamma\theta)$ is strictly decreasing in $\eta$,
so $\mathrm{d}c^*/\mathrm{d}\eta<0$; and $V_H'(c_C)=\alpha\mu/w_C>0$ at
$\kappa=\kappa_G=0$ by A.3, so $V_H'(c^*)>0$ for $(\kappa,\kappa_G)$
small enough by continuity. Hence $c^*$ is neither flat nor the
household’s peak. Since $c^*$ is decreasing in the report and
$V_H'(c^*)>0$, a household reporting $\hat\eta<\eta$ raises the cash
share and its own payoff, so it understates $\eta$. $\square$

## A.9 Proof of Remark 1

With $u(z)=z^{1-\sigma}/(1-\sigma)$ for $\sigma\neq1$ and
$Y'=A(K')^{\alpha}(N')^{1-\alpha}$,
$$\frac{\partial}{\partial N'}\,L\,u(Y')=L(Y')^{-\sigma}\frac{\partial Y'}{\partial N'}=L(Y')^{-\sigma}\frac{(1-\alpha)Y'}{N'}=\frac{L(1-\alpha)(Y')^{1-\sigma}}{N'} .$$
Since
$(Y')^{1-\sigma}=\bigl(A(K')^{\alpha}(N')^{1-\alpha}\bigr)^{1-\sigma}$,
the elasticity of this valuation in $K'$ is $\alpha(1-\sigma)$, which
vanishes if and only if $\sigma=1$; and $\omega$ enters only through
$K'$. At $\sigma=1$ the valuation is $L(1-\alpha)/N'$, free of $K'$.
$\square$
