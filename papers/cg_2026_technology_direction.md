# Whose Technology? The Direction of Technical Change When the Workforce Is Produced
Carlos Galindo Escajeda
2026-09-01

# Abstract

The theory of induced innovation weighs an improvement to labour against an improvement to capital by the two factors’ shares of income, and, like the modern theory of appropriate technology, it takes both factors as given. This paper asks which way technical change should go when the economy produces its factors: capital out of capital income, and each generation of workers out of wages and out of public provision paid for by a tax on capital. Six agents choose the direction in turn: the owner of capital, the working class, a Pareto planner, a state that values its fund, a state that values output, and the market. In a first-order model of that economy each wants the direction that maximises capital income, corrected by the substitution gap, one minus the inverse of the elasticity of substitution, times the weight its own future places on the income that raises the next workforce. Call a technology more biased toward labour when, at given stocks, it gives labour a larger share. Capital’s choice then lands strictly between two points: it favours labour more than the technology that maximises capital’s own income, and less than the market’s. The worker wants one more biased toward labour than the market exactly when the private share of her class’s reproduction exceeds a threshold fixed by patience and the factor shares; below it both classes lean toward capital and disagree only about how far, because the public fund that reproduces the workforce is a claim on capital’s income. A treasury that values its fund wants exactly capital’s technology, a state that values output leans further toward capital than the market, and a Pareto planner sits between the classes. These rankings hold at every elasticity of substitution above one and, below one, wherever capital’s share lies between one minus the elasticity and the elasticity. When wages as well as capital income fill the fund, the treasury’s technology follows its tax base, from capital’s to the worker’s, and the worker still wants a technology more biased toward labour than capital does. Above unit elasticity, paying more of the capital tax to the workers as cash rather than as provision in kind moves capital, the worker and both states to technologies less biased toward labour and raises the worker’s threshold. Under Cobb–Douglas all six agents want the same technology. What the working class wants from technical change therefore depends on how its own reproduction is paid for.

**JEL Classification:** O33, D33, E11

**Keywords:** directed technical change; induced innovation; factor-augmenting technology; reproduction of the workforce; public provision; class conflict; linear-state games

# 1. Introduction

The benchmark answer to the question of which factor technical change should favour is a price answer. An innovator who can raise the efficiency of labour or of capital values each improvement by what the factor is paid, and so weighs the two by their shares of income. Kennedy (1964) put this rule at the centre of induced innovation. In the modern theory of appropriate technology firms pick a pair of labour- and capital-augmenting efficiencies from a menu on which more of one means less of the other, given the factors the economy has (Caselli and Coleman 2006; León-Ledesma and Satchi 2019), and the pair that maximises output weights labour augmentation by labour’s share. In all of these the factors are endowments. An agent’s interest in a factor stops at its price, and no class has a view.

The benchmark has an obvious political reading, and the two literatures that bring classes in keep it. Workers should want technology that makes labour more productive, owners technology that makes capital more productive, and the market should land in between. In the classical tradition, a bias toward saving labour is how capital answers the strength of labour (Kennedy 1964; Shah and Desai 1981; Foley 2003; Duménil and Lévy 2003; Julius 2005; surveyed by Tavani and Zamparelli 2017).[^1] In the model of automation and repression of Acemoglu, Gitmez and Shadmehr (2026), where the workforce is given, the political reading holds for the classes themselves: the level of automation that maximises labour’s income is below the output-maximising level, and the level that maximises capital’s is above it.

The departure is that a workforce is not given. Each generation of workers has to be fed, housed, schooled and kept well, and the money comes from two places: what its parents earn, and public provision paid for out of a tax on capital. Once labour is produced this way, a technology reaches every agent through two channels. The first is what it pays that agent today, and the benchmark has only this one. The second is its effect on the wages and the tax revenue out of which the next generation of workers will be raised. The owner of capital will hire those workers, the working class is those workers, and a treasury that lives on the capital tax draws on the same income that pays for them. All three care about the second channel, by different amounts.

Two arguments that now run separately make the question live. One is about automation: how much of the next wave of technique should save labour and how much should save capital. The other is about who pays for childhood, schooling and health: families or the state. Should the financing of the workforce shift what each class asks of new technique, the debate over automation cannot be settled apart from the debate over how the next generation is paid for.

The answer is one rule. Each agent wants the direction that would maximise capital income, corrected by one term. The term is the weight the agent’s own future places on the income from which the next workforce is built, multiplied by the substitution gap, which is zero at unit elasticity and grows with the elasticity above it (Proposition 2). The market’s weight is labour’s share of income. Capital’s weight is positive and strictly below labour’s share. Call a technology more biased toward labour when, at given stocks, it gives labour a larger share. Ranked this way, the agents’ choices come out the same at every elasticity of substitution above one and, below one, wherever capital’s share lies between one minus the elasticity and the elasticity (Section 8). Capital then wants a technology more biased toward labour than its own income dictates, because it hires the workers whom workers’ income raises, and less biased than the market supplies, because its stake in that income is a fraction of its stake in its own (Proposition 4). The classical view that capital’s interest is a bias against labour is right when capital is compared with the market and wrong when capital is compared with its own income.

The working class is where the political reading breaks. At every private share of her reproduction, the worker wants a technology more biased toward labour than the owner wants (Proposition 3). Against the market, what decides is how her class is reproduced. If a large enough share of her reproduction is paid out of her own wage, she wants more labour bias than the market supplies, as the political reading says. If more of it is paid publicly, she leans toward capital as the owner does, because the public fund that raises her children is built out of capital’s income (Proposition 5). Only two things set that threshold: how patient the economy is and how income divides between the factors. Reading a period as twenty-five years, annual discount factors of 0.96 to 0.98 and a labour share of two thirds put it between about 0.53 and 0.75. Below it the classes still disagree, but over how far to go in one direction rather than over which direction to take.

A state is one point on the menu per objective. A treasury that values the fund it administers wants exactly capital’s technology, and so does any agent whose objective is built only from capital income and the two stocks it finances (Proposition 8). A state that values output over time leans further toward capital than the market, because capital income is what next period’s output is built from, and not as far as capital. Whether it leans further than the worker is settled by a second threshold on the private share, strictly below the first, so that one number, the private share of the workforce’s reproduction, orders capital, the worker, both states and the market completely (Proposition 9). A Pareto planner, whatever weights it puts on the two classes, stays between them (Proposition 6). Paying part of the fund to the workers in cash rather than in kind moves capital, the worker and both states toward capital’s technology and raises both thresholds, and the order stays the same (Proposition 12).

The two ends of the elasticity line bracket all of this. Under Cobb–Douglas every income is a fixed share of output, every stake in technology is a stake in output, and all six agents want the same technology; the agreement survives partial depreciation and isoelastic objectives (Proposition 1). A model of this economy solved at unit elasticity therefore says nothing about the direction of technical change: the market does not win there; every agent wants the same technology, so there is nothing to choose. At the other end, when capital and labour are perfect substitutes, capital’s income stops depending on the workforce, and capital runs to pure capital augmentation, while the worker’s choice stays interior and approaches pure labour augmentation as the public part of her reproduction shrinks (Proposition 7). In between, the rankings are theorems of a first-order approximation of the economy, taken around a reference ratio of capital to labour. The approximation is exact at unit elasticity and at perfect substitution, and at a steady state it shares its first-order conditions with the exact economy, so that capital’s steady-state direction, where interior, is exactly the formula. When the elasticity is below one, every ordering of the agents’ directions flips, and augmenting labour now lowers labour’s share; the two reversals cancel, and the agent that wants the technology giving labour more is the same one as above unit elasticity.

In the models Tavani and Zamparelli (2017) survey the chooser is a firm or a capitalist household moving along Kennedy’s frontier, and no model they describe lets workers, a planner or a state choose the direction. In Julius (2005) firms choose the direction to raise the profit rate; workers’ bargaining strength and a profits tax that pays a social wage move distribution and, through it, the direction, while the workforce grows at a given rate. The tax-financed state of Tavani and Zamparelli (2018) pays for research that raises labour productivity, along a single dimension of technical change rather than a choice between two. Shah and Desai (1981) embed the firm’s induced bias in Goodwin’s growth cycle. On the neoclassical side the optimal rate and direction of technical change go back to Nordhaus (1967); the choosers this paper adds are the two classes and the two states. Acemoglu, Gitmez and Shadmehr (2026) have the outcome depart from capital’s preferred level of automation only through the state: a regime that fears revolt imposes labour’s level, and a repressive one automates beyond capital’s own. The correction found here has the same sign, but its chooser is capital itself, its motive is the workers capital will hire, and it stops strictly short of the market’s point. Galor and Moav (2006) find capitalists backing a tax on capital that pays for public provision to workers once physical and human capital are complements; the stake here is the same, and what it fixes is a point between two factor-augmenting directions. What the classical tradition states as a condition of accumulation becomes a coefficient in capital’s own choice of technique.

Section 2 describes the menu of technologies and the first-order approximation that makes the produced-factor economy solvable. Section 3 sets out the economy: the two classes, the three stocks and who chooses what. Section 4 shows that under Cobb–Douglas everyone wants the same technology and that away from it every agent’s direction obeys the one rule. Section 5 computes the owner’s and the worker’s weights, ranks them, and finds the private share at which the worker crosses the market. Section 6 does the same for the planner and the two states. Section 7 takes substitution to its limit. Section 8 carries the rankings below unit elasticity. Section 9 compares the approximation with the exact economy. Section 10 lets wages as well as capital income fill the fund, and shows that the state’s technology follows its tax base; it then pays part of the fund in cash, which moves capital, the worker and both states toward capital’s technology. Section 11 draws the policy reading. Appendix A contains the lemmas behind the first-order model and the algebra behind Propositions 3, 8, 9, 10, 11 and 12.

# 2. Two results and one frontier

**(i) The face and its tangent.** The economy is set out in Section 3. Under a constant-elasticity technology with $\sigma\neq1$ no value function affine in the logged states solves either class’s Bellman equation, except at $\sigma=\infty$ (Lemma A.1). What does close is the expansion of the two logged income composites to first order about a reference effective capital–labour ratio $\bar x$ (Lemma A.3): the composite that pays capital, $\ln s_K+\psi$, has slope $a_1=\rho+(1-\rho)s_K$ in $x$, and the composite that pays labour, $\ln s_L+\psi$, has slope $a_2=(1-\rho)s_K$, with $\rho=1-1/\sigma$ and $s_K$ capital’s share at $\bar x$. Two identities carry everything below:
$$1-a_1 \;=\; \frac{s_L}{\sigma}, \qquad a_1-a_2 \;=\; \rho \;=\; 1-\frac{1}{\sigma}. \tag{2.1}$$
At $\rho=0$ both slopes equal the Cobb–Douglas share $\alpha$ and the face is exact. Lemmas A.1 and A.3 prove them.

**(ii) The share effect.** With production $Y=[\theta_P(BK)^{\rho}+(1-\theta_P)(AL)^{\rho}]^{1/\rho}$, augmenting a factor raises that factor’s share when $\sigma>1$, lowers it when $\sigma<1$ and leaves it unchanged at $\sigma=1$. This is the price effect of Acemoglu (2002), and the term $s_L/\sigma$ below is its footprint.

**(iii) The frontier.** Technology is a pair $(A,B)$ of labour- and capital-augmenting efficiencies on a frontier of the kind the appropriate-technology literature uses (Caselli and Coleman 2006), written here as
$$A^{1/\varepsilon}+B^{1/\varepsilon}=1, \qquad (A,B)=\bigl(d^{\varepsilon},(1-d)^{\varepsilon}\bigr), \qquad d\in(0,1),\ \varepsilon>0, \tag{2.2}$$
so that $\ln A=\varepsilon\ln d$ and $\ln B=\varepsilon\ln(1-d)$. The number $d$ is the *direction*: $d\to1$ is pure labour augmentation, $d\to0$ pure capital augmentation. The parametrisation is chosen because it keeps the logged efficiencies as terms in the instrument alone, which is what keeps the game linear-state; it will turn out that $\varepsilon$ cancels from every direction, so the frontier’s curvature does no work.

Result (i) says where the economy can be solved; (ii) says that the direction moves shares only away from unit elasticity; (iii) supplies the object to be chosen. Sections 4 to 7 put the three together and let a class, a planner or a state do the choosing.

# 3. The model

One period is a generation; both stocks depreciate fully; both classes have logarithmic period objectives. Write $k=\ln K$, $n=\ln L$, $g=\ln G$ for the logged capital stock, worker stock and public stock, $a=\ln A$, $b=\ln B$, and $x=b+k-a-n$ for the logged effective capital–labour ratio. Effective labour is the worker stock; the object reproduced is capacity, not headcount. Constant returns give $\ln Y=a+n+\psi(x)$ with $\psi'(x)=s_K(x)$.

The owner of capital chooses a saving rate $i$; a tax $\tau$ on capital income funds the public stock, which is provided in kind ($\varphi=1$: all of the tax is spent on the stock); and the direction $d$ is chosen by whichever agent the section in question studies.

**Who chooses.** One agent at a time is assigned the direction. The saving rate and the tax are the owner’s; both follow paths that do not respond to the state, and no direction below depends on those paths. Because the direction is chosen afresh each period and no knowledge accumulates from one period’s choice to the next, the assigned agent’s best choice is the same constant at every date and against every such path of the other instruments, and in that sense it is a dominant strategy. The object is therefore the solution of one agent’s problem in a restricted game: one agent, one instrument, a constant policy, with every other instrument on a path that does not respond to the state.

Write the two logged income composites
$$E_C \;=\; \ln s_K(x)+\psi(x)+a+n, \qquad E_W \;=\; \ln s_L(x)+\psi(x)+a+n,$$
capital’s income and labour’s, both logged. The transitions are
$$\begin{aligned}
\ln C_C &= \ln(1-i)+\ln(1-\tau)+E_C, \\
k' &= \ln i+\ln(1-\tau)+E_C, \\
n' &= n+\gamma\theta_R\,(E_W-n)+\gamma(1-\theta_R)\,(g-n), \\
g' &= \ln\tau+E_C,
\end{aligned}$$
with owner felicity $(1-\omega)\ln C_C+\omega k$ and worker felicity $n$. Here $\gamma$ is the elasticity of the reproduction technology and $\theta_R$ the weight of the workers’ own income in it, $1-\theta_R$ the weight of the public stock per worker; $\beta$ is the common discount factor; $\theta_R$ is a different object from the technology’s distribution parameter $\theta_P$. Throughout $\beta,\gamma,\theta_R,\omega\in(0,1)$, $s_K\in(0,1)$ and $\rho\in(0,1)$, the box on which the tangent model is licensed above unit elasticity (Lemma 1). Every sign statement of Sections 4 to 6 is read on that open box, and Section 8 states which of them survive below unit elasticity, and where.

Under the tangent of Section 2(i), and using $x=b+k-a-n$,
$$E_C = \text{const}+a_1 b+a_1 k+(1-a_1)\,a+(1-a_1)\,n, \qquad E_W = \text{const}+a_2 b+a_2 k+(1-a_2)\,a+(1-a_2)\,n. \tag{3.1}$$
Every transition is now affine in $(k,n,g)$ plus terms in $(i,\tau,d)$ alone: the game is linear-state in the sense of Dockner, Jørgensen, Van Long and Sorger (2000, §7.2). Each agent’s value function is affine in $(k,n,g)$ with coefficients solving a linear system — for the owner $(a_k,a_n,a_g)$ and for the worker $(p,q,r)$, the systems solved in Lemma A.3 — and each agent’s preferred direction is a constant, free of the state and of every other agent’s choice. Every ranking below therefore holds against any path of the other instruments that does not respond to the state, and needs no selection among equilibria within that class.

**Lemma 1 (The licensed domain).** *The transition matrix of $(k,n,g)$ under (3.1) has unit row sums, entrywise non-negative entries wherever $a_1,a_2\in[0,1]$, in particular for $\rho\in(0,1]$, and eigenvalues exactly $\{0,1,a_1-D\}$ with $D=\gamma(1-\theta_R)+\gamma\theta_R a_2$ and $|a_1-D|<1$. Hence its spectral radius is one, $\beta\cdot1<1$, and every coefficient below is a convergent discounted sum.*

*Proof.* The $k$- and $g$-rows are both $(a_1,1-a_1,0)$ and the $n$-row is $(\gamma\theta_R a_2,\,1-D,\,\gamma(1-\theta_R))$; each sums to one. Since $a_2\le1$, $D\le\gamma<1$, and $D\ge\gamma(1-\theta_R)>0$; every entry is then a non-negative number, so by Perron–Frobenius the spectral radius is the common row sum. The two equal rows give a zero eigenvalue, the row sums give one, and the trace gives the third. Finally $a_1-D\le1-D<1$ and $a_1-D\ge-D>-1$, so $|a_1-D|<1$. $\square$

**The object.** Substituting (3.1) into any agent’s discounted objective, the terms in the direction collect into
$$\varepsilon\bigl[\,A_i\ln d+B_i\ln(1-d)\,\bigr], \qquad A_i=M_i(1-a_1)+N_i(1-a_2),\quad B_i=M_i a_1+N_i a_2, \tag{3.2}$$
where $M_i$ is agent $i$’s total weight on capital’s composite $E_C$ — through its own felicity where that is capital income, and through $k'$ and $g'$ — and $N_i$ is its total weight on labour’s composite $E_W$, which enters only through $n'$. When $A_i$ and $B_i$ are both positive, as they are for every agent studied here on the box, the payoff (3.2) is strictly concave in $d$ with the unique interior maximiser
$$d_i \;=\; \frac{A_i}{A_i+B_i}, \tag{3.3}$$
and $\varepsilon$ has cancelled. For the owner $M_C=(1-\omega)+\beta(a_k+a_g)$ and $N_C=\beta\gamma\theta_R a_n$; for the worker $M_W=\beta(p+r)$ and $N_W=\beta\gamma\theta_R q$. The output-maximising market point is the direction that maximises $\ln Y=a+n+\psi(x)$ at the current stocks, which is $d_M=s_L$ exactly, since $\psi'=s_K$; this is the share-weighted rule of Kennedy (1964) and the point the frontier literature’s firms choose.

# 4. The deletion, and the decomposition

Section 3’s economy is now read for its direction. Two questions decide it: whether the agents who might choose the technology ever want different points, and, where they do, what separates one agent’s point from another’s.

**Proposition 1 (Deletion).** *At $\rho=0$ — Cobb–Douglas with share $\alpha$ — the owner, the worker, a Pareto planner at every weight, a state maximising its fiscal capacity, a state maximising output, and the market all prefer the same direction, $d=1-\alpha$. They prefer it at every date, whatever the directions at other dates, and against any paths of the saving and tax rates that do not respond to the state, wherever the objectives compared are finite. The same holds when next period’s capital and public stock also carry over the fractions $1-\delta_K$ and $1-\delta_G$ of the current stocks, with $\delta_K,\delta_G\in(0,1]$, and when each logarithm in a period objective is replaced by an isoelastic function of its argument.*

*Proof.* At $\rho=0$, $a_1=a_2=\alpha$, so $A_i=(M_i+N_i)(1-\alpha)$ and $B_i=(M_i+N_i)\alpha$ for every agent whatever its weights, and (3.3) gives $1-\alpha$; the market’s $s_L$ is $1-\alpha$. The extension does not need the face. At $\rho=0$ output is $K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}d^{\varepsilon(1-\alpha)}(1-d)^{\varepsilon\alpha}$ and every income is a fixed share of it, so the direction enters only through a factor that rises below $1-\alpha$ and falls above it, whatever the stocks. With the rates held on their paths, the owner’s consumption and every next-period stock are non-decreasing in current output and in the current stocks, with or without the carried-over fractions, and every period objective is increasing in its variable. Fix the directions at the other dates and replace one period’s direction by $1-\alpha$. That period’s output rises strictly and, by induction on dates, no later stock, output or income falls. Every objective above therefore rises, and strictly, because each rises strictly with that period’s output or with a stock built from it: the owner’s consumption, next period’s worker stock through labour income ($\gamma\theta_R>0$), next period’s public stock ($\tau>0$), and output itself. $\square$

In words, under Cobb–Douglas every income is a fixed share of output and every agent’s stake in the technology is a stake in output; the direction each wants is the direction the market supplies, and there is nothing for classes to disagree about. A model of this kind solved at unit elasticity is therefore silent on the question: not because it answers it in the market’s favour but because its technology cannot pose it.

**Proposition 2 (Decomposition).** *For $\rho\in(0,1)$, and more generally wherever $a_1,a_2\in(0,1)$, every agent’s preferred direction is*
$$d_i \;=\; \frac{s_L}{\sigma}+\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{\sigma}\Bigr)\,w_i, \qquad w_i=\frac{N_i}{M_i+N_i}\in[0,1], \tag{4.1}$$
*the capital-income direction $s_L/\sigma$ plus the substitution gap times agent $i$’s weight on the reproduction of the workforce. The market’s weight is $w_M=s_L$.*

*Proof.* From (3.2)–(3.3), $d_i=(1-a_1)+w_i\,(a_1-a_2)$, and (2.1) gives $1-a_1=s_L/\sigma$ and $a_1-a_2=\rho$. For the market, $s_L=(1-a_1)+\rho s_L$ by (2.1). $\square$

In words, two numbers describe the technology of an economy that produces its factors: the direction that maximises capital income, which every agent shares, and a weight that says how much that agent cares about the income from which the next generation of workers is built. Agents differ only in the weight, and the substitution gap decides how much the difference matters — at unit elasticity it does not matter at all, and at perfect substitutability the weight is the whole direction.

The direction of technical change therefore depends on who chooses it as well as on factor supplies: it is a family of points indexed by whose reproduction the chooser values, and Sections 5 to 7 compute those weights and order them.

# 5. Who wants which technology

The owner’s and the worker’s weights are, after solving the two linear systems (Lemma A.3),
$$w_C \;=\; \frac{s_L}{\sigma}\cdot\frac{\beta\gamma\theta_R}{(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)}, \qquad
w_W \;=\; \frac{\theta_R\,(1-\beta a_1)}{\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta\rho)}. \tag{5.1}$$
The owner’s weight is free of $\omega$; the worker’s is free of $\gamma$, the reproduction elasticity cancelling from her direction.

**Proposition 3 (Ordering and spread).** *For $\rho\in(0,1)$, $w_W>w_C$ on the whole box, so*
$$d_W-d_C \;=\; \Bigl(1-\frac{1}{\sigma}\Bigr)(w_W-w_C) \;>\;0 .$$
*The worker wants the more labour-augmenting technology, the disagreement is zero at $\sigma=1$, and it is the product of a substitution gap and a reproduction-weight gap.*

*Proof.* Write $H=\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta\rho)$ and $\Lambda=(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)$ for the denominators of (5.1), and $Q=(1-\beta a_1)+\beta\gamma\bigl(1-\theta_R(1-a_2)\bigr)$. Then $(w_W-w_C)H\Lambda=(1-\beta)\,\theta_R\,Q$ identically (Appendix A.4). Each of $H$, $\Lambda$ and $Q$ is a sum of positive multiples of one minus a product of numbers in $(0,1)$, so every factor is positive. $\square$

In words, at every elasticity other than one the two classes want different technologies, and what separates them measures two things at once: how far the direction can move shares, and how much more of the workers’ income the worker’s own continuation prices than the owner’s does. How reproduction is paid for does not settle this ranking. The private share moves both weights but leaves their order in place, so the worker stands above the owner at every private share; where each of them stands relative to the market is the separate question of Propositions 4 and 5.

**Proposition 4 (Capital’s direction).** *For $\rho\in(0,1)$,*
$$d_C \;=\; \frac{s_L}{\sigma}\cdot\frac{1-\beta+\beta\gamma}{(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)}, \qquad \frac{s_L}{\sigma}\;<\;d_C\;<\;s_L . \tag{5.2}$$
*Capital’s preferred direction is free of $\omega$; it is strictly more labour-augmenting than the direction that maximises its own income, by $\rho w_C>0$; and it is strictly less labour-augmenting than the market’s, by $\rho(w_C-s_L)<0$. Both bounds are strict on the whole box; the lower is approached as $\gamma\theta_R\to0$ and neither is attained.*

*Proof.* The closed form is (4.1) with (5.1). $w_C>0$ because every factor in (5.1) is positive. $w_C-s_L=-\,s_L\,[(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)]/[(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)]<0$: the numerator of $w_C-s_L$ over the common denominator is $s_L[\beta\gamma\theta_R(1-\rho)-(1-\beta)-\beta\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)]=-s_L[(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)]$, strictly negative. The limits are read off (5.1). $\square$

In words, the owner of capital would, left to his income, point technology at $s_L/\sigma$; he points it further toward labour, by an amount proportional to the weight his own value function puts on the workers’ income — because that income is what the next generation’s workforce is built from, and he hires that workforce. But the correction is bounded: it stops short of the market’s point, because his stake in the workers’ income is a fraction of his stake in his own. The classical presumption that capital’s interest is a labour-saving bias is, on this face, true against the market and false against capital’s own income. Capital wants a technology *less* biased against labour than the one that maximises its income, and *more* biased than the one that maximises output. Galor and Moav (2006) show that capitalists support a tax on capital that funds an in-kind public good for workers once physical and human capital are complements. The same stake in the publicly funded reproduction of the labour capital employs also sets capital’s preferred direction of technical change.

**Proposition 5 (The worker’s threshold).** *For $\rho\in(0,1)$, $w_W-s_L$ has the sign of $\theta_R-\bar\theta$ with*
$$\bar\theta \;=\; \frac{\beta s_L}{s_K(1-\beta)+\beta s_L}\;\in(0,1), \tag{5.3}$$
*free of $\sigma$, $\gamma$ and $\omega$. Hence the worker wants a more labour-augmenting technology than the market if and only if the private share of her reproduction exceeds $\bar\theta$; for $\theta_R<\bar\theta$ both classes prefer a technology more biased toward capital than the output-maximising one, with $d_C<d_W<s_L$; and as $\theta_R\to1$ the worker’s excess over the market tends to $(1-1/\sigma)\,s_K(1-\beta)/(1-\beta\rho)>0$.*

*Proof.* Put $w_W-s_L$ over the positive denominator $\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta\rho)$; the numerator is $\theta_R(1-\beta a_1)-s_L\beta(1-\theta_R)-s_L\theta_R(1-\beta\rho)$, which is linear in $\theta_R$ with coefficient $s_K(1-\beta)+\beta s_L>0$ and intercept $-\beta s_L$, hence equals $[s_K(1-\beta)+\beta s_L](\theta_R-\bar\theta)$. That $\bar\theta\in(0,1)$ follows from $1-\bar\theta=s_K(1-\beta)/[s_K(1-\beta)+\beta s_L]>0$. The ordering $d_C<d_W$ is Proposition 3 and $d_W<s_L$ is $w_W<s_L$; the limit is direct substitution. $\square$

In words, the working class’s interest in labour-augmenting technology is a property of how its reproduction is paid for. When workers reproduce their class out of their own income, they want technology pointed at labour, and more so than the market. When the public stock that reproduces them is built out of capital income, their interest in that income grows with the public share, and once the private share falls below $\bar\theta$ it dominates. The class whose members are made productive by labour-augmenting technology wants less of it than the market would supply, because the fund that makes the next generation of workers is a claim on capital’s income. The threshold rises with patience and with labour’s share, and where it lies is a matter of calibration rather than sign. At a discount factor of one half $\bar\theta$ equals labour’s share exactly, and it exceeds labour’s share when the discount factor exceeds one half. A period is a generation, so an annual discount factor between 0.96 and 0.98 puts $\beta$ between about 0.36 and 0.60 over twenty-five years, and with labour’s share at two thirds $\bar\theta$ then lies between about 0.53 and 0.75. The share $s_K$ in (5.3) is capital’s share at the reference ratio $\bar x$, and at a stationary state that ratio itself depends on $\theta_R$; the comparison of $\theta_R$ with $\bar\theta$ is therefore read at the shares of the state about which the tangent is taken.

The obvious answer of Section 1 — labour wants labour-augmenting technology — is the special case $\theta_R>\bar\theta$. Where the reproduction of the workforce is largely public, both classes sit on the capital side of the output-maximising technology and disagree about distance, not direction. To be sure, the two classes still disagree: Proposition 3 puts the worker strictly above the owner for every $\theta_R>0$. What closes is the disagreement with the market’s *sign*.

# 6. The state

Three agents may hold the direction on behalf of the economy, and they do not in general want the same point.

**Proposition 6 (The planner).** *A planner maximising $\lambda V_W+(1-\lambda)V_C$ has weight*
$$w_\lambda \;=\; \frac{\lambda N_W+(1-\lambda)N_C}{\lambda(M_W+N_W)+(1-\lambda)(M_C+N_C)},$$
*a mediant of $w_C$ and $w_W$: it equals $w_C$ at $\lambda=0$, $w_W$ at $\lambda=1$, and is strictly increasing in $\lambda$, with $\partial w_\lambda/\partial\lambda=(w_W-w_C)\,T_WT_C/[\lambda T_W+(1-\lambda)T_C]^2>0$ where $T_i=M_i+N_i>0$. Hence $d_C\le d(\lambda)\le d_W$ for every $\lambda\in[0,1]$.*

*Proof.* The planner’s payoff in $d$ is the $\lambda$-weighted sum of the two classes’ payoffs (3.2), whose value-function coefficients do not depend on $d$; the mediant form follows, the endpoints by substitution, and the derivative by differentiating and using Proposition 3. $\square$

A state with its own objective is a different matter, and which objective decides everything.

**Proposition 8 (The state under its own objectives).** *(i) A state whose period objective is the logged fund it administers, $\ln G_t$, has weight $w_S=w_C$ identically: it wants exactly capital’s technology. More generally, every agent whose period objective combines, with non-negative weights not all zero, capital’s logged income $E_C$ and the logged capital and public stocks $k$ and $g$, plus terms in the instruments alone has the weight $w_C$ of (5.1). The owner at every $\omega$ and this state are two such agents. (ii) A state whose period objective is logged output, $\ln Y_t$, has weight $w_Y=(s_L+N_Y)/(1+M_Y+N_Y)$, a closed form of the same shape as (5.1), and $w_C<w_Y<s_L$ on the whole box: the output-maximising state, looking forward, wants a technology more biased toward capital than the point that maximises output today, and less so than capital itself.*

*Proof.* (i) For any agent whose felicity is $\phi E_C$ plus non-negative multiples of $k$ and $g$ and terms in the instruments, the $n$-row of its coefficient system reads $a_n=\phi(1-a_1)+\beta[a_n(1-D)+(\text{weight on }E_C)(1-a_1)]$ with $\phi$ its felicity weight on $E_C$, so $M=\phi+\beta(a_k+a_g)=a_n[1-\beta(1-D)]/(1-a_1)$ and the ratio $N/M=\beta\gamma\theta_R(1-a_1)/[1-\beta(1-D)]$ is the same for every such agent; it is (5.1)’s $w_C$, and it is why $\omega$ is absent from it. The fund-valuing state is such an agent with $\phi=0$ and felicity on $g$. (ii) The direct term of $\ln Y$ carries the market’s loadings $(s_L,s_K)$, hence weight $s_L$ on the worker composite with total weight one; the continuation carries $(M_Y,N_Y)$ from the system with felicity loadings $(s_K,s_L,0)$ on $(k,n,g)$. Solving that system gives (6.1). With $J>0$ the denominator of $w_Y$ in (6.1) below and $\Lambda$ and $Q$ as in the proof of Proposition 3, $(w_Y-s_L)J=\beta s_L\bigl[\gamma\bigl(\theta_R(\beta s_L+s_K)-\beta\bigr)-(1-\beta)s_K\bigr]$, whose bracket rises with $\theta_R$ and equals $-(1-\beta)(1-\gamma)s_K<0$ at $\theta_R=1$; and $(w_Y-w_C)J\Lambda=(1-\beta)\,s_L\,(1-\beta+\beta\gamma)\,Q>0$ (Appendix A.5). $\square$

In words, each objective a state might hold picks its own point on the frontier. A state that lives on the tax it levies on capital wants what capital wants, exactly, because its stake in the workforce runs through the same income; a state that maximises output over time wants less labour-augmenting technology than the output-maximising point, because capital income is what next period’s output is built from, but more than capital wants, because output is also built from workers. Where it sits relative to the worker depends on how the workforce is reproduced, and the answer is exact. The treasury’s alignment with capital comes from its tax base: Section 10 fills the fund partly from wages and moves the treasury toward the worker in step.

**Proposition 9 (The output state against the worker).** *For $\rho\in(0,1)$ the output state’s weight is*
$$w_Y \;=\; \frac{s_L\bigl[(1-\beta)(1-\beta a_1)+\beta\gamma\bigl((1-\beta)+\theta_R(1-\rho)(\beta s_L+s_K)\bigr)\bigr]}{(1-\beta)(1-\beta\rho s_L)+\beta\gamma\bigl(1-\rho\theta_R(\beta s_L+s_K)\bigr)}, \tag{6.1}$$
*and $w_Y-w_W$ has the sign of $\theta_Y-\theta_R$, where*
$$\theta_Y \;=\; \frac{\beta s_L}{\beta s_L+s_K}\;<\;\bar\theta . \tag{6.2}$$
*Hence the directions are completely ordered on the whole box by the private share of reproduction: for $\theta_R<\theta_Y$, $d_C<d_W<d_Y<s_L$; for $\theta_Y<\theta_R<\bar\theta$, $d_C<d_Y<d_W<s_L$; for $\theta_R>\bar\theta$, $d_C<d_Y<s_L<d_W$. The fiscal state sits at $d_C$ throughout and the planner between $d_C$ and $d_W$. Both thresholds are free of $\sigma$, $\gamma$ and $\omega$.*

*Proof.* Write $J$ for the denominator of (6.1), $H=\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta\rho)$ for the denominator of $w_W$ in (5.1), and $Q=(1-\beta a_1)+\beta\gamma\bigl(1-\theta_R(1-a_2)\bigr)$. Then
$$(w_Y-w_W)\,H\,J \;=\; (1-\beta)(\beta s_L+s_K)(\theta_Y-\theta_R)\,Q$$
identically in the parameters. Each of $H$, $J$ and $Q$ is a sum of positive multiples of terms of the form one minus a product of numbers in $(0,1)$, so all three are positive on the box, and the sign follows. Moreover $\bar\theta-\theta_Y=\beta^2s_Ls_K/\bigl[(s_K(1-\beta)+\beta s_L)(s_K+\beta s_L)\bigr]>0$. The three orderings combine this with Propositions 3, 5 and 8, and every direction inherits its weight’s order through (4.1). The closed form and the identity are derived in Appendix A.5 and A.6. $\square$

In words, the working class ranks against a state that maximises output on the same object that ranks it against the market: how much of its reproduction it pays for itself. When the private share is low, the worker’s stake in capital income, which reaches her through the fund, is large enough that she wants a technology more biased toward capital than the output state does. The two thresholds are one odds condition read twice: $\theta_R/(1-\theta_R)=\beta s_L/s_K$ for the output state, and the same with $s_K$ discounted by $1-\beta$ for the market. Thus the worker overtakes the output state before she overtakes the market, and the distance between the two thresholds is the discounting of capital’s share.

# 7. Perfect substitutes

At perfect substitutability the tangent of Section 2(i) is exact, so the directions below are the exact economy’s own.

**Proposition 7 (Perfect substitutes).** *At $\rho=1$ the tangent model is exact. The owner’s coefficient on the worker stock is $a_n=0$, so $w_C=0$. Capital’s payoff (3.2) in the direction reduces to $\varepsilon B_C\ln(1-d)$, which is strictly decreasing, so it has no maximiser on $(0,1)$. Its preference runs to the limit $d\to0$, pure capital augmentation, a corner of the frontier and not the interior point (3.3). The worker’s direction is*
$$d_W \;=\; w_W \;=\; \frac{\theta_R(1-\beta)}{\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta)},$$
*interior for $\theta_R<1$ and tending to one as $\theta_R\to1$: as the public fund’s weight in her reproduction vanishes the worker’s point approaches pure labour augmentation, and the fund pulls her toward capital’s technology by exactly its weight in her continuation.*

*Proof.* At $\rho=1$ factor incomes are linear in the factors, $s_KY=\theta_PBK$, so nothing the owner’s programme reads depends on $n$ and the $a_n$-equation of the system forces $a_n=0$ (Lemma A.2); then $N_C=0$, so $A_C=M_C(1-a_1)+N_C(1-a_2)=0$ at $a_1=1$. For the worker, $a_1=1$ and $a_2=0$ in (5.1) give the display; $1-w_W=\beta(1-\theta_R)/[\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta)]$ is positive for $\theta_R<1$ and tends to zero as $\theta_R\to1$. $\square$

In words, when capital and labour are perfect substitutes capital’s income does not depend on the workforce at all, so capital has no stake in the workers’ reproduction and points technology entirely at itself.

At the far end of the elasticity line the two classes are as far apart as the frontier allows on capital’s side and as far apart as the fund allows on labour’s. Capital’s weight on the workers’ income has vanished, so what separates the two classes is the worker’s weight alone, and it is here that the fund’s pull on her is visible in one line.

# 8. Below unit elasticity

The rankings of Sections 5 and 6 are proved above unit elasticity, and they carry to a region below it. Below one the slopes $a_1,a_2$ stay in $(0,1)$ only where $s_K\in(1-\sigma,\sigma)$, which is empty for $\sigma\le1/2$. The proof of Lemma 1 as written, and every sign argument below, use that. On the region $\tfrac12<\sigma<1$, $s_K\in(1-\sigma,\sigma)$, the order of the weights survives: $w_W>w_C$ (Proposition 3), $0<w_C<s_L$ (Proposition 4), $w_W-s_L$ has the sign of $\theta_R-\bar\theta$ (Proposition 5), $w_S=w_C$ identically (Proposition 8(i)), $w_\lambda$ between $w_C$ and $w_W$ with $T_W,T_C>0$ (Proposition 6), $w_C<w_Y<s_L$ (Proposition 8(ii)), and $w_Y-w_W$ has the sign of $\theta_Y-\theta_R$ with $\theta_Y<\bar\theta$ (Proposition 9), both thresholds as in (5.3) and (6.2). By (4.1) every difference between two directions, including an agent’s distance from the market and from capital’s income direction, is $1-1/\sigma$ times a difference of weights, and $1-1/\sigma<0$ on the region. Thus every order of directions reverses: $d_W<d_C$, $d_W\le d(\lambda)\le d_C$, $s_L<d_Y<d_C<s_L/\sigma$, and the worker wants a technology less labour-augmenting than the market’s exactly when $\theta_R>\bar\theta$. Each of these orders holds on the whole region, together with $A_i,B_i>0$ and the entries of Lemma 1.[^2] The share effect of Section 2(ii) reverses at the same elasticity, so the reversal leaves unchanged which agent wants the technology that, at given stocks, gives labour the larger share: read in shares, every ranking of Sections 5 and 6 holds on the region as it does above one.

# 9. The first-order model and the exact economy

Between unit elasticity and perfect substitutability the exact model has no affine value function (Lemma A.1), so Propositions 2–6, 8 and 9 are exact statements about the first-order model of Section 2(i), while Propositions 1 and 7 are exact. The tangent model reaches the exact economy in two ways. First, at a stationary state the two models share their first-order conditions. Take a constant policy $(i,\tau,d)$ and start the exact economy at stocks whose ratios that policy keeps constant, at the ratio $x^*$. Along that path every derivative the payoff gradient carries is a slope of (3.1) at $\bar x=x^*$, and the costate is constant and equal to the tangent model’s value coefficients, so the gradient of every agent’s exact payoff in $(i,\tau,d)$ is the tangent model’s at $\bar x=x^*$ (Lemma A.7). Hence where capital’s self-consistent choice — a constant policy that is optimal from the stationary state it produces — is interior, its exact direction is (5.2) at $s_K(x^*)$. At that state, with capital’s rates held, the worker’s exact payoff has at capital’s direction the slope in $d$ of her tangent payoff, whose sign is that of $d_W-d_C$ in the tangent model, positive by Proposition 3. So the exact sign of $d_W-d_C$ there is the tangent sign unless the worker’s exact payoff has more than one peak in $d$. The same holds for a fully state-contingent programme that has an optimal policy, at a rest point where that policy’s choice is interior. That choice, held constant, attains the programme’s value, so it is a self-consistent choice in the sense just given, and for capital the direction is (5.2) at $s_K(x^*)$ (Lemma A.7(iii)). Second, numerically, the constant-policy maximisation on the exact reduction, at the cell $\beta=0.9$, $\gamma=0.5$, $\theta_R=0.4$, $\theta_P=0.3$ and $\omega=0.2$, with the linear frontier $\varepsilon=1$, gives $d_W-d_C=0.051$, $0.065$ and $0.073$ at the self-consistent ratio for $\sigma=2,3,4$, each with a single peak, and a positive sign from the non-stationary start $k-n=g-n=0$ as well. At $\sigma=6$ and $\sigma=8$ capital’s self-consistent choice is a corner: pure capital augmentation, a zero tax and a capital share of one, the endpoint at which its preferred tax is zero. Labour’s share is zero at those states, outside the box on which Propositions 4 and 5 are stated; the sign of $d_W-d_C$ there is again positive ($0.052$, $0.056$).

Two things that cancel or simplify in the tangent model do not in the exact one. The frontier’s curvature moves the exact directions of the two classes, though not their order at the curvatures computed: at $\sigma=2$ the self-consistent $d_W$ is $0.584$, $0.601$ and $0.623$ at $\varepsilon=0.5$, $1$ and $2$. And the market has a unique output-maximising direction exactly when $\varepsilon(1-1/\sigma)<1$, which holds at every elasticity below one and, on the linear frontier, at every finite elasticity. That direction is labour’s share at the ratio its own choice produces, not at the current ratio: at $\sigma=2$ and $\varepsilon=1$ it is $0.991$, against $s_L(x^*)=0.920$. When $\varepsilon(1-1/\sigma)>1$ the direction equal to labour’s share minimises output, which rises towards both ends of the frontier, so no direction maximises it; when $\varepsilon(1-1/\sigma)=1$ output is monotone in $d$ unless $\theta_P K^{\rho}=(1-\theta_P)L^{\rho}$, in which case every direction yields the same output. The order $d_C<d_W<d_M$ holds in every interior case computed above one on the linear frontier; the distance to the market does not carry over.

Below unit elasticity, at the same cell and $\sigma=0.6$, $0.75$ and $0.9$, capital’s self-consistent choice is interior. Its share $s_K(x^*)$ is $0.584$, $0.482$ and $0.372$, inside $(1-\sigma,\sigma)$, so the stationary state that capital’s own policy produces lies in the region of Section 8. There $d_W-d_C$ is $-0.023$, $-0.017$ and $-0.008$, with a single peak in the worker’s payoff, the same sign holds from the non-stationary start, and the exact order is $d_M<d_W<d_C$ at all three, the reverse of the order above one.

# 10. Who pays for the fund

Sections 5 and 6 fill the public stock from capital income alone, and the answers to who wants which technology turned on that fund. This section lets both incomes pay and finds that the order of the classes survives, while the state’s technology follows whatever income fills its fund. It then returns to the capital-income fund and pays part of it to the workers in cash rather than in kind: capital, the treasury, the worker and the output state all move toward capital’s technology, and the order among them stays as in Proposition 9.

Let a tax at rate $\tau$ on capital income and one at rate $\tau_L$ on labour income finance the stock, $G'=\tau e^{E_C}+\tau_L e^{E_W}$, and let the workers’ own input to rearing be their income net of the tax on it. Write $\eta$ for capital income’s share of the fund’s revenue at the state about which the tangent is taken: $\eta=1$ is the fund of Section 3, a proportional tax on output gives $\eta=s_K$, and $\eta=0$ is a fund built from wages alone. Both rates follow paths that do not respond to the state. To first order
$$g'=\text{const}+\eta E_C+(1-\eta)E_W,$$
plus terms in the two rates, so the game stays linear-state and the statements below are about this first-order model, as are those of Sections 4 to 6. The $g$-row of the matrix of Lemma 1 becomes $\eta$ times its $k$-row plus $1-\eta$ times $(a_2,1-a_2,0)$, so the matrix keeps non-negative entries and unit row sums and every coefficient remains a convergent discounted sum. One number carries the change. Write
$$\tilde\theta_R\;=\;\theta_R+\beta(1-\eta)(1-\theta_R)$$
for the weight of wages in rearing the next generation: the workers’ own income, plus the part of the public stock that wages finance, which reaches rearing one period later. At $\eta=1$, $\tilde\theta_R=\theta_R$.

**Proposition 10 (Who pays, and the classes).** *For $\rho\in(0,1)$ and every $\eta\in[0,1]$: (i) every agent whose period objective combines capital’s logged income and the logged capital stock, the owner at every $\omega$ among them, has the weight $w_C$ of (5.1) with $\theta_R$ replaced by $\tilde\theta_R$; it falls strictly as $\eta$ rises, and $s_L/\sigma<d_C<s_L$. (ii) The worker’s weight is $w_W=\tilde\theta_R(1-\beta a_1)/H_\eta$, with $H_\eta=\beta(1-\theta_R)\bigl(1-\beta\rho(1-\eta)\bigr)+\theta_R(1-\beta\rho)$, and $w_W>w_C$: the worker wants the more labour-augmenting technology, and the planner of Proposition 6 lies between the classes. (iii) $w_W-s_L$ has the sign of $\theta_R-\bar\theta(\eta)$, where*
$$\bar\theta(\eta)\;=\;\frac{\beta\bigl[\eta(1-\beta s_K)-(1-\beta)s_K\bigr]}{\beta\eta(1-\beta s_K)+(1-\beta)^2s_K}, \tag{10.1}$$
*which is (5.3) at $\eta=1$, rises strictly with $\eta$, is free of $\sigma$, $\gamma$ and $\omega$, and is positive exactly when $\eta>\eta^*=(1-\beta)s_K/(1-\beta s_K)$, a share below $s_K$; as $\theta_R\to1$ the worker’s excess over the market tends to the limit of Proposition 5 at every $\eta$.*

In words, who pays for the fund moves every agent’s point and leaves the order of the classes in place. A fund filled more by wages raises capital’s weight on the workers’ income, because wages then build part of the public stock that rears the workers capital will hire; capital still wants a technology less labour-augmenting than the worker’s and than the market’s. The worker’s lean toward capital’s technology needs two things at once: a public share of her reproduction above $1-\bar\theta(\eta)$, and a fund filled enough by capital income. Once wages carry more than $1-\eta^*$ of the fund, $\bar\theta(\eta)$ is not positive and she wants a more labour-augmenting technology than the market at every private share of her reproduction. Under a proportional tax on output her threshold is $\beta^2s_L/(1-\beta+\beta^2s_L)$, below the $\bar\theta$ of (5.3).

**Proposition 11 (Who pays, and the state).** *For $\rho\in(0,1)$ and every $\eta\in[0,1]$: (i) the state whose period objective is the logged fund has the weight $w_S=w_W-(1-\beta)\eta\theta_RQ_\eta/(H_\eta S_\eta)$, with $Q_\eta,S_\eta>0$ given in Appendix A.8. It falls strictly as $\eta$ rises, from $w_S=w_W$ at $\eta=0$ to $w_S=w_C$ at $\eta=1$, and lies strictly between the two for $\eta\in(0,1)$. At $\eta=0$ every agent whose period objective combines labour’s logged income and the logged worker and public stocks has the weight $w_W=(1-\beta a_1)/(1-\beta\rho)$. (ii) The output state has $w_C<w_Y<s_L$; $w_Y-w_W$ has the sign of $\theta_Y(\eta)-\theta_R$, where*
$$\theta_Y(\eta)\;=\;\frac{\beta(\eta-s_K)}{(1-\beta)s_K+\beta\eta}, \tag{10.2}$$
*which is (6.2) at $\eta=1$ and lies below $\bar\theta(\eta)$ for $\eta>0$; and $w_Y-w_S$ has the sign of $\eta-s_K$.*

In words, a state that values the fund it administers wants the technology of the income that fills it: capital’s when capital income pays, the worker’s when wages pay, and, exactly when the fund is a proportional tax on output, the technology the output-maximising state wants. Proposition 8(i) is the end of this line at which capital income pays, so the treasury there wants capital’s technology because capital’s income is its base. The worker ranks against the output state as before, on her private share, now against a threshold that falls with the wage share of the fund; below $\eta=s_K$ that threshold is negative and she wants a more labour-augmenting technology than the output state at every private share.

On the region of Section 8 the orders of the weights in Propositions 10 and 11 survive, with the same thresholds (10.1) and (10.2), and by (4.1) every order of the directions reverses, as it does there: each factor of Appendix A.8 keeps its sign on that region.

The fund of Section 3 is spent wholly on provision in kind. Return to that fund, $\eta=1$, and let the state spend a share $\varphi\in[0,1]$ of the capital tax on the public stock and pay the rest to the workers as cash, which joins their wages in rearing. Then $g'=\ln\varphi+\ln\tau+E_C$, and labour’s income is the share
$$\kappa_W\;=\;s_L+(1-\varphi)\tau s_K$$
of output, which falls as $\varphi$ rises. The constant $\ln\varphi$ moves no coefficient. Labour’s composite is now the logarithm of $\kappa_WY$, and to first order it loads capital and its augmentation with $\tilde a_2=\bigl(s_La_2+(1-\varphi)\tau s_Ka_1\bigr)/\kappa_W$, a weighted mean of $a_1$ and $a_2$, in place of $a_2$. The matrix of Lemma 1 keeps its form with $\tilde a_2$ in place of $a_2$, and every coefficient remains a convergent discounted sum. One number carries the change, the gap between the slopes of the two composites,
$$\Delta\;=\;a_1-\tilde a_2\;=\;\frac{\rho\,s_L}{\kappa_W},$$
which is $\rho$ at $\varphi=1$ and shrinks as cash replaces provision. By (3.2)–(3.3) every direction becomes $d_i=s_L/\sigma+\Delta\,w_i$, and the market’s point stays $d_M=s_L$.

**Proposition 12 (Cash or kind).** *Let the fund be financed from capital income, $\eta=1$, with $\tau\in(0,1)$, and let $\rho\in(0,1)$. For every $\varphi\in[0,1]$: (i) every agent of Proposition 8(i), the owner and the fiscal state among them, has the direction*
$$d_C\;=\;\frac{s_L}{\sigma}\cdot\frac{1-\beta+\beta\gamma}{(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R\Delta)}, \tag{10.3}$$
*which is (5.2) at $\varphi=1$ and rises strictly with $\varphi$, with $s_L/\sigma<d_C<s_L$. (ii) The worker’s direction exceeds $d_C$ and rises strictly with $\varphi$, and the planner of Proposition 6 lies between the two. $d_W-s_L$ has the sign of $\theta_R-\bar\theta(\varphi)$, where*
$$\bar\theta(\varphi)\;=\;\frac{\beta\kappa_W}{1-\beta s_K-(1-\beta)\kappa_W}, \tag{10.4}$$
*which is (5.3) at $\varphi=1$, is free of $\sigma$, $\gamma$ and $\omega$, falls strictly as $\varphi$ rises, and is below one exactly when $(1-\varphi)\tau<1-\beta$. (iii) The output state has $d_C<d_Y<s_L$, its direction rises strictly with $\varphi$, and $d_Y-d_W$ has the sign of $\theta_Y(\varphi)-\theta_R$, where*
$$\theta_Y(\varphi)\;=\;\frac{\beta\kappa_W}{1-(1-\beta)\kappa_W}, \tag{10.5}$$
*which is (6.2) at $\varphi=1$, falls strictly as $\varphi$ rises, and lies below $\bar\theta(\varphi)$ and below one. Hence the ordering of Proposition 9 holds at every $\varphi$ with (10.4) and (10.5) in place of (5.3) and (6.2).*

In words, a cash transfer out of the capital tax is a share of capital’s income in the workers’ hands. A technology that raises capital’s share then raises the workers’ income as well, so the income that raises the next workforce leans less on labour’s share, and every stake in that workforce pulls less toward labour. Paying the fund in cash moves capital and the treasury that shares its weight, the worker and the output state toward capital’s technology, and it raises both of the worker’s thresholds. At a discount factor of one half, labour’s share at two thirds and a tax of 0.3, $\bar\theta$ rises from 0.667 when the fund is spent wholly on provision to 0.754 when half of it is paid in cash and 0.852 when all of it is, and $\theta_Y$ rises from 0.500 to 0.558 and 0.622. The order among the agents stays as in Proposition 9; what grows is the range of private shares over which the worker sits on capital’s side of the market and of the output state. Once the cash paid out reaches the fraction $1-\beta$ of capital’s income, no private share of her reproduction makes the worker want more labour bias than the market. Provision in kind is the form of the fund at which capital, the worker and both states want the most labour-augmenting technology.

# 11. Conclusion

The frontier literature answers the question of which way technology should go with a point, the one that maximises output, and the induced-innovation tradition answers it with a bias, capital’s. Once the workforce has to be produced, each answer is one case of a single rule: take the direction that maximises capital income and correct it by the substitution gap times the chooser’s stake in the incomes that raise the next generation.

Capital’s weight is positive because capital hires the workers that workers’ income raises, and smaller than the market’s because that stake is a fraction of its stake in its own income. Measured by what a technology does to labour’s share, capital favours capital relative to the market and favours labour relative to what its own income alone would dictate. The worker’s stake turns on who pays for her class’s upbringing. Above the threshold private share she asks for more labour bias than the market; below it she joins capital on the market’s capital side, since her class’s upbringing is then paid out of capital’s income. A treasury financed by the capital tax wants what capital wants, a state that values output sits between capital and the market, a planner sits between the classes, and one number, the private share of the workforce’s reproduction, orders capital, the worker, the two states and the market against two thresholds set by patience and the factor shares. Paying the fund in cash rather than in kind moves capital, the worker and both states toward capital’s technology and raises both thresholds, so the range of private shares over which the worker sides with capital grows.

Three kinds of model give three answers. A model of this economy solved at unit elasticity cannot pose the question, because every agent wants the same technology there. A model with endowed factors and class incomes, such as that of Acemoglu, Gitmez and Shadmehr (2026), poses it and finds the political reading: labour for labour, capital for capital. An economy that produces its workforce reverses that reading for the working class whenever enough of its reproduction is paid for out of capital’s income, and it makes the stance of a state depend on what the state maximises: a treasury wants capital’s technology without representing capital.

The results are proved in an economy in which one period is a generation, both stocks depreciate fully and objectives are logarithmic. Cash in place of provision is treated with a fund raised from capital income, the worker values the size of her class in effective labour, the direction is chosen afresh each generation, and the saving rate and the tax follow paths that do not respond to the state. The Cobb–Douglas agreement also holds with partial depreciation and isoelastic objectives. Between unit elasticity and perfect substitution the rankings are theorems of the first-order model, which the exact economy shares at steady states. Below unit elasticity they are proved, for a fund spent in kind, where capital’s share lies between one minus the elasticity and the elasticity; beyond that region the orderings would rest on sign arguments that do not use slopes between zero and one. Four further extensions would each take an argument of its own. A state-contingent rule for saving or the tax makes the transitions nonlinear in the stocks. A per-head objective for the worker changes her weight, and the threshold with it. A fund that taxes wages and also pays cash hands part of labour’s own tax back to it, so combining Propositions 10 and 12 needs a rule for that return. A stock of knowledge in place of a flow keeps the model linear-state with a unit root and turns the question into one about balanced growth (Acemoglu 2003).

Public provision for the next generation of workers, paid for by taxing capital, is a transfer, and in this economy it also moves the working class’s view of technology. Past the point where the fund pays for enough of that upbringing, a point fixed by patience and the factor shares, workers and owners both want technique more favourable to capital than the market would choose, and what remains between them is a quarrel about degree. The old observation that capital needs the working class to be reproduced becomes, through a single coefficient, a prediction about what each class wants the machines to do.

# Appendix A. Proofs

Sections A.1–A.3 prove the three results about the economy that the body uses, at $\varphi=1$ and with the direction $d$ as an instrument. The appendix then supplies the algebra behind Propositions 3, 8(ii) and 9, and A.7 shows that at a stationary state the tangent model and the exact model share their first-order conditions. Throughout, $x=b+k-a-n$, the production function is that of Section 2(ii), and a conjectured value function is written $V=v+\mu_k k+\mu_n n+\mu_g g$.

**A.1. No affine value function off the two closure points.**

**Lemma A.1.** *Let $\rho<1$ and $\rho\neq0$. At any fixed direction, no function affine in $(k,n,g)$ solves the exact Bellman equation of the owner, or of the worker, identically in the states. With the direction chosen, none does on any set of states on which the maximising direction is interior and satisfies the second-order condition strictly.*

*Proof.* Substitute the exact transitions of Section 3 into felicity plus $\beta V$. Apart from affine terms in the states and additive terms in the instruments, the states enter only through the two composites $\ln s_K(x)+\psi(x)$ and $\ln s_L(x)+\psi(x)$, with coefficients $M$ and $N$: for the owner $M=(1-\omega)+\beta(\mu_k+\mu_g)$ and $N=\beta\gamma\theta_R\mu_n$; for the worker $M=\beta(\mu_k+\mu_g)$ and the same $N$. Write $\Phi(x)=M(\ln s_K+\psi)+N(\ln s_L+\psi)$. Since $\psi'=s_K$ and $s_K'=\rho s_Ks_L$, the two composites have slopes $\rho+(1-\rho)s_K$ and $(1-\rho)s_K$ and the common second derivative $\rho(1-\rho)s_Ks_L$, so
$$\Phi''(x)=\rho(1-\rho)\,s_K s_L\,(M+N).$$
If $M+N\neq0$, $\Phi$ is not affine in $x$, hence not affine in $k$, and no affine $V$ satisfies the equation at a fixed direction. When the direction is chosen, write $z=k-n$, so that $x=z+\varepsilon\ln[(1-d)/d]$ and the terms in $d$ are $\Phi(x)+(M+N)\varepsilon\ln d$. At an interior maximiser the first-order condition is $\Phi'(x^*)=(M+N)(1-d^*)$; where the second-order condition is strict, the implicit function theorem makes $d^*$ and $x^*$ differentiable in $z$, and the envelope theorem gives $W''(z)=\Phi''(x^*)\,\partial x^*/\partial z$ for the maximised value $W$. That derivative cannot vanish: differentiating the first-order condition, $\partial x^*/\partial z=0$ forces $\partial d^*/\partial z=0$, and then $\partial x^*/\partial z=1$. So $W$ is not affine either. It remains to show that $M+N=0$ is impossible. Suppose it holds. Then $\Phi=M(\ln s_K-\ln s_L)=M\bigl[\ln\tfrac{\theta_P}{1-\theta_P}+\rho x\bigr]$ is affine, and the coefficients must match.

For the owner, matching $k$, $n$ and $g$ gives $\mu_k=\omega+\rho M$, $\mu_n=(1-\rho)M+\beta\mu_n\bigl(1-\gamma(1-\theta_R)\bigr)$ and $\mu_g=\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)\mu_n$. With $M=-N=-\beta\gamma\theta_R\mu_n$ the second equation reads $\mu_n\bigl[1-\beta+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)+(1-\rho)\beta\gamma\theta_R\bigr]=0$, and the bracket is positive for $\rho<1$. So $\mu_n=0$, hence $M=0$ and $\mu_g=0$, hence $\mu_k=\omega$ and $M=(1-\omega)+\beta\omega>0$, a contradiction.

For the worker, matching gives $\mu_k=\rho M$, $\mu_n=1+(1-\rho)M+\beta\mu_n\bigl(1-\gamma(1-\theta_R)\bigr)$ and $\mu_g=\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)\mu_n$. With $M=-\beta\gamma\theta_R\mu_n$ the second equation reads $\mu_n\bigl[1-\beta+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)+(1-\rho)\beta\gamma\theta_R\bigr]=1$, so $\mu_n>0$ and $M<0$. But $M=\beta(\mu_k+\mu_g)=\beta\rho M+\beta^2\gamma(1-\theta_R)\mu_n$ gives $M(1-\beta\rho)=\beta^2\gamma(1-\theta_R)\mu_n>0$, and $1-\beta\rho>0$, so $M>0$, a contradiction. Since $s_Ks_L>0$, $\Phi''\neq0$ whenever $\rho(1-\rho)\neq0$. $\square$

At $\rho=0$ both composites are affine with slope $\alpha$. At $\rho=1$ factor incomes are linear in the factors, $s_KY=\theta_PBK$ and $s_LY=(1-\theta_P)AL$, so $\ln s_K+\psi=x+\ln\theta_P$ and $\ln s_L+\psi=\ln(1-\theta_P)$ are both affine. These are the only two points at which the exact model closes.

**A.2. Perfect substitutes.**

**Lemma A.2.** *At $\rho=1$ the tangent model is exact, with $a_1=1$ and $a_2=0$, and the owner’s coefficient on the worker stock is $a_n=0$.*

*Proof.* At $\rho=1$, $E_C=\ln\theta_P+b+k$, so the owner’s felicity, $k'$ and $g'$ are free of $n$ and of $a$, and (3.1) holds exactly with $a_1=1$, $a_2=0$. **A.3. The coefficient systems.**

**Lemma A.3.** *Under (3.1) with $a_1\in(0,1]$ and $a_2\in[0,1)$, and with $D=\gamma(1-\theta_R)+\gamma\theta_Ra_2$ as in Lemma 1, the owner’s and the worker’s value-function coefficients solve*
$$\begin{aligned}
a_k&=\omega+M_Ca_1+\beta a_n\gamma\theta_Ra_2, &\qquad p&=\beta\bigl[(p+r)a_1+q\gamma\theta_Ra_2\bigr],\\
a_n&=M_C(1-a_1)+\beta a_n(1-D), &\qquad q&=1+\beta\bigl[(p+r)(1-a_1)+q(1-D)\bigr],\\
a_g&=\beta a_n\gamma(1-\theta_R), &\qquad r&=\beta q\gamma(1-\theta_R),
\end{aligned}$$
*with $M_C=(1-\omega)+\beta(a_k+a_g)$. Each system has a unique solution, in which*
$$M_C=\frac{(1-\omega+\beta\omega)(1-\beta+\beta D)}{(1-\beta)(1-\beta a_1+\beta D)},\qquad a_n=\frac{M_C(1-a_1)}{1-\beta+\beta D},\qquad q=\frac{1-\beta a_1}{(1-\beta)(1-\beta a_1+\beta D)},\qquad p+r=\frac{\beta qD}{1-\beta a_1},$$
*with $M_C$, $q$ and $p+r$ positive and $a_n>0$ except at $a_1=1$, where $a_n=0$; and the weights of (4.1) are those of (5.1).*

*Proof.* The systems are the coefficient matching of Lemma A.1 with the two composites replaced by their tangents (3.1), the direction entering only through terms in the instruments. For the owner, adding the $k$- and $g$-equations gives $a_k+a_g=\omega+M_Ca_1+\beta a_nD$, so $M_C(1-\beta a_1)=1-\omega+\beta\omega+\beta^2a_nD$; the $n$-equation gives $a_n(1-\beta+\beta D)=M_C(1-a_1)$; eliminating $a_n$ gives $M_C$. For the worker, adding the $p$- and $r$-equations gives $(p+r)(1-\beta a_1)=\beta qD$, and substituting into the $q$-equation gives $q$. Every denominator is positive when $a_1\in(0,1]$ and $a_2\in[0,1)$, since $1-\beta a_1\ge1-\beta>0$ and $D>0$, and $a_n$ vanishes exactly at $a_1=1$. By (3.2), $M_C$ and $N_C=\beta\gamma\theta_Ra_n$ give
$$w_C=\frac{N_C}{M_C+N_C}=\frac{\beta\gamma\theta_R(1-a_1)}{1-\beta+\beta D+\beta\gamma\theta_R(1-a_1)}=\frac{\beta\gamma\theta_R\,s_L/\sigma}{(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)},$$
using $\beta D+\beta\gamma\theta_R(1-a_1)=\beta\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)$ and (2.1). For the worker, $M_W=\beta(p+r)$ and $N_W=\beta\gamma\theta_Rq$ give $N_W/M_W=\gamma\theta_R(1-\beta a_1)/(\beta D)$, and dividing through by $\gamma$,
$$w_W=\frac{\theta_R(1-\beta a_1)}{\beta(1-\theta_R+\theta_Ra_2)+\theta_R(1-\beta a_1)}=\frac{\theta_R(1-\beta a_1)}{\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta\rho)},$$
using $a_1-a_2=\rho$. That these coefficients are the discounted sums of the agents’ objectives is Lemma 1. $\square$

**A.5. Proposition 8(ii).** Under (3.1), $\ln Y=\text{const}+s_L a+s_K b+s_K k+s_L n$, and the output state’s coefficients $(y_k,y_n,y_g)$ solve the system of Lemma A.3 with the owner’s felicity loadings replaced by $(s_K,s_L,0)$:

**A.7. The tangent model at a stationary state.**

**Lemma A.7.** *Let $\rho\in(0,1)$. In the exact model of Section 3, let a constant policy $u=(i,\tau,d)\in(0,1)^3$ be followed from stocks whose ratios $k-n$ and $g-n$ that policy keeps constant, so that $x_t=x^*$ at every date, and let the agent be any of those of Sections 4 to 6. (i) The agent’s exact payoff is continuously differentiable in $u$, and its gradient equals the gradient in $u$ of the agent’s payoff in the tangent model with $\bar x=x^*$. (ii) If $u$ maximises the owner’s exact payoff over constant policies from those stocks, it satisfies the tangent model’s first-order conditions at $\bar x=x^*$, and its direction is (5.2) at $s_K(x^*)$. (iii) If instead, with any instrument the agent does not choose held constant, an optimal state-contingent policy of the agent’s is interior at those stocks and its choice there keeps the ratios constant, then that choice, held constant, attains the agent’s exact value from those stocks. It therefore satisfies the tangent model’s first-order conditions at $\bar x=x^*$, and for the owner its direction is (5.2) at $s_K(x^*)$. The vector $\lambda^*$ of tangent value coefficients at $\bar x=x^*$ satisfies $V(s)\ge V(s^*)+\lambda^*\cdot(s-s^*)+o(|s-s^*|)$, where $V$ is the agent’s exact value and $s^*$ those stocks, so it is the gradient of $V$ there wherever $V$ is differentiable.*

*Proof.* Write $s=(k,n,g)$, $\mathbf 1=(1,1,1)$, $F(s,u)$ for the agent’s period objective and $f(s,u)$ for the exact transitions. Two facts carry the proof. First, adding $c$ to all three logged stocks leaves $x$ unchanged and adds $c$ to $k'$, $n'$, $g'$, and adds a fixed multiple $\kappa c$ to every period objective of Sections 4 to 6: $(1-\omega)\ln C_C+\omega k$, $n$, $g$, $\ln Y$ and their non-negative combinations. Second, apart from terms linear in the stocks, the stocks and the direction enter only through $E_C$ and $E_W$, and by the slopes computed in Lemma A.1, $\partial E_C/\partial(k,n)=(a_1,1-a_1)$ and $\partial E_C/\partial d=a_1\,b'(d)+(1-a_1)\,a'(d)$, with $a_2$ in place of $a_1$ for $E_W$, where $a_1=\rho+(1-\rho)s_K(x)$ and $a_2=(1-\rho)s_K(x)$ are the slopes of Section 2(i) at the current $x$; and $\ln Y=a+n+\psi(x)$ has slopes $(s_K,s_L)$ in $(k,n)$ and $s_La'(d)+s_Kb'(d)$ in $d$, those of A.5 with $\bar x=x$. So the partial derivatives $F_s$, $f_s$, $F_u$ and $f_u$ depend on the stocks only through $x$ and at $x$ are the tangent model’s with $\bar x=x$. In particular $f_s$ is the matrix of Lemma 1 with $\bar x=x$, which has non-negative entries and unit row sums at every $x$.

1.  By the chain rule, summed by parts, $\partial U/\partial u=\sum_{t\ge0}\beta^t\,[F_u(s_t,u)+\beta f_u(s_t,u)^{\top}\lambda_{t+1}]$ with $\lambda_t=F_s(s_t,u)+\beta f_s(s_t,u)^{\top}\lambda_{t+1}$, taken as its bounded forward sum. Because every $f_s$ has non-negative entries and unit row sums, the exact map is non-expansive in the largest-coordinate norm. So, for $u$ in a compact subset of the interior, the stocks stay within a distance linear in $t$ of the given path and their derivatives in $u$ grow at most linearly in $t$; the payoff and the series above converge uniformly, and $U$ is continuously differentiable. On the given path $s_{t+1}=s_t+\mu\mathbf 1$ for a constant $\mu$ and $x_t=x^*$, so every coefficient is constant and $\lambda_t=\lambda^*$, the solution of $\lambda^*=F_s+\beta f_s^{\top}\lambda^*$ at $x^*$. The solution is unique, because $\det(I-\beta f_s^{\top})=(1-\beta)[1-\beta(a_1-D)]>0$ by Lemma 1. That equation is the coefficient matching of the tangent model at $\bar x=x^*$ — for the owner and the worker, the systems of Lemma A.3 — so $\lambda^*$ is the vector of tangent value coefficients, and $\partial U/\partial u=[F_u+\beta f_u^{\top}\lambda^*]/(1-\beta)$ at $x^*$, which is the gradient of the tangent payoff at $\bar x=x^*$.

2.  An interior maximiser has a zero gradient, so by (i) it satisfies the tangent first-order conditions at $\bar x=x^*$. The one in $d$ is the first-order condition of (3.2), whose unique solution is (3.3), that is (5.2) at $s_K(x^*)$.

3.  Let $V$ be the agent’s exact value, $s^*$ the stocks and $u^*$ the policy’s choice there, so that the next stocks are $s^*+\mu\mathbf 1$. $V$ is finite. Every augmentation factor is at most one and $\rho>0$, so $\ln Y\le\max(k,n)$; hence $E_C$, $E_W$, $k'$ and $g'$ are at most $\max(k,n)$, $n'$ is a convex combination of $n$, $E_W$ and $g$, the largest of the three logged stocks never rises, and every period objective is at most $\kappa\max(k_0,n_0,g_0)$, so $V$ is bounded above; and $V(s^*)$ is at least the finite payoff of $u^*$ held constant. By the first fact, raising all three initial stocks by $c$ raises the payoff of every sequence of choices by $\kappa c/(1-\beta)$, so $V(s+c\mathbf 1)=V(s)+\kappa c/(1-\beta)$, and $F(s,u)+\beta V(f(s,u))-V(s)$ is unchanged when $s$ rises by $c\mathbf 1$. The first choice of an optimal policy satisfies $V(s^*)=F(s^*,u^*)+\beta V(f(s^*,u^*))$, because what the policy does from the next stocks on is optimal from them. So the same equation holds with the same $u^*$ at every $s_t=s^*+t\mu\mathbf 1$, which is the path of $u^*$ held constant. Summing it over $t<T$ gives $V(s^*)=\sum_{t<T}\beta^tF(s_t,u^*)+\beta^TV(s_T)$, and $\beta^TV(s_T)=\beta^T[V(s^*)+\kappa\mu T/(1-\beta)]\to0$. Hence the payoff of $u^*$ held constant is $V(s^*)$, which is at least the payoff of every constant policy from $s^*$: $u^*$ is an interior maximiser over constant policies, so its gradient in the agent’s instruments is zero, which by (i) gives the tangent first-order conditions at $\bar x=x^*$; for the owner, (ii) applies. As in (i), the payoff of $u^*$ held constant is differentiable in the initial stocks, because the derivatives of later stocks in the initial ones are products of matrices with non-negative entries and unit row sums; at $s^*$ its gradient is $\lambda^*$. It lies below $V$ and equals it at $s^*$, so $V(s)\ge V(s^*)+\lambda^*\cdot(s-s^*)+o(|s-s^*|)$, and where $V$ is differentiable at $s^*$ its gradient there is $\lambda^*$. $\square$

With $Q_\eta=(1-\beta a_1)+\beta\gamma\bigl[(1-\theta_R)\bigl(1-\beta\rho(1-\eta)\bigr)+\theta_Ra_2\bigr]$, a direct calculation gives $(w_W-w_C)H_\eta\Lambda_\eta=(1-\beta)\tilde\theta_RQ_\eta$, and both classes’ $T_i=M_i+N_i$ are positive, so Proposition 6’s mediant holds at every $\eta$. $$(w_W-s_L)H_\eta=(1-\beta)s_K\tilde\theta_R-\beta\eta s_L(1-\theta_R)=K_\eta\bigl(\theta_R-\bar\theta(\eta)\bigr),\qquad K_\eta=(1-\beta)s_K\bigl(1-\beta(1-\eta)\bigr)+\beta\eta s_L,$$
which is free of $\rho$ and $\gamma$. The numerator of (10.1) vanishes at $\eta^*$, with $s_K-\eta^*=\beta s_Ks_L/(1-\beta s_K)>0$; $\partial\bar\theta/\partial\eta$ times the square of the denominator of (10.1) is $\beta(1-\beta)s_K(1-\beta s_K)>0$; and at $\theta_R=1$, $w_W-s_L=(1-\beta)s_K/(1-\beta\rho)$ at every $\eta$.

*The fiscal state.* With $f_g=1$ and $S_\eta=(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\eta\rho\theta_R)-\beta\rho(1-\eta)(1-\beta+\beta\gamma)$, a direct calculation gives
$$(w_S-w_C)\Lambda_\eta S_\eta=(1-\beta)(1-\eta)(1-\beta+\beta\gamma)Q_\eta,\qquad (w_W-w_S)H_\eta S_\eta=(1-\beta)\eta\theta_RQ_\eta,$$
$$\frac{\partial w_S}{\partial\eta}\,S_\eta^2=-(1-\beta+\beta\gamma)\Bigl[(1-\beta)(1-\beta a_1)+\beta\gamma\bigl((1-\theta_R)(1-\beta a_1)+\theta_R(1-\beta)(1-\rho)s_K\bigr)\Bigr].$$
$S_\eta$ is affine in $\eta$, with $S_1=\Lambda_1$ and $S_0=(1-\beta+\beta\gamma)(1-\beta\rho)$. At $\eta=0$, if $f_C=f_k=0$, then $M=\beta v_k$ and the $k$-equation give $M(1-\beta a_1)=\beta a_2N$, so $w=(1-\beta a_1)/(1-\beta a_1+\beta a_2)=(1-\beta a_1)/(1-\beta\rho)$ whatever $f_W$, $f_n$ and $f_g$; this is $w_W$ at $\eta=0$.

*The output state.* The system of A.5 under the fund of Section 10 gives $w_Y=(s_L+N_Y)/(1+M_Y+N_Y)$, and with $J_\eta=(1-\beta)(1-\beta\rho s_L)+\beta\gamma\bigl[1-\rho\bigl(\theta_R(\beta s_L+s_K)+\beta(1-\theta_R)(1-\eta)\bigr)\bigr]$ a direct calculation gives
$$(w_Y-s_L)J_\eta=-\beta s_L\bigl[\beta\gamma\eta(1-\theta_R)+(1-\beta)s_K(1-\gamma\theta_R)\bigr],\qquad (w_Y-w_C)J_\eta\Lambda_\eta=(1-\beta)s_L(1-\beta+\beta\gamma)Q_\eta,$$
$$(w_Y-w_W)H_\eta J_\eta=(1-\beta)\bigl[(1-\beta)s_K+\beta\eta\bigr]\bigl(\theta_Y(\eta)-\theta_R\bigr)Q_\eta,\qquad (w_Y-w_S)S_\eta J_\eta=(1-\beta)(1-\beta+\beta\gamma)(\eta-s_K)Q_\eta,$$
and $\bar\theta(\eta)-\theta_Y(\eta)=\beta^2\eta s_Ls_K/\bigl([(1-\beta)s_K+\beta\eta][\beta\eta(1-\beta s_K)+(1-\beta)^2s_K]\bigr)$.

*Signs.* Whenever $a_1,a_2\in(0,1)$ and $\rho<1$, $H_\eta$, $\Lambda_\eta$, $Q_\eta$, $K_\eta$ and the bracket in $\partial w_S/\partial\eta$ are sums of positive terms. $S_\eta$ is positive: for $\rho\in(0,1)$ it is affine in $\eta$ with positive values at both ends, and for $\rho<0$ every term is positive. $J_\eta$ is positive because $\theta_R(\beta s_L+s_K)+\beta(1-\theta_R)(1-\eta)=1-\bigl[(1-\theta_R)\bigl(1-\beta(1-\eta)\bigr)+\theta_R(1-\beta)s_L\bigr]<1$, so its bracket exceeds $1-\rho$ for $\rho\in(0,1)$ and exceeds one for $\rho<0$. Every sign in Propositions 10 and 11 follows, on the box and on the region of Section 8, and at $\eta=1$ every display reduces to its counterpart in A.3–A.6.

**A.9. Proposition 12.** Under the split of Section 10, labour’s income is $\kappa_WY$ and its logged composite has slopes $\tilde a_2=\bigl(s_La_2+(1-\varphi)\tau s_Ka_1\bigr)/\kappa_W$ in $(b,k)$ and $1-\tilde a_2$ in $(a,n)$, so $a_1-\tilde a_2=s_L(a_1-a_2)/\kappa_W=\Delta$, and $\ln\varphi$ enters $g'$ as a constant. Since $\tilde a_2$ lies between $a_2$ and $a_1$, it lies in $(0,1)$, and Lemma A.3, the argument of Proposition 8(i) and the system of A.5 hold with $\tilde a_2$ in place of $a_2$. By (3.2)–(3.3), $d_i=(1-a_1)+\Delta w_i$ with $1-a_1=s_L/\sigma$, while $a_1=s_K+\rho s_L$ and $\Delta\kappa_W=\rho s_L$. Write $\Lambda_\varphi=(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R\Delta)$, $H_\varphi=\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta\Delta)$, $Q_\varphi=(1-\beta a_1)+\beta\gamma\bigl(1-\theta_R(1-\tilde a_2)\bigr)$ and $D_\varphi=1-\beta s_K-(1-\beta)\kappa_W$. The first three are positive as in A.4, and $D_\varphi-\beta s_L=(1-\beta)(1-\kappa_W)\ge0$. Throughout, $\partial\kappa_W/\partial\varphi=-\tau s_K<0$, so $\Delta$ rises strictly with $\varphi$.

*Capital’s class.* Lemma A.3 gives $w_C=\beta\gamma\theta_R(1-a_1)/\Lambda_\varphi$, using $\beta D+\beta\gamma\theta_R(1-a_1)=\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R\Delta)$, and $d_C=s_L/\sigma+\Delta w_C$ is (10.3). Its denominator falls as $\Delta$ rises, so $d_C$ rises strictly with $\varphi$; $w_C>0$ gives $d_C>s_L/\sigma$, and $d_C$ is at most its value at $\varphi=1$, which is below $s_L$ by Proposition 4. The argument of Proposition 8(i) uses only the matrix of Lemma 1 and the loadings on $E_C$, $k$ and $g$, so every agent of that class has the weight $w_C$.

*The worker.* Lemma A.3 gives $w_W=\theta_R(1-\beta a_1)/H_\varphi$, so $d_W=s_L/\sigma+\Delta\,\theta_R(1-\beta a_1)/H_\varphi$, whose derivative in $\Delta$ is $\theta_R(1-\beta a_1)\bigl(\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R\bigr)/H_\varphi^2>0$; hence $d_W$ rises strictly with $\varphi$. The identity of A.4 becomes $(w_W-w_C)H_\varphi\Lambda_\varphi=(1-\beta)\theta_RQ_\varphi$, so $d_W-d_C=\Delta(w_W-w_C)>0$; both classes’ $T_i$ are positive by Lemma A.3, so Proposition 6’s mediant holds. Against the market, $s_L=s_L/\sigma+\Delta\kappa_W$ gives $d_W-s_L=\Delta(w_W-\kappa_W)$, and
$$(w_W-\kappa_W)H_\varphi=\theta_R\bigl(1-\beta a_1-(1-\beta)\kappa_W+\beta\rho s_L\bigr)-\beta\kappa_W=D_\varphi\bigl(\theta_R-\bar\theta(\varphi)\bigr).$$
Further, $\partial\bar\theta/\partial\kappa_W\cdot D_\varphi^2=\beta(1-\beta s_K)>0$ and $1-\bar\theta(\varphi)=s_K\bigl(1-\beta-(1-\varphi)\tau\bigr)/D_\varphi$.

*The output state.* The direct term $s_La+s_Kb$ of $\ln Y$ does not depend on $\varphi$; written on the two composites it puts weight $(a_1-s_K)/\Delta=\kappa_W$ on labour’s and $1-\kappa_W$ on capital’s. The system of A.5 with $\tilde a_2$ then gives $w_Y=(\kappa_W+N_Y)/(1+M_Y+N_Y)$ and $d_Y=s_L/\sigma+\Delta w_Y$. With $u=1/\kappa_W-(1-\beta)$, $m=\beta\gamma+(1-\beta)s_K$, $\ell=(1-\beta)(1-\beta\rho s_L)+\beta\gamma$, $c=\gamma\theta_Rs_L$ and $J_\varphi=\ell-\beta\rho cu$, a direct calculation gives
$$(w_Y-\kappa_W)J_\varphi=-\beta\kappa_W(m-cu),\qquad d_Y-s_L=-\beta\rho s_L\,\frac{m-cu}{\ell-\beta\rho cu}.$$
Since $s_L\le\kappa_W<1$, $s_Lu\le1-(1-\beta)\kappa_W\le\beta s_L+s_K<1$, so $J_\varphi>0$ and $m-cu>m-\gamma(\beta s_L+s_K)=(1-\beta)(1-\gamma)s_K>0$: $d_Y<s_L$. The derivative in $u$ of the ratio, times $(\ell-\beta\rho cu)^2$, is $c(\beta\rho m-\ell)=-c(1-\beta\rho)(1-\beta+\beta\gamma)<0$, and $u$ rises strictly with $\varphi$, so $d_Y$ rises strictly with $\varphi$. Further
$$(w_Y-w_C)J_\varphi\Lambda_\varphi=(1-\beta)\kappa_W(1-\beta+\beta\gamma)Q_\varphi,\qquad (w_Y-w_W)H_\varphi J_\varphi=(1-\beta)\bigl(1-(1-\beta)\kappa_W\bigr)\bigl(\theta_Y(\varphi)-\theta_R\bigr)Q_\varphi,$$
so $d_C<d_Y$, and $d_Y-d_W=\Delta(w_Y-w_W)$ has the sign of $\theta_Y(\varphi)-\theta_R$. Finally $\partial\theta_Y/\partial\kappa_W\cdot\bigl(1-(1-\beta)\kappa_W\bigr)^2=\beta$; $1-\theta_Y(\varphi)=(1-\kappa_W)/\bigl(1-(1-\beta)\kappa_W\bigr)>0$ because $\kappa_W<1$ when $\tau<1$; and $\bar\theta(\varphi)-\theta_Y(\varphi)=\beta^2\kappa_Ws_K/\bigl[\bigl(1-(1-\beta)\kappa_W\bigr)D_\varphi\bigr]>0$. At $\varphi=1$, $\kappa_W=s_L$, $\Delta=\rho$ and $\tilde a_2=a_2$, and every display reduces to its counterpart in A.3–A.6.

Every identity in this appendix becomes a polynomial identity once the displayed denominators are cleared.
