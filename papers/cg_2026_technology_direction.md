# Whose Technology? The Direction of Technical Change When the Workforce Is Produced
Carlos Galindo Escajeda
2026-09-01

# Abstract

Which way should technology go — toward making labour more productive, or capital? In the theory of directed and appropriate technical change the answer is a point on a technology frontier chosen by firms given the factors they have, and with an elasticity of substitution above one it favours the abundant factor. This paper puts the same frontier into a two-class economy in which the factors are not given but produced: capital is accumulated from capital income, and the workforce is reproduced from worker income and a public stock built out of a tax on capital. On the tractable face of that economy — one period a generation, full depreciation, logarithmic objectives, and the first-order model in which the constant-elasticity technology closes — every agent’s preferred point on the frontier has one closed form: the direction that maximises capital income, $s_L/\sigma$, plus the substitution gap $1-1/\sigma$ times the weight that agent’s own optimisation places on the reproduction of the workforce. The market’s weight is labour’s share. Capital’s is strictly positive and strictly below it, so capital always wants a technology more labour-augmenting than its own income would dictate and less than the market supplies. The worker’s weight crosses the market’s at an explicit threshold on the private share of her reproduction, $\bar\theta=\beta s_L/[\beta s_L+(1-\beta)s_K]$: below it both classes want a technology more biased toward capital than the output-maximising one, and the class conflict is over how far, not which way. A Pareto planner sits between the two classes at every weight; a state that values the fund it administers chooses exactly capital’s technology, as an identity, because any agent whose objective carries no direct weight on the workforce has capital’s weight; and a state that values output is a fourth point, always below the market. Under Cobb–Douglas all six directions coincide, so the question this paper asks does not exist in the technology every neighbouring model uses. The mechanism is the one the classical tradition states as a condition — that the maintenance and reproduction of the working class is a condition of the reproduction of capital — here obtained as a weight in capital’s own choice of technique.

# 1. Introduction

If a class could choose the technology of the economy it lives in, which would it choose? The obvious answer is that workers would make labour more productive and capitalists would make capital more productive, and that the market, weighing the two by their shares, would land somewhere between. In the literature on directed and appropriate technical change the market’s answer is derived rather than assumed: firms choose factor-augmenting efficiencies from a frontier, and the point they choose is the one that maximises output given the factors the economy has (Caselli and Coleman 2006; León-Ledesma and Satchi 2019; Acemoglu 2002). The classes never appear, because in that literature the factors are endowments and nobody’s interest in them goes beyond their price.

This paper shows that the obvious answer is wrong once the factors are produced, and shows exactly how. It puts the same technology frontier into a two-class economy in which capital is accumulated out of capital income and the workforce is reproduced out of worker income and a public stock financed by a tax on capital — the economy of a companion paper on reproduction under capital-biased technical change (Galindo 2026), taken here verbatim with one new primitive. In that economy every agent who might choose the technology — the owner of capital, the working class, a Pareto planner, a state with its own objective, and the output-maximising market — has a preferred point on the frontier with one closed form: the direction that maximises capital income, plus the substitution gap $1-1/\sigma$ times the weight that agent places on the reproduction of the workforce. The market’s weight is labour’s share. The owner’s is positive, so capital wants a more labour-augmenting technology than its own income would dictate, because the workforce it hires next generation is built from the workers’ income this generation; and it is strictly below the market’s, so the correction never carries capital past the output-maximising point. The worker’s weight is where the surprise lies. It falls with the public share of her reproduction, because the public stock is built from capital income, and it crosses the market’s weight at a threshold that depends on the discount factor and the factor shares and on nothing else. Below that threshold the working class wants a technology more biased toward capital than the market would supply — and so, always, does capital. The conflict between the classes is then over how far to go in capital’s direction, not over which direction to go.

The result belongs to the classical tradition, and this paper says so rather than dressing it in the frontier literature’s clothes. The line from Kennedy (1964) through Shah and Desai (1981), Foley (2003), Duménil and Lévy (2003) and Julius (2005) — surveyed by Tavani and Zamparelli (2017) — reads the direction of technical change as induced by distribution, and reads a labour-saving bias as capital’s response to labour’s strength. The model here is a member of the analytical strand of that tradition: classes are defined by what they own, the method is game-theoretic, and no value accounting is used. What it adds is one object the induced-innovation models do not carry, because in them, as in the frontier models, labour is not produced: the weight each class places on the reproduction of the workforce. That weight is what the tradition states as a condition — “the maintenance and reproduction of the working class is, and must ever be, a necessary condition to the reproduction of capital” (Marx, *Capital* I, ch. 23) — and here it is not a condition but a coefficient in capital’s own choice of technique. Nothing in the reproduction technology below is attributed to that tradition; its law of population is a different object, and the map from income to numbers used here is a primitive of this model and of its source.

Three things should be said plainly before the model. First, the tractable face is a linear-state game in the sense of Dockner, Jørgensen, Van Long and Sorger (2000, §7.2): every transition is affine in the logged states plus terms in the instruments alone, so each agent’s preferred direction is a constant, free of the state and of every other agent’s choice, and the “conflict” is a disagreement over a level and not a strategic interaction. That is the known degeneracy of the class and is cited, not claimed; the contribution is the economics carried on top of it. Second, off Cobb–Douglas the exact model has no affine value function (Galindo 2026, Proposition 1), and the results here are theorems of the first-order model that paper builds — exact at unit elasticity and at perfect substitutability, first-order between, with first-order conditions that coincide with the exact model’s at the self-consistent stationary ratio. Section 8 states that scope in full and does not soften it. Third, under Cobb–Douglas the question does not exist: all six directions coincide (Proposition 1). The neighbouring models’ technology is not a simplification of the question; it is a deletion of it.

# 2. Two results taken as given, and one frontier

**(i) The face and its tangent.** The economy is that of Galindo (2026), Section 3, restated in Section 3 below. Under a constant-elasticity technology with $\sigma\neq1$ no value function affine in the logged states solves either class’s Bellman equation, except at $\sigma=\infty$ (its Proposition 1). What does close is the expansion of the two logged income composites to first order about a reference effective capital–labour ratio $\bar x$ (its Proposition 3): the composite that pays capital, $\ln s_K+\psi$, has slope $a_1=\rho+(1-\rho)s_K$ in $x$, and the composite that pays labour, $\ln s_L+\psi$, has slope $a_2=(1-\rho)s_K$, with $\rho=1-1/\sigma$ and $s_K$ capital’s share at $\bar x$. Two identities carry everything below:
$$1-a_1 \;=\; \frac{s_L}{\sigma}, \qquad a_1-a_2 \;=\; \rho \;=\; 1-\frac{1}{\sigma}. \tag{2.1}$$
At $\rho=0$ both slopes equal the Cobb–Douglas share $\alpha$ and the face is exact.

**(ii) The share effect.** With production $Y=[\theta_P(BK)^{\rho}+(1-\theta_P)(AL)^{\rho}]^{1/\rho}$, augmenting a factor raises that factor’s share when $\sigma>1$, lowers it when $\sigma<1$ and leaves it unchanged at $\sigma=1$. This is the price effect of Acemoglu (2002) and is standard; the term $s_L/\sigma$ below is its footprint, and nothing about it is claimed here.

**(iii) The frontier.** Technology is a pair $(A,B)$ of labour- and capital-augmenting efficiencies on a frontier of the kind the appropriate-technology literature uses (Caselli and Coleman 2006), written here as
$$A^{1/\varepsilon}+B^{1/\varepsilon}=1, \qquad (A,B)=\bigl(d^{\varepsilon},(1-d)^{\varepsilon}\bigr), \qquad d\in(0,1),\ \varepsilon>0, \tag{2.2}$$
so that $\ln A=\varepsilon\ln d$ and $\ln B=\varepsilon\ln(1-d)$. The number $d$ is the *direction*: $d\to1$ is pure labour augmentation, $d\to0$ pure capital augmentation. The parametrisation is chosen because it keeps the logged efficiencies as terms in the instrument alone, which is what keeps the game linear-state; it will turn out that $\varepsilon$ cancels from every direction, so the frontier’s curvature does no work.

*So what.* Result (i) says where the economy can be solved; (ii) says the direction moves shares only away from unit elasticity; (iii) supplies an object to choose. Everything below is what happens when the three are put together and the choosers are classes.

# 3. The model

One period is a generation; both stocks depreciate fully; both classes have logarithmic period objectives. Write $k=\ln K$, $n=\ln N_W$, $g=\ln G$ for the logged capital stock, worker stock and public stock, $a=\ln A$, $b=\ln B$, and $x=b+k-a-n$ for the logged effective capital–labour ratio. Effective labour is the worker stock; the object reproduced is capacity, not headcount. Constant returns give $\ln Y=a+n+\psi(x)$ with $\psi'(x)=s_K(x)$.

The owner of capital chooses a saving rate $i$; a tax $\tau$ on capital income funds the public stock, which is provided in kind ($\varphi=1$, the provision corner of the source model); and the direction $d$ is chosen by whichever agent the section in question studies. Write the two logged income composites
$$E_C \;=\; \ln s_K(x)+\psi(x)+a+n, \qquad E_W \;=\; \ln s_L(x)+\psi(x)+a+n,$$
capital’s income and labour’s, both logged. The transitions are
$$\begin{aligned}
\ln C_C &= \ln(1-i)+\ln(1-\tau)+E_C, \\
k' &= \ln i+\ln(1-\tau)+E_C, \\
n' &= n+\gamma\theta_R\,(E_W-n)+\gamma(1-\theta_R)\,(g-n), \\
g' &= \ln\tau+E_C,
\end{aligned}$$
with owner felicity $(1-\omega)\ln C_C+\omega k$ and worker felicity $n$. Here $\gamma$ is the elasticity of the reproduction technology and $\theta_R$ the weight of the workers’ own income in it, $1-\theta_R$ the weight of the public stock per worker; $\beta$ is the common discount factor; $\theta_R$ is a different object from the technology’s distribution parameter $\theta_P$. Throughout $\beta,\gamma,\theta_R,\omega\in(0,1)$, $s_K\in(0,1)$ and $\rho\in(0,1)$, the box on which the tangent model is licensed (Lemma 1); every sign statement is read on that open box and nowhere else.

Under the tangent of Section 2(i), and using $x=b+k-a-n$,
$$E_C = \text{const}+a_1 b+a_1 k+(1-a_1)\,a+(1-a_1)\,n, \qquad E_W = \text{const}+a_2 b+a_2 k+(1-a_2)\,a+(1-a_2)\,n. \tag{3.1}$$
Every transition is now affine in $(k,n,g)$ plus terms in $(i,\tau,d)$ alone: the game is linear-state. Each agent’s value function is affine in $(k,n,g)$ with coefficients solving a linear system — for the owner $(a_k,a_n,a_g)$ and for the worker $(p,q,r)$, the systems of Galindo (2026), Proposition 3 — and each agent’s preferred direction is a constant.

**Lemma 1 (The licensed domain).** *The transition matrix of $(k,n,g)$ under (3.1) has unit row sums, entrywise non-negative entries for $\rho\in(0,1]$, and eigenvalues exactly $\{0,1,a_1-D\}$ with $D=\gamma(1-\theta_R)+\gamma\theta_R a_2$ and $|a_1-D|<1$. Hence its spectral radius is one, $\beta\cdot1<1$, and every coefficient below is a convergent discounted sum.*

*Proof.* The $k$- and $g$-rows are both $(a_1,1-a_1,0)$ and the $n$-row is $(\gamma\theta_R a_2,\,1-D,\,\gamma(1-\theta_R))$; each sums to one. Each entry is a product or complement of numbers in $(0,1)$, hence non-negative, so by Perron–Frobenius the spectral radius is the common row sum. The two equal rows give a zero eigenvalue, the row sums give one, and the trace gives the third. $|a_1-D|<1$ is the pair of inequalities $1\mp(a_1-D)>0$, each a polynomial with positive coefficients after the substitution $x\mapsto s/(1+s)$ of every parameter. $\square$

**The object.** Substituting (3.1) into any agent’s discounted objective, the terms in the direction collect into
$$\varepsilon\bigl[\,A_i\ln d+B_i\ln(1-d)\,\bigr], \qquad A_i=M_i(1-a_1)+N_i(1-a_2),\quad B_i=M_i a_1+N_i a_2, \tag{3.2}$$
where $M_i$ is agent $i$’s total weight on capital’s composite $E_C$ — through its own felicity where that is capital income, and through $k'$ and $g'$ — and $N_i$ is its total weight on labour’s composite $E_W$, which enters only through $n'$. The payoff (3.2) is strictly concave in $d$ with the unique interior maximiser
$$d_i \;=\; \frac{A_i}{A_i+B_i}, \tag{3.3}$$
and $\varepsilon$ has cancelled. For the owner $M_C=(1-\omega)+\beta(a_k+a_g)$ and $N_C=\beta\gamma\theta_R a_n$; for the worker $M_W=\beta(p+r)$ and $N_W=\beta\gamma\theta_R q$. The output-maximising market point is the direction that maximises $\ln Y=a+n+\psi(x)$ at the current stocks, which is $d_M=s_L$ exactly, since $\psi'=s_K$; this is the share-weighted rule of Kennedy (1964) and the point the frontier literature’s firms choose.

# 4. The deletion, and the decomposition

**Proposition 1 (Deletion).** *At $\rho=0$ — Cobb–Douglas with share $\alpha$ — the owner, the worker, a Pareto planner at every weight, a state maximising its fiscal capacity, a state maximising output, and the market all prefer the same direction, $d=1-\alpha$.*

*Proof.* At $\rho=0$, $a_1=a_2=\alpha$, so $A_i=(M_i+N_i)(1-\alpha)$ and $B_i=(M_i+N_i)\alpha$ for every agent whatever its weights, and (3.3) gives $1-\alpha$; the market’s $s_L$ is $1-\alpha$. $\square$

In words, under Cobb–Douglas every income is a fixed share of output and every agent’s stake in the technology is a stake in output; the direction each wants is the direction the market supplies, and there is nothing for classes to disagree about. The result is the reason the models nearest to this one are silent on the question: not because they answer it in the market’s favour but because their technology cannot pose it.

**Proposition 2 (Decomposition).** *For $\rho\in(0,1)$ every agent’s preferred direction is*
$$d_i \;=\; \frac{s_L}{\sigma}+\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{\sigma}\Bigr)\,w_i, \qquad w_i=\frac{N_i}{M_i+N_i}\in[0,1], \tag{4.1}$$
*the capital-income direction $s_L/\sigma$ plus the substitution gap times agent $i$’s weight on the reproduction of the workforce. The market’s weight is $w_M=s_L$.*

*Proof.* From (3.2)–(3.3), $d_i=(1-a_1)+w_i\,(a_1-a_2)$, and (2.1) gives $1-a_1=s_L/\sigma$ and $a_1-a_2=\rho$. For the market, $s_L=(1-a_1)+\rho s_L$ by (2.1). $\square$

In words, two numbers describe the technology of an economy that produces its factors: the direction that maximises capital income, which every agent shares, and a weight that says how much that agent cares about the income from which the next generation of workers is built. Agents differ only in the weight, and the substitution gap decides how much the difference matters — at unit elasticity it does not matter at all, and at perfect substitutability the weight is the whole direction.

*So what.* The direction of technical change stops being a property of factor supplies. It is a family of points indexed by whose reproduction the chooser values, and the rest of the paper is the closed forms of the weights and their order.

# 5. Who wants which technology

The owner’s and the worker’s weights are, after solving the two linear systems,
$$w_C \;=\; \frac{s_L}{\sigma}\cdot\frac{\beta\gamma\theta_R}{(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)}, \qquad
w_W \;=\; \frac{\theta_R\,(1-\beta a_1)}{\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta\rho)}. \tag{5.1}$$
The owner’s weight is free of $\omega$, exactly as his preferred tax is on the face; the worker’s is free of $\gamma$, the reproduction elasticity cancelling from her direction as it does from the fiscal rules of the source model.

**Proposition 3 (Ordering and spread).** *For $\rho\in(0,1)$, $w_W>w_C$ on the whole box, so*
$$d_W-d_C \;=\; \Bigl(1-\frac{1}{\sigma}\Bigr)(w_W-w_C) \;>\;0 .$$
*The worker wants the more labour-augmenting technology, the disagreement is zero at $\sigma=1$, and it is the product of a substitution gap and a reproduction-weight gap.*

*Proof.* $w_W-w_C$ is a rational function whose numerator is $\theta_R(\beta-1)\,[\,\beta\gamma\theta_R(1-a_2)-\beta\gamma+\beta a_1-1\,]$ and whose denominator is the product of $-\,[\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta\rho)]$ and $-\,[(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)]$. The bracket in the numerator is $-[(1-\beta a_1)+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R(1-a_2))]<0$, so the numerator is a product of two negatives and $\theta_R$; the denominator is a product of two negatives. Each factor is decided by the substitution $x\mapsto s/(1+s)$ of every parameter, after which every polynomial has coefficients of one sign. The certificate runs that cover. $\square$

**Proposition 4 (Capital’s direction).** *For $\rho\in(0,1)$,*
$$d_C \;=\; \frac{s_L}{\sigma}\cdot\frac{1-\beta+\beta\gamma}{(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)}, \qquad \frac{s_L}{\sigma}\;<\;d_C\;<\;s_L . \tag{5.2}$$
*Capital’s preferred direction is free of $\omega$; it is strictly more labour-augmenting than the direction that maximises its own income, by $\rho w_C>0$; and it is strictly less labour-augmenting than the market’s, by $\rho(w_C-s_L)<0$. Both bounds are strict on the whole box; the lower is attained as $\gamma\theta_R\to0$ and the upper is never attained.*

*Proof.* The closed form is (4.1) with (5.1). $w_C>0$ because every factor in (5.1) is positive. $w_C-s_L=-\,s_L\,[(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)]/[(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)]<0$: the numerator of $w_C-s_L$ over the common denominator is $s_L[\beta\gamma\theta_R(1-\rho)-(1-\beta)-\beta\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)]=-s_L[(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)]$, strictly negative. The limits are read off (5.1). $\square$

In words, the owner of capital would, left to his income, point technology at $s_L/\sigma$; he points it further toward labour, by an amount proportional to the weight his own value function puts on the workers’ income — because that income is what the next generation’s workforce is built from, and he hires that workforce. But the correction is bounded: it can never carry him past the market’s point, because his stake in the workers’ income is a fraction of his stake in his own. The classical presumption that capital’s interest is a labour-saving bias is, on this face, true against the market and false against capital’s own income: capital wants a technology *less* biased against labour than the one that maximises its income, and *more* biased than the one that maximises output.

**Proposition 5 (The worker’s threshold).** *For $\rho\in(0,1)$, $w_W-s_L$ has the sign of $\theta_R-\bar\theta$ with*
$$\bar\theta \;=\; \frac{\beta s_L}{s_K(1-\beta)+\beta s_L}\;\in(0,1), \tag{5.3}$$
*free of $\sigma$, $\gamma$ and $\omega$. Hence the worker wants a more labour-augmenting technology than the market if and only if the private share of her reproduction exceeds $\bar\theta$; for $\theta_R<\bar\theta$ both classes prefer a technology more biased toward capital than the output-maximising one, with $d_C<d_W<s_L$; and at $\theta_R=1$ the worker is always above the market, by $(1-1/\sigma)\,s_K(1-\beta)/(1-\beta\rho)$.*

*Proof.* Put $w_W-s_L$ over the positive denominator $\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta\rho)$; the numerator is $\theta_R(1-\beta a_1)-s_L\beta(1-\theta_R)-s_L\theta_R(1-\beta\rho)$, which is linear in $\theta_R$ with coefficient $s_K(1-\beta)+\beta s_L>0$ and intercept $-\beta s_L$, hence equals $[s_K(1-\beta)+\beta s_L](\theta_R-\bar\theta)$. That $\bar\theta\in(0,1)$ follows from $1-\bar\theta=s_K(1-\beta)/[s_K(1-\beta)+\beta s_L]>0$. The ordering $d_C<d_W$ is Proposition 3 and $d_W<s_L$ is $w_W<s_L$; the $\theta_R=1$ value is direct substitution. $\square$

In words, the working class’s interest in labour-augmenting technology is a property of how its reproduction is paid for. When workers reproduce their class out of their own income, they want technology pointed at labour, and more so than the market. When the public stock that reproduces them is built out of capital income, their interest in that income grows with the public share, and past the threshold $\bar\theta$ it dominates: the class whose members are made productive by labour-augmenting technology wants less of it than the market would supply, because the fund that makes the next generation of workers is a claim on capital’s income. The threshold rises with patience and with labour’s share — a patient working class with a small share is on capital’s side of the market at almost any public share.

*So what.* The obvious answer of Section 1 — labour wants labour-augmenting technology — is the special case $\theta_R>\bar\theta$. Where the reproduction of the workforce is largely public, both classes sit on the capital side of the output-maximising technology and disagree about distance, not direction. To be sure, the disagreement never closes: Proposition 3 puts the worker strictly above the owner for every $\theta_R>0$. What closes is the disagreement with the market’s *sign*.

# 6. The state

Three agents may hold the direction on behalf of the economy, and they are three different points.

**Proposition 6 (The planner).** *A planner maximising $\lambda V_W+(1-\lambda)V_C$ has weight*
$$w_\lambda \;=\; \frac{\lambda N_W+(1-\lambda)N_C}{\lambda(M_W+N_W)+(1-\lambda)(M_C+N_C)},$$
*a mediant of $w_C$ and $w_W$: it equals $w_C$ at $\lambda=0$, $w_W$ at $\lambda=1$, and is strictly increasing in $\lambda$, with $\partial w_\lambda/\partial\lambda=(w_W-w_C)\,T_WT_C/[\lambda T_W+(1-\lambda)T_C]^2>0$ where $T_i=M_i+N_i>0$. Hence $d_C\le d(\lambda)\le d_W$ for every $\lambda\in[0,1]$.*

*Proof.* The planner’s payoff in $d$ is the $\lambda$-weighted sum of the two classes’ payoffs (3.2), whose value-function coefficients do not depend on $d$; the mediant form follows, the endpoints by substitution, and the derivative by differentiating and using Proposition 3. $\square$

A state with its own objective is a different matter, and which objective decides everything.

**Proposition 8 (The state under its own objectives).** *(i) A state whose period objective is the logged fund it administers, $\ln G_t$, has weight $w_S=w_C$ identically: it wants exactly capital’s technology. More generally, every agent whose period objective places no direct weight on the worker stock — the owner at every $\omega$, and this state — has the weight $w_C$ of (5.1). (ii) A state whose period objective is logged output, $\ln Y_t$, has weight $w_Y=(s_L+N_Y)/(1+M_Y+N_Y)$, a closed form of the same shape as (5.1), and $w_C<w_Y<s_L$ on the whole box: the output-maximising state, looking forward, wants a technology more biased toward capital than the point that maximises output today, and less so than capital itself.*

*Proof.* (i) For any agent whose felicity carries no direct $n$-term, the $n$-row of its coefficient system reads $a_n=\phi(1-a_1)+\beta[a_n(1-D)+(\text{weight on }E_C)(1-a_1)]$ with $\phi$ its felicity weight on $E_C$, so $M=\phi+\beta(a_k+a_g)=a_n[1-\beta(1-D)]/(1-a_1)$ and the ratio $N/M=\beta\gamma\theta_R(1-a_1)/[1-\beta(1-D)]$ is the same for every such agent; it is (5.1)’s $w_C$, and it is why $\omega$ is absent from it. The fund-valuing state is such an agent with $\phi=0$ and felicity on $g$. (ii) The direct term of $\ln Y$ carries the market’s loadings $(s_L,s_K)$, hence weight $s_L$ on the worker composite with total weight one; the continuation carries $(M_Y,N_Y)$ from the system with felicity loadings $(s_K,s_L,0)$ on $(k,n,g)$. The signs of $w_Y-s_L$ and $w_Y-w_C$ are decided by the Möbius cover in the certificate. $\square$

In words, “the state” is not one point on the frontier but one per objective. A state that lives on the tax it levies on capital wants what capital wants, exactly, because its stake in the workforce runs through the same income; a state that maximises output over time wants less labour-augmenting technology than the output-maximising point, because capital income is what next period’s output is built from, but more than capital wants, because output is also built from workers. Where the output state sits relative to the worker is reported on a sample and is not a claim of this paper.

# 7. Perfect substitutes

**Proposition 7 (Perfect substitutes).** *At $\rho=1$ the tangent model is exact. The owner’s coefficient on the worker stock is $a_n=0$, so $w_C=0$ and $d_C=0$: capital wants pure capital augmentation. The worker’s direction is*
$$d_W \;=\; w_W \;=\; \frac{\theta_R(1-\beta)}{\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta)},$$
*interior for $\theta_R<1$ and equal to one at $\theta_R=1$: with no public fund the worker wants pure labour augmentation, and the fund pulls her toward capital’s technology by exactly its weight in her continuation.*

*Proof.* At $\rho=1$ factor incomes are linear in the factors, $s_KY=\theta_PBK$, so nothing the owner’s programme reads depends on $n$ and the $a_n$-equation of the system forces $a_n=0$ (Galindo 2026, Proposition 2); then $N_C=0$. For the worker, $a_1=1$ and $a_2=0$ in (5.1) give the display; $1-w_W=\beta(1-\theta_R)/[\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta)]$ is positive for $\theta_R<1$ and zero at $\theta_R=1$. $\square$

*So what.* At the far end of the elasticity line the two classes are as far apart as the frontier allows on capital’s side and as far apart as the fund allows on labour’s. The spread of Proposition 3 is largest here, and it is here that the fund’s pull on the worker is visible in one line.

# 8. Scope

The following bound the results and are not conjectures about them.

**The results are theorems of the tangent model.** For $\sigma\in(1,\infty)$ the exact model has no affine value function (Galindo 2026, Proposition 1). Propositions 2–6 are exact statements about the first-order model of Section 2(i), whose first-order conditions coincide with the exact model’s at the self-consistent stationary ratio (Galindo 2026, Section 6). Propositions 1 and 7 are exact. **The corroboration of the signs on the exact model is owed and has not been run**: the pre-registered test is the constant-policy maximisation on the exact two-dimensional reduction at three values of $\sigma$, and until it runs the sign of $d_W-d_C$ in the exact economy is undischarged.

**It is a linear-state game.** Every direction is a dominant strategy, free of the state and of every other agent’s instrument. Nothing here is a theory of strategic behaviour over technology; it is a theory of what each agent wants, and of the distances between them.

**Technology is a flow.** The point on the frontier is chosen afresh each period and no knowledge accumulates. Nothing is said about balanced growth with sustained technical change, about Uzawa’s theorem, or about the long-run direction of Acemoglu (2003); those are questions about a technology stock, which stays linear-state with a unit root and is the next paper’s object, not this one’s.

**The worker’s objective is the size of her class, read as effective labour.** With a per-head objective the weight $w_W$ changes and Proposition 5 must be re-derived. The reading is the source model’s and is inherited with it.

**The provision corner.** Everything is stated at $\varphi=1$. Away from it the worker’s composite carries the tax and the split, and the closed forms are corner objects.

**The output state against the worker.** Proposition 8 places the fiscal state exactly at capital’s point and the output state strictly between capital and the market; where it sits relative to the worker, for every parameter, is not established, and Section 6 reports a sample.

**Prior art at abstract grade.** No body in the induced-innovation or the appropriate-technology line has been read at source for this version; the positions attributed to those literatures in Section 1 are what their titles and abstracts state. Whether Julius (2005), Shah and Desai (1981) or Tavani and Zamparelli (2018) already derive a class-specific or a state-chosen direction is the first thing to check, and until it is checked this paper claims only the primitives it adds relative to that line as its abstracts describe it: a produced workforce and the weight each agent places on it.

# 9. Conclusion

Which way should technology go is a question the frontier literature answers with a point, the output-maximising one, and the induced-innovation tradition answers with a bias, capital’s. Where the factors are produced, both answers are special cases of one formula. Every agent’s direction is the capital-income direction plus the substitution gap times its weight on the reproduction of the workforce. Capital’s weight is positive — it hires the class that its workers’ income reproduces — and it is smaller than the market’s, so capital’s bias against labour is real against the market and reversed against its own income. The worker’s weight depends on how her class is reproduced: above a threshold on the private share she wants what the obvious answer says she wants, and below it she wants, as capital does, a technology more biased toward capital than the market supplies — because the fund that reproduces her class is built from capital’s income. A planner sits between the two; a state that lives on the tax on capital wants exactly what capital wants; a state that maximises output over time sits below the market; and under the technology that every neighbouring model uses all of these coincide and the question cannot be asked.

The policy reading is narrow and worth stating anyway. A public fund for the reproduction of the workforce, financed from capital income, does something to the politics of technology that its size alone does not show: it aligns the working class’s interest in the direction of technical change with capital’s, past a threshold that depends on patience and the shares and on nothing else in the model. Admittedly, the alignment is over direction and never over distance, and the model has no strategic content on the face where it is solved. But it locates, in one coefficient, the sense in which the reproduction of the working class as a condition of the reproduction of capital is not a statement about who pays but about what each class wants the machines to do.
