Abstract

Which way should technology go — toward making labour more productive, or capital? This paper puts the technology frontier of directed and appropriate technical change into a two-class economy whose factors are produced: capital from capital income, the workforce from worker income and a public stock financed by a tax on capital. Every agent’s preferred direction is s_L/\sigma, the direction that maximises capital income, plus the substitution gap 1-1/\sigma times the weight its own optimisation places on reproducing the workforce, in the first-order model of that economy with one period a generation, full depreciation and logarithmic objectives. The market’s weight is labour’s share; capital’s is strictly positive and strictly below it. Call a technology more biased toward labour if, at given stocks, it gives labour a larger share. Capital then wants a technology more biased toward labour than its own income dictates and less than the market supplies. The worker wants one more biased toward labour than the market exactly when the private share of her reproduction exceeds \bar\theta=\beta s_L/[\beta s_L+(1-\beta)s_K]; below that, she too leans toward capital, because the public stock that reproduces her class is financed from capital’s income, and the two classes disagree over how far, not which way. A Pareto planner sits between the classes, a state valuing its fund wants exactly capital’s technology, and a state valuing output leans more toward capital than the market. Each ranking holds for every finite elasticity of substitution above one, where more bias toward labour means more labour augmentation, and below one, where it means less, wherever capital’s share lies between 1-\sigma and \sigma. Under Cobb–Douglas all six directions coincide.

1. Introduction

If a class could choose the technology of the economy it lives in, which would it choose? The obvious answer is that workers would make labour more productive and capitalists would make capital more productive, and that the market, weighing the two by their shares, would land somewhere between. In the literature on directed and appropriate technical change the market’s answer is derived rather than assumed: firms choose factor-augmenting efficiencies from a frontier, and the point they choose is the one that maximises output given the factors the economy has (Caselli and Coleman 2006; León-Ledesma and Satchi 2019; Acemoglu 2002). In that literature the factors are endowments and the classes never appear: nobody’s interest in the factors goes beyond their price. Where the classes do appear with endowed factors, in the model of automation and repression of Acemoglu, Gitmez and Shadmehr (2026), the obvious answer holds for the classes themselves: the level of automation that maximises labour’s income lies below the output-maximising level, and the level that maximises capital’s lies above it (their Proposition 2). Capital chooses otherwise there through the state, to survive politically. A capitalist state facing the threat of revolt imposes the level that maximises labour’s income (their Proposition 3), and one that represses instead automates beyond capital’s (their Proposition 4).

This paper shows that the obvious answer is wrong once the factors are produced, and shows exactly how. It puts the same technology frontier into a two-class economy in which capital is accumulated out of capital income and the workforce is reproduced out of worker income and a public stock financed by a tax on capital. The direction of technology is the one new primitive of that economy. In it every agent who might choose the technology — the owner of capital, the working class, a Pareto planner, a state with its own objective, and the output-maximising market — has a preferred point on the frontier with one closed form. It is the direction that maximises capital income, plus the substitution gap 1-1/\sigma times the weight that agent places on the reproduction of the workforce. The market’s weight is labour’s share. The owner’s is positive, so, with a finite elasticity of substitution above one, capital wants a more labour-augmenting technology than its own income would dictate, because the workforce it hires next generation is built from the workers’ income this generation; and it is strictly below the market’s, so the correction never carries capital past the output-maximising point. The sign is the one Acemoglu, Gitmez and Shadmehr find for a capitalist state that fears revolt. The mechanism and the size are not. There the correction buys survival and carries the state past the market’s point to labour’s own. Here it is capital’s own, it prices the reproduction of the workforce that capital will hire, and it stops short of the market’s point. The worker’s weight is where the surprise lies. It falls with the public share of her reproduction, because the public stock is built from capital income, and it crosses the market’s weight at a threshold that depends on the discount factor and the factor shares and on nothing else. Below that threshold the working class wants a technology more biased toward capital than the market would supply — and so, always, does capital. The conflict between the classes is then over how far to go in capital’s direction, not over which direction to go.

The result belongs to the classical tradition. The line from Kennedy (1964) through Shah and Desai (1981), Foley (2003), Duménil and Lévy (2003) and Julius (2005) — surveyed by Tavani and Zamparelli (2017) — reads the direction of technical change as induced by distribution, and reads a labour-saving bias as capital’s response to labour’s strength. The model here is a member of the analytical strand of that tradition: classes are defined by what they own, the method is game-theoretic, and no value accounting is used. In the survey of that line, and in the two of its bodies cited below, the direction is still chosen by firms. The survey describes firms choosing a profile of technical change on Kennedy’s frontier, and, once innovation is costly, capitalists choosing how much of their saved profits to spend on it (Tavani and Zamparelli 2017, working paper, pp. 16, 28–29). No model it describes has workers, a planner or a state choose the direction. In Julius (2005, pp. 106, 115) firms pick it on Kennedy’s frontier to raise the profit rate, while workers’ bargaining strength and a state that taxes profits to pay a social wage act on distribution and, through it, on the direction (pp. 116–117). In Tavani and Zamparelli (2018) a state funds labour-augmenting research out of taxes, and technical change has a single dimension. What this paper adds to that line is who chooses, and the object that makes the choosers differ. That object is the weight each agent places on the reproduction of a workforce that, in Julius (2005, p. 116), simply grows at a given rate. That weight is what the tradition states as a condition, and here it is not a condition but a coefficient in capital’s own choice of technique.1

Three features of the model govern how the results read. First, each agent’s preferred direction is a constant, free of the state and of every other agent’s choice. Every ranking below therefore holds against any path of the other instruments that does not respond to the state, and needs no selection among equilibria within that class.2 Second, the results are theorems of a first-order model of that economy, which is exact at unit elasticity and at perfect substitutability and first-order between. Above unit elasticity, from a state at which a constant policy holds the capital–labour ratio fixed, the gradient of every agent’s exact payoff in the instruments is the first-order model’s taken at that ratio, so there the two models share their first-order conditions (Lemma A.7). Off Cobb–Douglas the exact model has no affine value function (Lemma A.1, for both classes). Third, under Cobb–Douglas all six directions coincide (Proposition 1), so the question of direction arises only away from unit elasticity. Every ordering of directions below is proved for an elasticity above one. Below one the orderings are established where capital’s share lies between 1-\sigma and \sigma, the region on which both slopes of Section 2(i) lie in (0,1); there every ordering of directions reverses, and so does the effect of a direction on labour’s share, so which agent wants the technology that, at given stocks, gives labour the larger share is the same on both sides of unit elasticity.

Section 2 collects the two results about the economy that everything below uses, the first-order expansion that makes the game linear-state and the effect of augmenting a factor on that factor’s share, and writes down the frontier the agents choose from. Section 3 sets out the two-class economy, its three stocks and who chooses what. Section 4 shows that under Cobb–Douglas all six agents want the same technology, and that away from it every agent’s direction is the capital-income direction plus the substitution gap times a single weight. Section 5 computes the owner’s and the worker’s weights, orders them, and finds the threshold on the private share of reproduction at which the worker crosses the market. Section 6 does the same for a Pareto planner and for two states, one that values the fund it administers and one that values output. Section 7 takes substitution to its limit, where the tangent model is exact and capital’s stake in the workers’ income vanishes. Section 8 sets out the domain of the results, the region below unit elasticity on which they survive, and what the tangent leaves out. Section 9 restates the formula and reads it as policy.

2. Two results and one frontier

(i) The face and its tangent. The economy is set out in Section 3. Under a constant-elasticity technology with \sigma\neq1 no value function affine in the logged states solves either class’s Bellman equation, except at \sigma=\infty (Lemma A.1). What does close is the expansion of the two logged income composites to first order about a reference effective capital–labour ratio \bar x (Lemma A.3): the composite that pays capital, \ln s_K+\psi, has slope a_1=\rho+(1-\rho)s_K in x, and the composite that pays labour, \ln s_L+\psi, has slope a_2=(1-\rho)s_K, with \rho=1-1/\sigma and s_K capital’s share at \bar x. Two identities carry everything below: 1-a_1 \;=\; \frac{s_L}{\sigma}, \qquad a_1-a_2 \;=\; \rho \;=\; 1-\frac{1}{\sigma}. \tag{2.1} At \rho=0 both slopes equal the Cobb–Douglas share \alpha and the face is exact. Appendix A proves both results.

(ii) The share effect. With production Y=[\theta_P(BK)^{\rho}+(1-\theta_P)(AL)^{\rho}]^{1/\rho}, augmenting a factor raises that factor’s share when \sigma>1, lowers it when \sigma<1 and leaves it unchanged at \sigma=1. This is the price effect of Acemoglu (2002) and is standard; the term s_L/\sigma below is its footprint, and nothing about it is claimed here.

(iii) The frontier. Technology is a pair (A,B) of labour- and capital-augmenting efficiencies on a frontier of the kind the appropriate-technology literature uses (Caselli and Coleman 2006), written here as A^{1/\varepsilon}+B^{1/\varepsilon}=1, \qquad (A,B)=\bigl(d^{\varepsilon},(1-d)^{\varepsilon}\bigr), \qquad d\in(0,1),\ \varepsilon>0, \tag{2.2} so that \ln A=\varepsilon\ln d and \ln B=\varepsilon\ln(1-d). The number d is the direction: d\to1 is pure labour augmentation, d\to0 pure capital augmentation. The parametrisation is chosen because it keeps the logged efficiencies as terms in the instrument alone, which is what keeps the game linear-state; it will turn out that \varepsilon cancels from every direction, so the frontier’s curvature does no work.

Result (i) says where the economy can be solved; (ii) says that the direction moves shares only away from unit elasticity; (iii) supplies the object to be chosen. Sections 4 to 7 put the three together and let a class, a planner or a state do the choosing.

3. The model

One period is a generation; both stocks depreciate fully; both classes have logarithmic period objectives. Write k=\ln K, n=\ln L, g=\ln G for the logged capital stock, worker stock and public stock, a=\ln A, b=\ln B, and x=b+k-a-n for the logged effective capital–labour ratio. Effective labour is the worker stock; the object reproduced is capacity, not headcount. Constant returns give \ln Y=a+n+\psi(x) with \psi'(x)=s_K(x).

The owner of capital chooses a saving rate i; a tax \tau on capital income funds the public stock, which is provided in kind (\varphi=1: all of the tax is spent on the stock); and the direction d is chosen by whichever agent the section in question studies.

Who chooses. One agent at a time is assigned the direction. The saving rate and the tax are the owner’s; both follow paths that do not respond to the state, and no direction below depends on those paths. Because the direction is chosen afresh each period (Section 8), the assigned agent’s best choice is the same constant at every date and against every such path of the other instruments, and in that sense it is a dominant strategy. The object is therefore the solution of one agent’s problem in a restricted game — one agent, one instrument, a constant policy. It is not an equilibrium of a game in which several agents choose the direction at once, and it is not a Ramsey plan.

Write the two logged income composites E_C \;=\; \ln s_K(x)+\psi(x)+a+n, \qquad E_W \;=\; \ln s_L(x)+\psi(x)+a+n, capital’s income and labour’s, both logged. The transitions are \begin{aligned} \ln C_C &= \ln(1-i)+\ln(1-\tau)+E_C, \\ k' &= \ln i+\ln(1-\tau)+E_C, \\ n' &= n+\gamma\theta_R\,(E_W-n)+\gamma(1-\theta_R)\,(g-n), \\ g' &= \ln\tau+E_C, \end{aligned} with owner felicity (1-\omega)\ln C_C+\omega k and worker felicity n. Here \gamma is the elasticity of the reproduction technology and \theta_R the weight of the workers’ own income in it, 1-\theta_R the weight of the public stock per worker; \beta is the common discount factor; \theta_R is a different object from the technology’s distribution parameter \theta_P. Throughout \beta,\gamma,\theta_R,\omega\in(0,1), s_K\in(0,1) and \rho\in(0,1), the box on which the tangent model is licensed above unit elasticity (Lemma 1). Every sign statement of Sections 4 to 6 is read on that open box, and Section 8 states which of them survive below unit elasticity, and where.

Under the tangent of Section 2(i), and using x=b+k-a-n, E_C = \text{const}+a_1 b+a_1 k+(1-a_1)\,a+(1-a_1)\,n, \qquad E_W = \text{const}+a_2 b+a_2 k+(1-a_2)\,a+(1-a_2)\,n. \tag{3.1} Every transition is now affine in (k,n,g) plus terms in (i,\tau,d) alone: the game is linear-state. Each agent’s value function is affine in (k,n,g) with coefficients solving a linear system — for the owner (a_k,a_n,a_g) and for the worker (p,q,r), the systems solved in Lemma A.3 — and each agent’s preferred direction is a constant.

Lemma 1 (The licensed domain). The transition matrix of (k,n,g) under (3.1) has unit row sums, entrywise non-negative entries for \rho\in(0,1], and eigenvalues exactly \{0,1,a_1-D\} with D=\gamma(1-\theta_R)+\gamma\theta_R a_2 and |a_1-D|<1. Hence its spectral radius is one, \beta\cdot1<1, and every coefficient below is a convergent discounted sum.

Proof. The k- and g-rows are both (a_1,1-a_1,0) and the n-row is (\gamma\theta_R a_2,\,1-D,\,\gamma(1-\theta_R)); each sums to one. Each entry is a product or complement of numbers in (0,1), hence non-negative, so by Perron–Frobenius the spectral radius is the common row sum. The two equal rows give a zero eigenvalue, the row sums give one, and the trace gives the third. |a_1-D|<1 is the pair of inequalities 1\mp(a_1-D)>0, each a polynomial with positive coefficients after the substitution x\mapsto s/(1+s) of every parameter. \square

The object. Substituting (3.1) into any agent’s discounted objective, the terms in the direction collect into \varepsilon\bigl[\,A_i\ln d+B_i\ln(1-d)\,\bigr], \qquad A_i=M_i(1-a_1)+N_i(1-a_2),\quad B_i=M_i a_1+N_i a_2, \tag{3.2} where M_i is agent i’s total weight on capital’s composite E_C — through its own felicity where that is capital income, and through k' and g' — and N_i is its total weight on labour’s composite E_W, which enters only through n'. When A_i and B_i are both positive, as they are for every agent studied here on the box, the payoff (3.2) is strictly concave in d with the unique interior maximiser d_i \;=\; \frac{A_i}{A_i+B_i}, \tag{3.3} and \varepsilon has cancelled. For the owner M_C=(1-\omega)+\beta(a_k+a_g) and N_C=\beta\gamma\theta_R a_n; for the worker M_W=\beta(p+r) and N_W=\beta\gamma\theta_R q. The output-maximising market point is the direction that maximises \ln Y=a+n+\psi(x) at the current stocks, which is d_M=s_L exactly, since \psi'=s_K; this is the share-weighted rule of Kennedy (1964) and the point the frontier literature’s firms choose.

4. The deletion, and the decomposition

Section 3’s economy is now read for its direction. Two questions decide it: whether the agents who might choose the technology ever want different points, and, where they do, what separates one agent’s point from another’s.

Proposition 1 (Deletion). At \rho=0 — Cobb–Douglas with share \alpha — the owner, the worker, a Pareto planner at every weight, a state maximising its fiscal capacity, a state maximising output, and the market all prefer the same direction, d=1-\alpha. They prefer it at every date, whatever the directions at other dates, and against any paths of the saving and tax rates that do not respond to the state, wherever the objectives compared are finite. The same holds when next period’s capital and public stock also carry over the fractions 1-\delta_K and 1-\delta_G of the current stocks, with \delta_K,\delta_G\in(0,1], and when each logarithm in a period objective is replaced by an isoelastic function of its argument.

Proof. At \rho=0, a_1=a_2=\alpha, so A_i=(M_i+N_i)(1-\alpha) and B_i=(M_i+N_i)\alpha for every agent whatever its weights, and (3.3) gives 1-\alpha; the market’s s_L is 1-\alpha. The extension does not need the face. At \rho=0 output is K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}d^{\varepsilon(1-\alpha)}(1-d)^{\varepsilon\alpha} and every income is a fixed share of it, so the direction enters only through a factor that rises below 1-\alpha and falls above it, whatever the stocks. With the rates held on their paths, the owner’s consumption and every next-period stock are non-decreasing in current output and in the current stocks, with or without the carried-over fractions, and every period objective is increasing in its variable. Fix the directions at the other dates and replace one period’s direction by 1-\alpha. That period’s output rises strictly and, by induction on dates, no later stock, output or income falls. Every objective above therefore rises, and strictly, because each rises strictly with that period’s output or with a stock built from it: the owner’s consumption, next period’s worker stock through labour income (\gamma\theta_R>0), next period’s public stock (\tau>0), and output itself. \square

In words, under Cobb–Douglas every income is a fixed share of output and every agent’s stake in the technology is a stake in output; the direction each wants is the direction the market supplies, and there is nothing for classes to disagree about. A model of this kind solved at unit elasticity is therefore silent on the question: not because it answers it in the market’s favour but because its technology cannot pose it.

Proposition 2 (Decomposition). For \rho\in(0,1) every agent’s preferred direction is d_i \;=\; \frac{s_L}{\sigma}+\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{\sigma}\Bigr)\,w_i, \qquad w_i=\frac{N_i}{M_i+N_i}\in[0,1], \tag{4.1} the capital-income direction s_L/\sigma plus the substitution gap times agent i’s weight on the reproduction of the workforce. The market’s weight is w_M=s_L.

Proof. From (3.2)–(3.3), d_i=(1-a_1)+w_i\,(a_1-a_2), and (2.1) gives 1-a_1=s_L/\sigma and a_1-a_2=\rho. For the market, s_L=(1-a_1)+\rho s_L by (2.1). \square

In words, two numbers describe the technology of an economy that produces its factors: the direction that maximises capital income, which every agent shares, and a weight that says how much that agent cares about the income from which the next generation of workers is built. Agents differ only in the weight, and the substitution gap decides how much the difference matters — at unit elasticity it does not matter at all, and at perfect substitutability the weight is the whole direction.

The direction of technical change is therefore not settled by factor supplies alone. It is a family of points indexed by whose reproduction the chooser values, and Sections 5 to 7 compute those weights and order them.

5. Who wants which technology

The owner’s and the worker’s weights are, after solving the two linear systems (Lemma A.3), w_C \;=\; \frac{s_L}{\sigma}\cdot\frac{\beta\gamma\theta_R}{(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)}, \qquad w_W \;=\; \frac{\theta_R\,(1-\beta a_1)}{\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta\rho)}. \tag{5.1} The owner’s weight is free of \omega; the worker’s is free of \gamma, the reproduction elasticity cancelling from her direction.

Proposition 3 (Ordering and spread). For \rho\in(0,1), w_W>w_C on the whole box, so d_W-d_C \;=\; \Bigl(1-\frac{1}{\sigma}\Bigr)(w_W-w_C) \;>\;0 . The worker wants the more labour-augmenting technology, the disagreement is zero at \sigma=1, and it is the product of a substitution gap and a reproduction-weight gap.

Proof. Write H=\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta\rho) and \Lambda=(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R) for the denominators of (5.1), and Q=(1-\beta a_1)+\beta\gamma\bigl(1-\theta_R(1-a_2)\bigr). Then (w_W-w_C)H\Lambda=(1-\beta)\,\theta_R\,Q identically (Appendix A.4). Each of H, \Lambda and Q is a sum of positive multiples of one minus a product of numbers in (0,1), so every factor is positive. \square

In words, away from unit elasticity the two classes never want the same technology, and what separates them measures two things at once: how far the direction can move shares, and how much more of the workers’ income the worker’s own continuation prices than the owner’s does. How reproduction is paid for does not settle this ranking. The private share moves both weights and never crosses them, so the worker stands above the owner at every private share; where each of them stands relative to the market is the separate question of Propositions 4 and 5.

Proposition 4 (Capital’s direction). For \rho\in(0,1), d_C \;=\; \frac{s_L}{\sigma}\cdot\frac{1-\beta+\beta\gamma}{(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)}, \qquad \frac{s_L}{\sigma}\;<\;d_C\;<\;s_L . \tag{5.2} Capital’s preferred direction is free of \omega; it is strictly more labour-augmenting than the direction that maximises its own income, by \rho w_C>0; and it is strictly less labour-augmenting than the market’s, by \rho(w_C-s_L)<0. Both bounds are strict on the whole box; the lower is approached as \gamma\theta_R\to0 and neither is attained.

Proof. The closed form is (4.1) with (5.1). w_C>0 because every factor in (5.1) is positive. w_C-s_L=-\,s_L\,[(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)]/[(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)]<0: the numerator of w_C-s_L over the common denominator is s_L[\beta\gamma\theta_R(1-\rho)-(1-\beta)-\beta\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)]=-s_L[(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)], strictly negative. The limits are read off (5.1). \square

In words, the owner of capital would, left to his income, point technology at s_L/\sigma; he points it further toward labour, by an amount proportional to the weight his own value function puts on the workers’ income — because that income is what the next generation’s workforce is built from, and he hires that workforce. But the correction is bounded: it can never carry him past the market’s point, because his stake in the workers’ income is a fraction of his stake in his own. The classical presumption that capital’s interest is a labour-saving bias is, on this face, true against the market and false against capital’s own income. Capital wants a technology less biased against labour than the one that maximises its income, and more biased than the one that maximises output. To be sure, capital’s stake in the publicly funded reproduction of the labour it employs is not new: Galor and Moav (2006) show that capitalists support a tax on capital that funds an in-kind public good for workers once physical and human capital are complements. What is new is that the same stake sets capital’s preferred direction of technical change.

Proposition 5 (The worker’s threshold). For \rho\in(0,1), w_W-s_L has the sign of \theta_R-\bar\theta with \bar\theta \;=\; \frac{\beta s_L}{s_K(1-\beta)+\beta s_L}\;\in(0,1), \tag{5.3} free of \sigma, \gamma and \omega. Hence the worker wants a more labour-augmenting technology than the market if and only if the private share of her reproduction exceeds \bar\theta; for \theta_R<\bar\theta both classes prefer a technology more biased toward capital than the output-maximising one, with d_C<d_W<s_L; and as \theta_R\to1 the worker’s excess over the market tends to (1-1/\sigma)\,s_K(1-\beta)/(1-\beta\rho)>0.

Proof. Put w_W-s_L over the positive denominator \beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta\rho); the numerator is \theta_R(1-\beta a_1)-s_L\beta(1-\theta_R)-s_L\theta_R(1-\beta\rho), which is linear in \theta_R with coefficient s_K(1-\beta)+\beta s_L>0 and intercept -\beta s_L, hence equals [s_K(1-\beta)+\beta s_L](\theta_R-\bar\theta). That \bar\theta\in(0,1) follows from 1-\bar\theta=s_K(1-\beta)/[s_K(1-\beta)+\beta s_L]>0. The ordering d_C<d_W is Proposition 3 and d_W<s_L is w_W<s_L; the limit is direct substitution. \square

In words, the working class’s interest in labour-augmenting technology is a property of how its reproduction is paid for. When workers reproduce their class out of their own income, they want technology pointed at labour, and more so than the market. When the public stock that reproduces them is built out of capital income, their interest in that income grows with the public share, and past the threshold \bar\theta it dominates. The class whose members are made productive by labour-augmenting technology wants less of it than the market would supply, because the fund that makes the next generation of workers is a claim on capital’s income. The threshold rises with patience and with labour’s share, and where it lies is a matter of calibration rather than sign. At a discount factor of one half \bar\theta equals labour’s share exactly, and it exceeds labour’s share when the discount factor exceeds one half. A period is a generation, so an annual discount factor between 0.96 and 0.98 puts \beta between about 0.36 and 0.60 over twenty-five years, and with labour’s share at two thirds \bar\theta then lies between about 0.53 and 0.75. The share s_K in (5.3) is capital’s share at the reference ratio \bar x, and at a stationary state that ratio itself depends on \theta_R; the comparison of \theta_R with \bar\theta is therefore read at the shares of the state about which the tangent is taken.

The obvious answer of Section 1 — labour wants labour-augmenting technology — is the special case \theta_R>\bar\theta. Where the reproduction of the workforce is largely public, both classes sit on the capital side of the output-maximising technology and disagree about distance, not direction. To be sure, the disagreement never closes: Proposition 3 puts the worker strictly above the owner for every \theta_R>0. What closes is the disagreement with the market’s sign.

6. The state

Three agents may hold the direction on behalf of the economy, and they do not in general want the same point.

Proposition 6 (The planner). A planner maximising \lambda V_W+(1-\lambda)V_C has weight w_\lambda \;=\; \frac{\lambda N_W+(1-\lambda)N_C}{\lambda(M_W+N_W)+(1-\lambda)(M_C+N_C)}, a mediant of w_C and w_W: it equals w_C at \lambda=0, w_W at \lambda=1, and is strictly increasing in \lambda, with \partial w_\lambda/\partial\lambda=(w_W-w_C)\,T_WT_C/[\lambda T_W+(1-\lambda)T_C]^2>0 where T_i=M_i+N_i>0. Hence d_C\le d(\lambda)\le d_W for every \lambda\in[0,1].

Proof. The planner’s payoff in d is the \lambda-weighted sum of the two classes’ payoffs (3.2), whose value-function coefficients do not depend on d; the mediant form follows, the endpoints by substitution, and the derivative by differentiating and using Proposition 3. \square

A state with its own objective is a different matter, and which objective decides everything.

Proposition 8 (The state under its own objectives). (i) A state whose period objective is the logged fund it administers, \ln G_t, has weight w_S=w_C identically: it wants exactly capital’s technology. More generally, every agent whose period objective combines, with non-negative weights not all zero, capital’s logged income E_C and the logged capital and public stocks k and g, plus terms in the instruments alone has the weight w_C of (5.1). The owner at every \omega and this state are two such agents. (ii) A state whose period objective is logged output, \ln Y_t, has weight w_Y=(s_L+N_Y)/(1+M_Y+N_Y), a closed form of the same shape as (5.1), and w_C<w_Y<s_L on the whole box: the output-maximising state, looking forward, wants a technology more biased toward capital than the point that maximises output today, and less so than capital itself.

Proof. (i) For any agent whose felicity is \phi E_C plus non-negative multiples of k and g and terms in the instruments, the n-row of its coefficient system reads a_n=\phi(1-a_1)+\beta[a_n(1-D)+(\text{weight on }E_C)(1-a_1)] with \phi its felicity weight on E_C, so M=\phi+\beta(a_k+a_g)=a_n[1-\beta(1-D)]/(1-a_1) and the ratio N/M=\beta\gamma\theta_R(1-a_1)/[1-\beta(1-D)] is the same for every such agent; it is (5.1)’s w_C, and it is why \omega is absent from it. The fund-valuing state is such an agent with \phi=0 and felicity on g. (ii) The direct term of \ln Y carries the market’s loadings (s_L,s_K), hence weight s_L on the worker composite with total weight one; the continuation carries (M_Y,N_Y) from the system with felicity loadings (s_K,s_L,0) on (k,n,g). Solving that system gives (6.1). With J>0 the denominator of (6.1) and K and Q as in the proof of Proposition 3, (w_Y-s_L)J=\beta s_L\bigl[\gamma\bigl(\theta_R(\beta s_L+s_K)-\beta\bigr)-(1-\beta)s_K\bigr], whose bracket rises with \theta_R and equals -(1-\beta)(1-\gamma)s_K<0 at \theta_R=1; and (w_Y-w_C)JK=(1-\beta)\,s_L\,(1-\beta+\beta\gamma)\,Q>0 (Appendix A.5). \square

In words, “the state” is not one point on the frontier but one per objective. A state that lives on the tax it levies on capital wants what capital wants, exactly, because its stake in the workforce runs through the same income; a state that maximises output over time wants less labour-augmenting technology than the output-maximising point, because capital income is what next period’s output is built from, but more than capital wants, because output is also built from workers. Where it sits relative to the worker depends on how the workforce is reproduced, and the answer is exact.

Proposition 9 (The output state against the worker). For \rho\in(0,1) the output state’s weight is w_Y \;=\; \frac{s_L\bigl[(1-\beta)(1-\beta a_1)+\beta\gamma\bigl((1-\beta)+\theta_R(1-\rho)(\beta s_L+s_K)\bigr)\bigr]}{(1-\beta)(1-\beta\rho s_L)+\beta\gamma\bigl(1-\rho\theta_R(\beta s_L+s_K)\bigr)}, \tag{6.1} and w_Y-w_W has the sign of \theta_Y-\theta_R, where \theta_Y \;=\; \frac{\beta s_L}{\beta s_L+s_K}\;<\;\bar\theta . \tag{6.2} Hence the directions are completely ordered on the whole box by the private share of reproduction: for \theta_R<\theta_Y, d_C<d_W<d_Y<s_L; for \theta_Y<\theta_R<\bar\theta, d_C<d_Y<d_W<s_L; for \theta_R>\bar\theta, d_C<d_Y<s_L<d_W. The fiscal state sits at d_C throughout and the planner between d_C and d_W. Both thresholds are free of \sigma, \gamma and \omega.

Proof. Write J for the denominator of (6.1), H=\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta\rho) for the denominator of w_W in (5.1), and Q=(1-\beta a_1)+\beta\gamma\bigl(1-\theta_R(1-a_2)\bigr). Then (w_Y-w_W)\,H\,J \;=\; (1-\beta)(\beta s_L+s_K)(\theta_Y-\theta_R)\,Q identically in the parameters. Each of H, J and Q is a sum of positive multiples of terms of the form one minus a product of numbers in (0,1), so all three are positive on the box, and the sign follows. Moreover \bar\theta-\theta_Y=\beta^2s_Ls_K/\bigl[(s_K(1-\beta)+\beta s_L)(s_K+\beta s_L)\bigr]>0. The three orderings combine this with Propositions 3, 5 and 8, and every direction inherits its weight’s order through (4.1). The closed form and the identity are derived in Appendix A.5 and A.6. \square

In words, the working class ranks against a state that maximises output on the same object that ranks it against the market: how much of its reproduction it pays for itself. When the private share is low, the worker’s stake in capital income, which reaches her through the fund, is large enough that she wants a technology more biased toward capital than the output state does. The two thresholds are one odds condition read twice: \theta_R/(1-\theta_R)=\beta s_L/s_K for the output state, and the same with s_K discounted by 1-\beta for the market. Thus the worker overtakes the output state before she overtakes the market, and the distance between the two thresholds is the discounting of capital’s share.

7. Perfect substitutes

At perfect substitutability the tangent of Section 2(i) is exact, so the directions below are the exact economy’s own.

Proposition 7 (Perfect substitutes). At \rho=1 the tangent model is exact. The owner’s coefficient on the worker stock is a_n=0, so w_C=0. Capital’s payoff (3.2) in the direction reduces to \varepsilon B_C\ln(1-d), which is strictly decreasing, so it has no maximiser on (0,1). Its preference runs to the limit d\to0, pure capital augmentation, a corner of the frontier and not the interior point (3.3). The worker’s direction is d_W \;=\; w_W \;=\; \frac{\theta_R(1-\beta)}{\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta)}, interior for \theta_R<1 and tending to one as \theta_R\to1: as the public fund’s weight in her reproduction vanishes the worker’s point approaches pure labour augmentation, and the fund pulls her toward capital’s technology by exactly its weight in her continuation.

Proof. At \rho=1 factor incomes are linear in the factors, s_KY=\theta_PBK, so nothing the owner’s programme reads depends on n and the a_n-equation of the system forces a_n=0 (Lemma A.2); then N_C=0, so A_C=M_C(1-a_1)+N_C(1-a_2)=0 at a_1=1. For the worker, a_1=1 and a_2=0 in (5.1) give the display; 1-w_W=\beta(1-\theta_R)/[\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta)] is positive for \theta_R<1 and tends to zero as \theta_R\to1. \square

In words, when capital and labour are perfect substitutes capital’s income does not depend on the workforce at all, so capital has no stake in the workers’ reproduction and points technology entirely at itself.

At the far end of the elasticity line the two classes are as far apart as the frontier allows on capital’s side and as far apart as the fund allows on labour’s. The spread of Proposition 3 is largest here, and it is here that the fund’s pull on the worker is visible in one line.

8. Scope

The following set out the domain of the results and their link to the exact economy.

The results are theorems of the tangent model. For \sigma\in(1,\infty) the exact model has no affine value function (Lemma A.1). Propositions 2–6, 8 and 9 are exact statements about the first-order model of Section 2(i); Propositions 1 and 7 are exact. The tangent model reaches the exact economy in two ways. First, at a stationary state the two models share their first-order conditions. Take a constant policy (i,\tau,d) and start the exact economy at stocks whose ratios that policy keeps constant, at the ratio x^*. Along that path every derivative the payoff gradient carries is a slope of (3.1) at \bar x=x^*, and the costate is constant and equal to the tangent model’s value coefficients, so the gradient of every agent’s exact payoff in (i,\tau,d) is the tangent model’s at \bar x=x^* (Lemma A.7). Hence where capital’s self-consistent choice — a constant policy that is optimal from the stationary state it produces — is interior, its exact direction is (5.2) at s_K(x^*). At that state, with capital’s rates held, the worker’s exact payoff has at capital’s direction the slope in d of her tangent payoff, whose sign is that of d_W-d_C in the tangent model, positive by Proposition 3. So the exact sign of d_W-d_C there is the tangent sign unless the worker’s exact payoff has more than one peak in d. The same holds for a fully state-contingent programme that has an optimal policy, at a rest point where that policy’s choice is interior. That choice, held constant, attains the programme’s value, so it is a self-consistent choice in the sense just given, and for capital the direction is (5.2) at s_K(x^*) (Lemma A.7(iii)). Second, numerically, the constant-policy maximisation on the exact reduction, at the cell \beta=0.9, \gamma=0.5, \theta_R=0.4, \theta_P=0.3 and \omega=0.2, with the linear frontier \varepsilon=1, gives d_W-d_C=0.051, 0.065 and 0.073 at the self-consistent ratio for \sigma=2,3,4, each with a single peak, and a positive sign from the non-stationary start k-n=g-n=0 as well. At \sigma=6 and \sigma=8 capital’s self-consistent choice is a corner: pure capital augmentation, a zero tax and a capital share of one, the endpoint at which its preferred tax is zero. Labour’s share is zero at those states, outside the box on which Propositions 4 and 5 are stated, so neither says anything there; the sign of d_W-d_C is again positive (0.052, 0.056).3

What the tangent loses. Two things that cancel or simplify in the tangent model do not in the exact one. The frontier’s curvature moves the exact directions of the two classes, though not their order at the curvatures computed: at \sigma=2 the self-consistent d_W is 0.584, 0.601 and 0.623 at \varepsilon=0.5, 1 and 2. And the market has a unique output-maximising direction exactly when \varepsilon(1-1/\sigma)<1, which holds at every elasticity below one and, on the linear frontier, at every finite elasticity. That direction is labour’s share at the ratio its own choice produces, not at the current ratio: at \sigma=2 and \varepsilon=1 it is 0.991, against s_L(x^*)=0.920. When \varepsilon(1-1/\sigma)>1 the direction equal to labour’s share minimises output, which rises towards both ends of the frontier, so no direction maximises it; when \varepsilon(1-1/\sigma)=1 output is monotone in d unless \theta_P K^{\rho}=(1-\theta_P)L^{\rho}, in which case every direction yields the same output. The order d_C<d_W<d_M holds in every interior case computed above one on the linear frontier; the distance to the market does not carry over.

An elasticity above one. Propositions 2–6, 8 and 9 are proved for 1<\sigma<\infty and Proposition 7 at \sigma=\infty, and every sentence of this paper that says which way an agent wants technology to go is read there unless it names the region below one. Below one the slopes a_1,a_2 stay in (0,1) only where s_K\in(1-\sigma,\sigma), which is empty for \sigma\le1/2. The proof of Lemma 1 as written, and every sign argument below, use that; outside the region the discounted sums of Lemma 1 can still converge, and the orderings there are not established. On the region \tfrac12<\sigma<1, s_K\in(1-\sigma,\sigma), the order of the weights survives: w_W>w_C (Proposition 3), 0<w_C<s_L (Proposition 4), w_W-s_L has the sign of \theta_R-\bar\theta (Proposition 5), w_S=w_C identically (Proposition 8(i)), w_\lambda between w_C and w_W with T_W,T_C>0 (Proposition 6), w_C<w_Y<s_L (Proposition 8(ii)), and w_Y-w_W has the sign of \theta_Y-\theta_R with \theta_Y<\bar\theta (Proposition 9), both thresholds as in (5.3) and (6.2). By (4.1) every difference between two directions, including an agent’s distance from the market and from capital’s income direction, is 1-1/\sigma times a difference of weights, and 1-1/\sigma<0 on the region. Thus every order of directions reverses: d_W<d_C, d_W\le d(\lambda)\le d_C, s_L<d_Y<d_C<s_L/\sigma, and the worker wants a technology less labour-augmenting than the market’s exactly when \theta_R>\bar\theta. Each of these orders holds on the whole region, together with A_i,B_i>0 and the entries of Lemma 1.4 The share effect of Section 2(ii) reverses at the same elasticity, so the reversal leaves unchanged which agent wants the technology that, at given stocks, gives labour the larger share. The exact-model computation above was repeated at the same cell for \sigma=0.6, 0.75 and 0.9. At each, capital’s self-consistent choice is interior and its share s_K(x^*) is 0.584, 0.482 and 0.372, inside (1-\sigma,\sigma), so the stationary state that capital’s own policy produces lies in the region. There d_W-d_C is -0.023, -0.017 and -0.008, with a single peak in the worker’s payoff, the same sign holds from the non-stationary start, and the exact order is d_M<d_W<d_C at all three, the reverse of the order above one. Generations, depreciation and objectives. One period is a generation, both stocks depreciate fully and every objective is logarithmic, and every statement above other than Proposition 1 is made there. On that domain, and against paths of the other instruments that do not respond to the state, every direction is a dominant strategy, free of the state and of the other instruments, so the results characterise what each agent wants, and the distances between them, without an assumption about what the agents know of one another’s paths. Against a state-contingent rule for i or \tau the transitions are in general no longer affine in the states, and no ranking here is claimed for any such rule.

Technology is a flow. The point on the frontier is chosen afresh each period and no knowledge accumulates.5

The worker’s objective is the size of her class, read as effective labour.6

The provision corner. Everything is stated at \varphi=1.7

Prior art. In Julius (2005, pp. 106, 115–117), on directed innovation, the wage bargain and the profits tax, and in Tavani and Zamparelli (2018), neither a class nor a state chooses a point on a two-factor frontier. In the models surveyed by Tavani and Zamparelli (2017), which covers the non-neoclassical literature, the chooser is a firm or a capitalist household, and no planner or government appears; Shah and Desai (1981) place induced technical change inside Goodwin’s cycle. The neoclassical line of induced innovation that the survey names (p. 17) includes an optimal rate and direction of technical change (Nordhaus 1967). The additions claimed here are the class and state choosers and the weight on reproduction that separates them.

9. Conclusion

Which way should technology go is a question the frontier literature answers with a point, the output-maximising one, and the induced-innovation tradition answers with a bias, capital’s. Where the factors are produced, both answers are special cases of one formula. Every agent’s direction is the capital-income direction plus the substitution gap times its weight on the reproduction of the workforce. Capital’s weight is positive — it hires the class that its workers’ income reproduces — and it is smaller than the market’s, so capital’s bias against labour, judged by the share a technology gives labour at given stocks, is real against the market and reversed against its own income. That holds for every finite elasticity above one and, below one, wherever capital’s share lies between 1-\sigma and \sigma; against the market the bias leans toward capital augmentation above one and toward labour augmentation below. The worker’s weight depends on how her class is reproduced. Above a threshold on the private share she wants a technology more biased toward labour than the market supplies, which above one is what the obvious answer says she wants. Below it she wants, as capital does, a technology more biased toward capital than the market supplies — because the fund that reproduces her class is built from capital’s income. A planner sits between the two; a state that lives on the tax on capital wants exactly what capital wants; a state that maximises output over time wants a technology more biased toward capital than the market’s; and at unit elasticity all of these coincide and the question cannot be asked.

The policy reading follows directly. A public fund for the reproduction of the workforce, financed from capital income, does something to the politics of technology that its size alone does not show: it aligns the working class’s interest in the direction of technical change with capital’s, past a threshold that depends on patience and the shares and on nothing else in the model. Past that threshold the classes agree on the direction and disagree only over how far to go in it. One coefficient thus turns the reproduction of the working class as a condition of the reproduction of capital from a statement about who pays into a statement about what each class wants the machines to do.

Appendix A. Proofs

Sections A.1–A.3 prove the three results about the economy that the body uses, at \varphi=1 and with the direction d as an instrument. The appendix then supplies the algebra behind Propositions 3, 8(ii) and 9, and A.7 shows that at a stationary state the tangent model and the exact model share their first-order conditions. Throughout, x=b+k-a-n, the production function is that of Section 2(ii), and a conjectured value function is written V=v+\mu_k k+\mu_n n+\mu_g g.

A.1. No affine value function off the two closure points.

Lemma A.1. Let \rho<1 and \rho\neq0. At any fixed direction, no function affine in (k,n,g) solves the exact Bellman equation of the owner, or of the worker, identically in the states. With the direction chosen, none does on any set of states on which the maximising direction is interior and satisfies the second-order condition strictly.

Proof. Substitute the exact transitions of Section 3 into felicity plus \beta V. Apart from affine terms in the states and additive terms in the instruments, the states enter only through the two composites \ln s_K(x)+\psi(x) and \ln s_L(x)+\psi(x), with coefficients M and N: for the owner M=(1-\omega)+\beta(\mu_k+\mu_g) and N=\beta\gamma\theta_R\mu_n; for the worker M=\beta(\mu_k+\mu_g) and the same N. Write \Phi(x)=M(\ln s_K+\psi)+N(\ln s_L+\psi). Since \psi'=s_K and s_K'=\rho s_Ks_L, the two composites have slopes \rho+(1-\rho)s_K and (1-\rho)s_K and the common second derivative \rho(1-\rho)s_Ks_L, so \Phi''(x)=\rho(1-\rho)\,s_K s_L\,(M+N). If M+N\neq0, \Phi is not affine in x, hence not affine in k, and no affine V satisfies the equation at a fixed direction. When the direction is chosen, write z=k-n, so that x=z+\varepsilon\ln[(1-d)/d] and the terms in d are \Phi(x)+(M+N)\varepsilon\ln d. At an interior maximiser the first-order condition is \Phi'(x^*)=(M+N)(1-d^*); where the second-order condition is strict, the implicit function theorem makes d^* and x^* differentiable in z, and the envelope theorem gives W''(z)=\Phi''(x^*)\,\partial x^*/\partial z for the maximised value W. That derivative cannot vanish: differentiating the first-order condition, \partial x^*/\partial z=0 forces \partial d^*/\partial z=0, and then \partial x^*/\partial z=1. So W is not affine either. It remains to show that M+N=0 is impossible. Suppose it holds. Then \Phi=M(\ln s_K-\ln s_L)=M\bigl[\ln\tfrac{\theta_P}{1-\theta_P}+\rho x\bigr] is affine, and the coefficients must match.

For the owner, matching k, n and g gives \mu_k=\omega+\rho M, \mu_n=(1-\rho)M+\beta\mu_n\bigl(1-\gamma(1-\theta_R)\bigr) and \mu_g=\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)\mu_n. With M=-N=-\beta\gamma\theta_R\mu_n the second equation reads \mu_n\bigl[1-\beta+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)+(1-\rho)\beta\gamma\theta_R\bigr]=0, and the bracket is positive for \rho<1. So \mu_n=0, hence M=0 and \mu_g=0, hence \mu_k=\omega and M=(1-\omega)+\beta\omega>0, a contradiction.

For the worker, matching gives \mu_k=\rho M, \mu_n=1+(1-\rho)M+\beta\mu_n\bigl(1-\gamma(1-\theta_R)\bigr) and \mu_g=\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)\mu_n. With M=-\beta\gamma\theta_R\mu_n the second equation reads \mu_n\bigl[1-\beta+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)+(1-\rho)\beta\gamma\theta_R\bigr]=1, so \mu_n>0 and M<0. But M=\beta(\mu_k+\mu_g)=\beta\rho M+\beta^2\gamma(1-\theta_R)\mu_n gives M(1-\beta\rho)=\beta^2\gamma(1-\theta_R)\mu_n>0, and 1-\beta\rho>0, so M>0, a contradiction. Since s_Ks_L>0, \Phi''\neq0 whenever \rho(1-\rho)\neq0. \square

At \rho=0 both composites are affine with slope \alpha. At \rho=1 factor incomes are linear in the factors, s_KY=\theta_PBK and s_LY=(1-\theta_P)AL, so \ln s_K+\psi=x+\ln\theta_P and \ln s_L+\psi=\ln(1-\theta_P) are both affine. These are the only two points at which the exact model closes.

A.2. Perfect substitutes.

Lemma A.2. At \rho=1 the tangent model is exact, with a_1=1 and a_2=0, and the owner’s coefficient on the worker stock is a_n=0.

Proof. At \rho=1, E_C=\ln\theta_P+b+k, so the owner’s felicity, k' and g' are free of n and of a, and (3.1) holds exactly with a_1=1, a_2=0. The n-equation of the owner’s coefficient matching, which is the system of Lemma A.3 and whose derivation does not use a_1,a_2\in(0,1), then reads a_n=\beta a_n\bigl(1-\gamma(1-\theta_R)\bigr), since 1-a_1=0 and D=\gamma(1-\theta_R), so a_n=0. \square

A.3. The coefficient systems.

Lemma A.3. Under (3.1) with a_1,a_2\in(0,1), and with D=\gamma(1-\theta_R)+\gamma\theta_Ra_2 as in Lemma 1, the owner’s and the worker’s value-function coefficients solve \begin{aligned} a_k&=\omega+M_Ca_1+\beta a_n\gamma\theta_Ra_2, &\qquad p&=\beta\bigl[(p+r)a_1+q\gamma\theta_Ra_2\bigr],\\ a_n&=M_C(1-a_1)+\beta a_n(1-D), &\qquad q&=1+\beta\bigl[(p+r)(1-a_1)+q(1-D)\bigr],\\ a_g&=\beta a_n\gamma(1-\theta_R), &\qquad r&=\beta q\gamma(1-\theta_R), \end{aligned} with M_C=(1-\omega)+\beta(a_k+a_g). Each system has a unique solution, in which M_C=\frac{(1-\omega+\beta\omega)(1-\beta+\beta D)}{(1-\beta)(1-\beta a_1+\beta D)},\qquad a_n=\frac{M_C(1-a_1)}{1-\beta+\beta D},\qquad q=\frac{1-\beta a_1}{(1-\beta)(1-\beta a_1+\beta D)},\qquad p+r=\frac{\beta qD}{1-\beta a_1}, all positive; and the weights of (4.1) are those of (5.1).

Proof. The systems are the coefficient matching of Lemma A.1 with the two composites replaced by their tangents (3.1), the direction entering only through terms in the instruments. For the owner, adding the k- and g-equations gives a_k+a_g=\omega+M_Ca_1+\beta a_nD, so M_C(1-\beta a_1)=1-\omega+\beta\omega+\beta^2a_nD; the n-equation gives a_n(1-\beta+\beta D)=M_C(1-a_1); eliminating a_n gives M_C. For the worker, adding the p- and r-equations gives (p+r)(1-\beta a_1)=\beta qD, and substituting into the q-equation gives q. Every factor is positive when a_1,a_2\in(0,1). By (3.2), M_C and N_C=\beta\gamma\theta_Ra_n give w_C=\frac{N_C}{M_C+N_C}=\frac{\beta\gamma\theta_R(1-a_1)}{1-\beta+\beta D+\beta\gamma\theta_R(1-a_1)}=\frac{\beta\gamma\theta_R\,s_L/\sigma}{(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R)}, using \beta D+\beta\gamma\theta_R(1-a_1)=\beta\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R) and (2.1). For the worker, M_W=\beta(p+r) and N_W=\beta\gamma\theta_Rq give N_W/M_W=\gamma\theta_R(1-\beta a_1)/(\beta D), and dividing through by \gamma, w_W=\frac{\theta_R(1-\beta a_1)}{\beta(1-\theta_R+\theta_Ra_2)+\theta_R(1-\beta a_1)}=\frac{\theta_R(1-\beta a_1)}{\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta\rho)}, using a_1-a_2=\rho. That these coefficients are the discounted sums of the agents’ objectives is Lemma 1. \square

A.4. Proposition 3. With H=\beta(1-\theta_R)+\theta_R(1-\beta\rho), \Lambda=(1-\beta)+\beta\gamma(1-\rho\theta_R) and Q=(1-\beta a_1)+\beta\gamma\bigl(1-\theta_R(1-a_2)\bigr), (w_W-w_C)\,H\,\Lambda\;=\;(1-\beta)\,\theta_R\,Q . By Lemma A.3, w_W=\theta_R(1-\beta a_1)/H and w_C=\beta\gamma\theta_R(1-a_1)/\Lambda, so the identity is (1-\beta a_1)\Lambda-\beta\gamma(1-a_1)H=(1-\beta)Q, which follows on expanding with a_2=a_1-\rho.

A.5. Proposition 8(ii). Under (3.1), \ln Y=\text{const}+s_L a+s_K b+s_K k+s_L n, and the output state’s coefficients (y_k,y_n,y_g) solve the system of Lemma A.3 with the owner’s felicity loadings replaced by (s_K,s_L,0): y_k=s_K+\beta\bigl[(y_k+y_g)a_1+y_n\gamma\theta_Ra_2\bigr],\quad y_n=s_L+\beta\bigl[(y_k+y_g)(1-a_1)+y_n(1-D)\bigr],\quad y_g=\beta y_n\gamma(1-\theta_R). Then (w_Y-s_L)\,J=\beta s_L\bigl[\gamma\bigl(\theta_R(\beta s_L+s_K)-\beta\bigr)-(1-\beta)s_K\bigr],\qquad (w_Y-w_C)\,J\,\Lambda=(1-\beta)\,s_L\,(1-\beta+\beta\gamma)\,Q . A.7. The tangent model at a stationary state.

Lemma A.7. Let \rho\in(0,1). In the exact model of Section 3, let one of the agents of Sections 4 to 6 follow a constant policy u=(i,\tau,d)\in(0,1)^3 from stocks whose ratios k-n and g-n that policy keeps constant, so that x_t=x^* at every date. (i) The agent’s exact payoff is continuously differentiable in u, and its gradient equals the gradient in u of the agent’s payoff in the tangent model with \bar x=x^*. (ii) If u maximises the owner’s exact payoff over constant policies from those stocks, it satisfies the tangent model’s first-order conditions at \bar x=x^*, and its direction is (5.2) at s_K(x^*). (iii) If instead, with any instrument the agent does not choose held constant, an optimal state-contingent policy of the agent’s is interior at those stocks and its choice there keeps the ratios constant, then that choice, held constant, attains the agent’s exact value from those stocks. It therefore satisfies the tangent model’s first-order conditions at \bar x=x^*, and for the owner its direction is (5.2) at s_K(x^*). The vector \lambda^* of tangent value coefficients at \bar x=x^* satisfies V(s)\ge V(s^*)+\lambda^*\cdot(s-s^*)+o(|s-s^*|), where V is the agent’s exact value and s^* those stocks, so it is the gradient of V there wherever V is differentiable.

Proof. Write s=(k,n,g), \mathbf 1=(1,1,1), F(s,u) for the agent’s period objective and f(s,u) for the exact transitions. Two facts carry the proof. First, adding c to all three logged stocks leaves x unchanged and adds c to k', n', g', and adds a fixed multiple \kappa c to every period objective of Sections 4 to 6: (1-\omega)\ln C_C+\omega k, n, g, \ln Y and their non-negative combinations. Second, apart from terms linear in the stocks, the stocks and the direction enter only through E_C and E_W, and by the slopes computed in Lemma A.1, \partial E_C/\partial(k,n)=(a_1,1-a_1) and \partial E_C/\partial d=a_1\,b'(d)+(1-a_1)\,a'(d), with a_2 in place of a_1 for E_W, where a_1=\rho+(1-\rho)s_K(x) and a_2=(1-\rho)s_K(x) are the slopes of Section 2(i) at the current x; and \ln Y=a+n+\psi(x) has slopes (s_K,s_L) in (k,n) and s_La'(d)+s_Kb'(d) in d, those of A.5 with \bar x=x. So the partial derivatives F_s, f_s, F_u and f_u depend on the stocks only through x and at x are the tangent model’s with \bar x=x. In particular f_s is the matrix of Lemma 1 with \bar x=x, which has non-negative entries and unit row sums at every x.

  1. By the chain rule, summed by parts, \partial U/\partial u=\sum_{t\ge0}\beta^t\,[F_u(s_t,u)+\beta f_u(s_t,u)^{\top}\lambda_{t+1}] with \lambda_t=F_s(s_t,u)+\beta f_s(s_t,u)^{\top}\lambda_{t+1}, taken as its bounded forward sum. Because every f_s has non-negative entries and unit row sums, the exact map is non-expansive in the largest-coordinate norm. So, for u in a compact subset of the interior, the stocks stay within a distance linear in t of the given path and their derivatives in u grow at most linearly in t; the payoff and the series above converge uniformly, and U is continuously differentiable. On the given path s_{t+1}=s_t+\mu\mathbf 1 for a constant \mu and x_t=x^*, so every coefficient is constant and \lambda_t=\lambda^*, the solution of \lambda^*=F_s+\beta f_s^{\top}\lambda^* at x^*. The solution is unique, because \det(I-\beta f_s^{\top})=(1-\beta)[1-\beta(a_1-D)]>0 by Lemma 1. That equation is the coefficient matching of the tangent model at \bar x=x^* — for the owner and the worker, the systems of Lemma A.3 — so \lambda^* is the vector of tangent value coefficients, and \partial U/\partial u=[F_u+\beta f_u^{\top}\lambda^*]/(1-\beta) at x^*, which is the gradient of the tangent payoff at \bar x=x^*.

  2. An interior maximiser has a zero gradient, so by (i) it satisfies the tangent first-order conditions at \bar x=x^*. The one in d is the first-order condition of (3.2), whose unique solution is (3.3), that is (5.2) at s_K(x^*).

  3. Let V be the agent’s exact value, s^* the stocks and u^* the policy’s choice there, so that the next stocks are s^*+\mu\mathbf 1. V is finite. Every augmentation factor is at most one and \rho>0, so \ln Y\le\max(k,n); hence E_C, E_W, k' and g' are at most \max(k,n), n' is a convex combination of n, E_W and g, the largest of the three logged stocks never rises, and every period objective is at most \kappa\max(k_0,n_0,g_0), so V is bounded above; and V(s^*) is at least the finite payoff of u^* held constant. By the first fact, raising all three initial stocks by c raises the payoff of every sequence of choices by \kappa c/(1-\beta), so V(s+c\mathbf 1)=V(s)+\kappa c/(1-\beta), and F(s,u)+\beta V(f(s,u))-V(s) is unchanged when s rises by c\mathbf 1. The first choice of an optimal policy satisfies V(s^*)=F(s^*,u^*)+\beta V(f(s^*,u^*)), because what the policy does from the next stocks on is optimal from them. So the same equation holds with the same u^* at every s_t=s^*+t\mu\mathbf 1, which is the path of u^* held constant. Summing it over t<T gives V(s^*)=\sum_{t<T}\beta^tF(s_t,u^*)+\beta^TV(s_T), and \beta^TV(s_T)=\beta^T[V(s^*)+\kappa\mu T/(1-\beta)]\to0. Hence the payoff of u^* held constant is V(s^*), which is at least the payoff of every constant policy from s^*: u^* is an interior maximiser over constant policies, so its gradient in the agent’s instruments is zero, which by (i) gives the tangent first-order conditions at \bar x=x^*; for the owner, (ii) applies. As in (i), the payoff of u^* held constant is differentiable in the initial stocks, because the derivatives of later stocks in the initial ones are products of matrices with non-negative entries and unit row sums; at s^* its gradient is \lambda^*. It lies below V and equals it at s^*, so V(s)\ge V(s^*)+\lambda^*\cdot(s-s^*)+o(|s-s^*|), and where V is differentiable at s^* its gradient there is \lambda^*. \square
