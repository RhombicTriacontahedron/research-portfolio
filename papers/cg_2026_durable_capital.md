## Abstract

Would the owner of capital drive the capital-income tax to zero once machines last, if its revenue paid for the schools, clinics and other public stock that reproduce the workforce he hires? The paper answers in a two-class growth model in which labour is not owned. Next period’s workforce moves, by a law nobody optimises, towards a mix of this period’s wages and the public stock. The owner sets the tax and nothing else. At unit elasticity of substitution, with the capital-income tax as the only instrument, he never sets it below an explicit positive floor. The floor is written in his discount factor, his weight on holding capital, labour’s share and the public stock’s weight in reproducing labour, and it holds at every state and every rate of wear. Where his chosen tax is not raising the capital stock, a sharper floor applies. It rises as machines become more durable, because more of next period’s machines are inherited rather than bought out of taxed profit. Along his own path, wherever capital settles, the long-run tax obeys it. Whoever chooses it, a tax held below a positive threshold empties the public stock, then the workforce, then capital income. That holds at unit elasticity, and above it exactly when capital at the limit of deepening, reinvested at its fixed share, cannot cover depreciation. The floor belongs to the instrument. Given a wage tax as well, and with machines lasting one period, the owner funds the same stock out of wages and leaves capital income untaxed.

## Introduction

The best-known result in the theory of capital taxation is that, in the long run, the tax on capital income should be zero. Chamley (1986) and Judd (1985) established it in economies where infinitely lived agents, or a dynasty of capitalists, save optimally and a planner chooses taxes. The revenue in those papers pays for government consumption or for transfers. It is not an input into the labour that capital employs.

Suppose instead that it is. A tax on capital income fills a public stock: schools, clinics, the institutions that reproduce a workforce. Labour is not owned. Nobody holds it as an asset and nobody chooses how much of it to accumulate; next period’s workforce simply moves, by a law nobody optimises, towards a mix of this period’s wages and that public stock. The person who sets the tax is not a planner who weighs workers. It is the owner of the capital that hires the labour. The owner sets the tax each period and nothing else. The share of profit he reinvests is held fixed, and so is the direction of technique. The tax he chooses, as the solution of his own dynamic programme, is the *assigned* tax. Capital is owned; successor labour, the workforce that law produces for next period, is not.

Durability is what makes the question live. When machines last one period, a take from current profit and a tax on capital income are the same thing. When some machines survive, the two split: the tax still fills the public stock, but surviving machines no longer have to be rebuilt out of after-tax profit. An owner who inherits most of next period’s machines might seem to have less reason to care about anything done with this period’s profit, the public stock included. Would he then drive the capital-income tax to zero?

At the Cobb–Douglas technology of the textbook growth model the answer is no, and it is stronger than no. At Cobb–Douglas the owner never sets the tax below an explicit positive floor, at any stock of machines, any stock of labour, any public stock and any rate at which machines wear out (Proposition 10). On that face, where the tax he chooses does not raise the capital stock, durability *raises* the floor: more of next period’s machines are inherited rather than bought, so the tax bites a smaller part of them, funding the public stock becomes cheaper, and the lowest tax the owner will tolerate goes up (Proposition 11). Along his own path, wherever capital settles, the long-run tax obeys that sharper floor. The uniform floor is small, about two per cent at a standard illustration, but it is positive and it does not move with the state. At Cobb–Douglas, every feasible path on which the tax stays below a positive threshold from some date on, chosen by the owner or not, empties the public stock, then the workforce, then capital income itself (Proposition 5). Above Cobb–Douglas the same is true exactly when capital, left to itself at the limit of deepening and reinvested at its fixed share, cannot cover depreciation (Proposition 5$'$).[^1] Durability undoes none of these statements. The same accounting caps the tax at every state, and the cap can bind: under conditions on the parameters, the owner’s own optimal path runs down an economy that a constant tax he could have levied would have grown (Propositions 12 and 14).

The floor belongs to the instrument. If the owner may also tax wages, he still funds the public stock, but out of wages, and sets the capital-income tax to zero (Proposition 15, with machines lasting one period). Owners will not let the workforce go unreproduced; given the chance, they make labour pay for its own reproduction.[^2]

### Related literature

The standard reading of Chamley and Judd’s zero is intertemporal. A tax on capital income is a tax on future consumption relative to current consumption, and over an infinite horizon that distortion compounds; a labour tax hits each period’s margin once. With time to smooth, the planner raises revenue from labour and leaves intertemporal prices alone. Three features of that setup separate it from the one here. The revenue does not produce the labour that capital will hire tomorrow: in Chamley (1986) it pays for a given stream of government consumption, and in Judd (1985) for government consumption and transfers to workers who hold no assets. The chooser is a planner: Judd’s may weigh workers, and Chamley’s maximises a representative agent’s welfare; neither is the owner of the machines acting in his own interest. And savers choose how much to save, which is the margin behind the commitment problem that surrounds the result: once capital is in place the planner would like to tax it, and only a planner who can bind himself keeps the long-run tax at zero.

Where public spending is instead a productive input, the case for taxing capital changes. Financing a public input with a capital tax can be efficient, though not when producing the input has economies of scale (Matsumoto and Feehan 2010); taxing capital can be optimal even when a labour tax is available (Feehan and Batina 2007); and when publicly provided education and health care drive growth and population is endogenous, the second-best taxes on capital and labour income turn on whether that input is optimally provided (Renström and Spataro 2021). In each of these a government sets the tax. The question here is not whether a planner should tax capital to finance a public input. It is whether the owner of capital will tax himself to finance the reproduction of labour he does not own.

Judd (1999) is the nearest neighbour on spending. His spending is chosen and enters utility and production, human capital is accumulated privately by the agent who owns it, the planner can tax wages as well as capital, and the long-run capital tax is still zero: “rational government expenditure does not imply positive taxation of capital income” (p. 17). Here the spending is an input into a labour force nobody owns and the capital tax alone funds it. Once the owner can also tax wages and machines last one period, Judd’s zero returns, reached from the owner’s side (Proposition 15). Straub and Werning (2020) show that inside Judd’s (1985) model the long-run tax is positive when the intertemporal elasticity of substitution is below one, and at unit elasticity whenever redistribution towards workers is desirable. That tax is chosen by a planner who weighs workers; the assigned tax is the owner’s own. None of these papers gives the owner of capital the tax instrument, makes the revenue the only source of a public stock that reproduces unowned labour, and then asks what he will do when machines last.

Galor and Moav (2006) are the nearest neighbour on motive. Their capitalists support a tax spent on workers’ schooling because educated workers complement their capital; their capital lasts one generation, and their tax is zero until capital per worker passes a critical level. With the Cobb–Douglas technology both papers share, the owner here never takes the tax below the floor, at any state and any rate of wear. Acemoglu, Gitmez and Shadmehr (2026) share the chooser and the single instrument: a state run by capital owners taxes capital and pays workers, to forestall revolt. Here there is no revolt. The owner funds the labour he hires because that labour is an input into his own future profit.

Other established routes to a nonzero long-run capital tax run through a non-linear schedule (Saez 2013), through non-insurable labour-income risk among ex ante identical households (Jacobs and Schindler 2012), and through the distortion a labour tax imposes on privately chosen investment in human capital (Jacobs and Bovenberg 2010). The floor here holds with a linear tax, no risk, two classes that are not ex ante identical and no private investment in labour. What delivers it is what the revenue buys and which instrument may pay for it.

### Outline

*Environment* sets out the two classes, the three stocks that move from one period to the next (machines, the public stock and labour) and who chooses what. *Laws that survive* records the two facts that do not depend on wear. A zero tax empties the public stock at every rate of wear, and once some capital survives the owner’s value loses the closed form it has when capital lasts one period. *Stationary assigned tax* writes the owner’s exact first-order condition. In it, next period’s public stock is valued through its weight in reproducing labour at every rate of wear. When capital lasts one period, at Cobb–Douglas, the condition gives the same tax at every state, and that constant is the owner’s unique optimal plan. *Paths, not only stationary points* turns to the object Chamley and Judd study, a tax path. Without any optimality condition it shows when a vanishing tax empties the economy, and at Cobb–Douglas it shows that a zero tax at a single date is never the owner’s choice. *Interior policy at a given state* shows that at Cobb–Douglas the owner’s best response exists at every state, so he neither shuts down nor confiscates. *A floor that does not move with the state* proves the floor and the sharper floor under durability, then runs the same argument downwards to cap the tax. The band between the two can exclude every rest point of the economy, and under a further condition the owner’s own path runs down an economy that a constant tax would grow. *Stationary first-order condition* shows that the substitutions that would give the owner’s tax at a rest point as a closed form in the rate of wear are not available. *Contact with Chamley and Judd* sets the floor against the zero: what the contact rests on, why the floor does not rest on commitment, what a wage tax does to it, and how the two models nearest in motive and in chooser differ. The *Conclusion* draws three readings of the result.

## Environment

The economy has two classes that do not change size. Owners hold the machines. Workers supply labour and own nothing that stores value. Three stocks move from one period to the next. A fraction $\delta$ of the machines wears out; the owner consumes a fixed share of after-tax capital income and reinvests the rest, and holding that share fixed makes his problem a single choice, the tax. A tax on capital income fills a public stock $G$, and nothing else does: depreciation does not fill it, so a zero tax is a zero public stock whether machines last a year or a century. Next period’s workforce is chosen by no one. It moves towards a weighted average, in logs, of this period’s wage bill and this period’s public stock, and the weight $\gamma(1-\theta_R)$ on the public stock is what makes that stock an input into the reproduction of labour; where the weight is zero, every floor below is zero. The owner cares about his own consumption and, with some weight, about the machines he holds. Workers neither save nor vote on the tax.

Owners hold $K$; workers supply $L$. Production is CES with elasticity $\sigma$. Write $x=k-n$ for the logged effective capital–labour ratio and $\ln Y=a+n+\psi(x)$ for output, with
$$
\psi(x)=\rho_\sigma^{-1}\ln\bigl(\theta e^{\rho_\sigma x}+1-\theta\bigr),\qquad \rho_\sigma=\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma},\qquad s_K(x)=\psi'(x),\qquad s_L=1-s_K,
$$
where $\theta$ is the distribution parameter of the technology, $a$ is constant given the direction $d$, and $\bar x$ is the ratio at which the shares are reported. At $\sigma=1$, $\psi(x)=\alpha x$ and $(s_K,s_L)=(\alpha,1-\alpha)$, and the $\sigma=1$ displays below also set $a=0$.[^3] For $\sigma>1$ the shares move with the state. Logged income composites $E_C=\ln s_K+\psi+a+n$ and $E_W=\ln s_L+\psi+a+n$. Direction $d$, investment share $i$, capital-income tax $\tau$, in-kind share $\varphi$ (the fraction of revenue that enters the public stock). Worker felicity $n$. Owner felicity $(1-\omega)\ln C_C+\omega k$. Common discount factor $\beta\in(0,1)$. Labour law:

$$
n'=n+\gamma\theta_R(E_W-n)+\gamma(1-\theta_R)(g-n).
$$

**Consumption, capital and the public stock.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln C_C&=\ln(1-i)+\ln(1-\tau)+E_C,\\
K'&=(1-\delta)K+i(1-\tau)e^{E_C},\\
g'&=\ln(\varphi\tau)+E_C.
\end{aligned}
$$

At $\delta=1$, $K'=i(1-\tau)e^{E_C}$. Tax formulas below that recover a one-generation peak are written at $\varphi=1$. The same corner is used here unless a sentence names the split.

Throughout, $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\theta_R,i\in(0,1)$, $\omega\in[0,1)$ and $\delta\in(0,1]$. This is the *licensed box*. Above the Cobb–Douglas face it also contains the distribution parameter, $\theta\in(0,1)$: at $\theta=0$ or $\theta=1$ one factor’s share vanishes identically, and the logged shares and the constants of Appendix A.1, which carry $\ln\theta$ and $\ln(1-\theta)$, are not finite. A statement that names a narrower range, as Lemma 2 does for $\delta$, restricts it.

**Who chooses.** One player sets one instrument. The owner chooses $\tau$ each period, knowing the state $(K,n,g)$. The direction $d$, which the literature on directed technical change treats as a choice (Kennedy 1964; Acemoglu 2002), the investment share $i$ and the in-kind share $\varphi$ are held fixed, and workers choose nothing: they hold no assets, and their stock $n$ moves by the labour law. The owner’s problem is therefore a single-agent dynamic programme, and the *assigned* tax is its solution — a policy $\tau(K,n,g)$, or the path that policy generates from a given start. It is not the equilibrium set of a game in which workers or a government also choose, and it is not a Ramsey plan. The worker peak $\tau_W$ displayed below is the one-generation analogue with the instrument assigned to workers.

## Laws that survive

Two facts about this environment do not depend on how fast capital wears out. The public stock is filled by the tax alone, so a zero tax empties it at every rate of wear; and once some capital survives, owner value stops being affine in logged capital, so the closed form that makes the assigned tax a constant when capital lasts one period (Proposition 2) is no longer available.

**Lemma 1 (Public stock).** $G'=\varphi\tau e^{E_C}$ does not depend on $\delta$. For every fixed $\varphi\in(0,1]$, $\tau=0$ implies $G'=0$ for every $\delta\in(0,1]$.

**Lemma 2 (Loss of affinity).** For $\delta\in(0,1)$,

$$
k'=\ln\bigl((1-\delta)e^{k}+i(1-\tau)e^{E_C}\bigr)
$$

is not an affine function of $k$. Affine-in-logs closure of the owner’s Bellman equation therefore fails even on the Cobb–Douglas face $\sigma=1$, where the composites $E_C$, $E_W$ remain affine.

*Proof.* At $\sigma=1$ the composite moves with $k$: write $E_C=\ln\alpha+\alpha k+(1-\alpha)n$, $R=(1-\delta)e^{k}$ and $C=i(1-\tau)\alpha e^{\alpha k+(1-\alpha)n}$, so that $k'=\ln(R+C)$. Then

$$
\frac{\partial^2 k'}{\partial k^2}=\frac{RC(1-\alpha)^2}{(R+C)^2}>0
\qquad\bigl(\delta<1,\ \alpha<1,\ i(1-\tau)>0\bigr),
$$

so $k'$ is strictly convex in $k$. Non-affinity of the transition is not on its own a failure of closure; Appendix A.5 completes the step by showing that no affine-in-logs $V$ solves the Bellman equation at any $\delta<1$. $\square$

## Stationary assigned tax

The owner’s first-order condition below holds at any interior best response at which his value is differentiable, whether or not the economy is at rest. At $\delta=1$ and $\sigma=1$ it gives the same tax at every state (Proposition 2). A stationary peak is a further object: a best response at a rest point of the economy.

A stationary point is a triple $(\tau,i,d)$ and a ratio $\bar x$ such that $K'=K$ and the labour and public-stock laws hold with constant $n$ and $g$. Capital stationarity reads

$$
\delta K=i(1-\tau)e^{E_C}.
$$

Hold $(d,i)$ fixed. States are $(K,n,g)$. Timing: $\tau$ enters $g'$ and $K'$, not $n'$.

$$
V(K,n,g)=\max_{\tau\in(0,1)}\bigl\{(1-\omega)\ln C_C+\omega\ln K+\beta V(K',n',g')\bigr\}.
$$

$V$ is the value of the sequence problem: the supremum over $\{\tau_t\}\subset(0,1)$ of $\sum_t\beta^t[(1-\omega)\ln C_{C,t}+\omega k_t]$ from the given state. The transition map is monotone and additively homogeneous, so every plan satisfies $\max(k_t,n_t,g_t)\le\max(k_0,n_0,g_0)+t\bar M^+$ with $\bar M$ the constant of Appendix A.4 at $\sigma=1$ and of Appendix A.1 above it. Each plan’s payoff is therefore bounded above by an affine function of $t$, its discounted sum is well defined in $[-\infty,\infty)$, and at a finite state the supremum is finite. Theorems 4.2 and 4.4 of Stokey, Lucas and Prescott (1989) then give the Bellman equation above, and give for every optimal plan that $\tau_t$ maximises its right-hand side at the date-$t$ state — which is what the path statements use. Those statements are not empty: at $\sigma=1$, choosing a maximiser at each date, which Proposition 6 supplies, generates an optimal plan from every finite state (end of Appendix A.4).

**Proposition 1 (Exact first-order condition).** At an interior optimum at which $V$ is differentiable at the continuation state, with $I:=i(1-\tau)e^{E_C}$,

$$
\frac{\tau}{1-\tau}=\frac{\beta V_g(K',n',g')}{(1-\omega)+\beta I V_K(K',n',g')}.
$$

The envelope is $V_g(K,n,g)=\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)V_n(K',n',g')$. On the licensed box $\gamma(1-\theta_R)>0$. If $V_n>0$ at $(K'',n'',g'')$ and $V_K\ge 0$ at $(K',n',g')$, the right-hand side is strictly positive and that candidate is interior.

*Proof.* Current utility contributes $-(1-\omega)/(1-\tau)$. State derivatives: $K_\tau'=-I/(1-\tau)$, $g_\tau'=1/\tau$, $n_\tau'=0$. Multiply through by $\tau(1-\tau)$. The envelope in $g$ uses $n'_g=\gamma(1-\theta_R)$ and $K'_g=g'_g=0$. $\square$

In the denominator $\beta IV_K=\beta\chi\,K'V_K$, with $\chi:=I/K'$ the share of next period’s capital bought out of after-tax profit. At $\delta<1$ that share equals $\delta$ when the tax leaves capital stationary and is below $\delta$ when capital falls; at $\delta=1$ it is one. That is the channel Proposition 11 prices. Nothing here signs the comparative static of the maximiser itself in $\delta$, because $V_g$ and $V_K$ move with $\delta$ as well.

When machines last one period the condition can be solved. The one-generation peaks

$$
\tau_C=\frac{\beta^2\gamma(1-\theta_R)\,(s_L/\sigma)}{1-\beta+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)+\beta\gamma\theta_R(s_K/\sigma)},
\qquad
\tau_W=\frac{(1-\theta_R)\bigl(1-\beta+\beta s_L/\sigma\bigr)}{1-\theta_R+\theta_R(s_K/\sigma)}
$$

are the solutions at $\delta=1$ under log-affine transitions: $\tau_C$ for the owner, and $\tau_W$ for a holder of the instrument whose felicity is $n$. Appendix A.7 derives both, exactly at $\sigma=1$ and, above that face, with the partial derivatives of the composites held at the reported ratio $\bar x$. A representative-agent public-share formula (Barro 1990) is one number. At $\delta=1$ this economy has a tax interval $[\tau_C,\tau_W]$, and which end obtains depends on who holds the instrument.

**Proposition 2 (Recovery).** At $\delta=1$, under the log-affine expansion, the first-order condition of Proposition 1 has the same solution at every state, the displayed $\tau_C$, and on the domain $\theta_R\in(0,1)$, $\gamma\in(0,1)$ that peak is strictly positive. At $\sigma=1$ the expansion is exact: the owner’s value is affine in logs, $\tau_C$ is his unique best response at every state, and the constant plan $\tau_t=\tau_C$ is the unique assigned-optimal plan from every finite state (Appendix A.7).

The peak $\tau_C$ needs no rest point of the economy, and there is none to be had: with the constants of Lemma 6’s displays no stationary point has $i<1$ once $\delta\ge\alpha$, and in particular none at $\delta=1$.

**Conjecture 1.** At $\sigma=1$, for every $\delta\in(0,1]$ at which every tax in the band $[\bar\tau,\hat\tau]$ of Propositions 10 and 12 has a stationary point with $i\in(0,1)$, there exists a stationary assigned peak $\tau_C(\delta)\in(0,1)$.

The band hypothesis cannot be weakened to “some stationary point has $i\in(0,1)$”. Under a condition on the parameters, Proposition 13 below gives an interval of $\delta$ at which such stationary points exist and none of them is a peak, because every one of them needs a tax above any the owner chooses. On the band the owner’s best response maps the band into itself, at every state, which is the hypothesis a fixed-point argument needs and not yet the argument.[^4]

**Conjecture 2.** $\liminf_{\delta\to 0}\tau_C(\delta)>0$ whenever $\theta_R$ is bounded away from $1$ and $\gamma>0$. At $\sigma=1$, Propositions 10 and 11 below bound every such peak that exists, with an explicit constant and without passing to the limit.

Chamley (1986) and Judd (1985) are statements about $t\to\infty$ at fixed $\delta$. The next section treats that limit.

## Paths, not only stationary points

Once $\delta<1$, Lemma 2 removes the closed form that made the assigned programme constant, so the object to study is a tax *path*. Chamley–Judd is a claim about one: $\tau_t\to 0$ as $t\to\infty$. That is the object to accept or reject for the assigned owner. The emptying results of this section, Propositions 3, 5 and 5$'$, use no optimality condition; they are statements about the technology. Because the public stock is filled only by the tax, a path on which the tax tends to zero and capital income does not explode is a path on which the public stock tends to zero. Labour chases the public stock, so labour then collapses, and at Cobb–Douglas labour is essential, so capital income collapses with it. A society that stops taxing capital income, in this environment, stops reproducing its workforce and then stops having capital income to tax. Above Cobb–Douglas the same logic needs one extra number, because capital can then replace itself out of its own income.

Write $\rho:=1-\gamma\in(0,1)$ for the own-lag in labour when $E_W$ is carried as an input, and $\eta:=\gamma(1-\theta_R)>0$ on the licensed box. The three weights $\rho$, $\gamma\theta_R$ and $\eta$ sum to one, so a common value of $n$, $E_W$ and $g$ is a rest point. Then

$$
n_{t+1}=\rho n_t+\gamma\theta_R E_{W,t}+\eta g_t.
$$

**Lemma 3 (Public-stock divergence).** Let $\varphi=1$. If $\tau_t\to 0$ and $\{E_{C,t}\}$ is bounded above, then $g_{t+1}=\ln\tau_t+E_{C,t}\to-\infty$.

**Lemma 4 (Labour collapse).** If $g_t\to-\infty$ and $\{E_{W,t}\}$ is bounded above, then $n_t\to-\infty$.

*Proof.* Iterate the labour law. The coefficient on $g_{t-k}$ is $\eta\rho^{k-1}$. If $g\to-\infty$, the moving average of $g$ goes to $-\infty$. A bounded $E_W$ cannot offset it. $\square$

**Lemma 5 (Essential labour at Cobb–Douglas).** Let $\sigma=1$. If $n_t\to-\infty$ and $\{k_t\}$ is bounded above, then $E_{C,t}\to-\infty$.

*Proof.* At $\sigma=1$, $E_{C,t}=\ln\alpha+\alpha k_t+(1-\alpha)n_t$. With $k_t\le\bar k$ the right-hand side is at most $\ln\alpha+\alpha\bar k+(1-\alpha)n_t$, which tends to $-\infty$. $\square$

For $\sigma>1$ there is no such statement to be had. Write $h(x)=\ln s_K(x)+\psi(x)-x$, so that capital income is $e^{a+h(x)}K$. Then $h'(x)=s_L(x)(\rho_\sigma-1)<0$, so $h$ decreases to $\rho_\sigma^{-1}\ln\theta$ and capital income is at least $\underline AK$ with $\underline A=\theta^{1/\rho_\sigma}e^{a}>0$ at *every* state. A collapsing labour input therefore does not carry capital income down at a bounded capital stock, and $\lim_{L\to0}Y=\underline AK>0$: labour is inessential. What replaces Lemma 5 in Proposition 3 is the compact share set.

**Proposition 3 (No feasible vanishing-tax path with bounded composites).** Assume $\varphi=1$, $\theta_R\in(0,1)$, $\gamma\in(0,1)$, $\sigma\in[1,\infty)$, $\omega\in[0,1)$, and that the factor shares $(s_K,s_L)$ stay in a compact subset of $(0,1)^2$ along the path; at $\sigma=1$ assume in addition that $\{k_t\}$ is bounded above. There is no *feasible* path along which $\tau_t\to 0$ and $\{E_{C,t}\}$ is bounded — in particular no assigned owner path. No optimality condition enters the proof.

*Proof.* Lemma 3 sends $g\to-\infty$. On a compact share set $\ln(s_L/s_K)$ is bounded, and $E_W-E_C$ is that quantity plus a bounded function of $d$; so $E_C$ bounded above gives $E_W$ bounded above, and Lemma 4 sends $n\to-\infty$. For $\sigma>1$, $s_K$ is continuous and strictly increasing in $x=k-n$, so a compact share set confines $x$ to a compact interval and $E_{C,t}-n_t=\ln s_K(x_t)+\psi(x_t)+a$ is bounded; hence $E_{C,t}\to-\infty$. For $\sigma=1$, $\{k_t\}$ bounded above and Lemma 5 give the same. Either way this contradicts boundedness of $\{E_{C,t}\}$ from below. $\square$

The share hypothesis is automatic at $\sigma=1$, where shares are the constants $(\alpha,1-\alpha)$, and it is what the Cobb–Douglas result of Proposition 5 dispenses with.

The extreme case, a zero tax at a single date, needs only that labour is essential.

**Proposition 4 (Shut-down at Cobb–Douglas).** Let $\sigma=1$ and extend the control set to $[0,1)$ with $\ln 0=-\infty$. A path with $\tau_T=0$ at a single date $T$ has $g_{T+1}=-\infty$, hence $n_t=-\infty$ for every $t>T+1$, since labour’s own lag then carries $-\infty$ whatever the public stock does afterwards. Labour being essential at $\sigma=1$, the path has $C_{C,t}=0$ from $t>T+1$, so its value is $-\infty$, while at a finite state any constant $\tau\in(0,1)$ has finite value (Proposition 6). Shut-down, at a single date or for ever, is therefore not assigned-optimal.[^5]

A vanishing rate on an exploding base would keep $G_t=\tau_t e^{E_{C,t}}$ finite. At Cobb–Douglas that path is not available.

**Proposition 5 (A small tax empties the economy at $\sigma=1$).** Let $\sigma=1$, $\delta\in(0,1]$, $\varphi=1$, $\alpha\in(0,1)$, $\theta_R\in(0,1)$, $\gamma\in(0,1)$, $i\in(0,1)$. There are constants $\tau_c\in(0,1)$ and $\kappa>0$, depending only on these parameters, such that along every feasible path of $(K_t,n_t,\tau_t)$ on which $\tau_t\le\tau_c$ from some date on, each of $k_t$, $n_t$ and $g_t$ is at most $c_0-\kappa t$ for a constant $c_0$ of the path, and $E_{C,t}\to-\infty$: capital, labour, the public stock and capital income go to zero together. In particular this holds along every feasible path with $\tau_t\to 0$. No such path keeps capital income bounded away from zero.

At $\sigma=1$ the labour law reads
$$
n_{t+1}-n_t=\gamma\theta_R\bigl[\ln(1-\alpha)+\alpha x_t\bigr]+\eta\bigl[g_t-n_t\bigr],
\qquad g_t=\ln\tau_{t-1}+E_{C,t-1},
$$
where $x_t:=k_t-n_t$ and $\eta=\gamma(1-\theta_R)>0$, so a vanishing tax enters labour growth with a one-period lag, through $\eta\ln\tau_{t-1}$. Capital obeys
$$
\frac{K_{t+1}}{K_t}=1-\delta+i(1-\tau_t)\alpha e^{-(1-\alpha)x_t}.
$$
A vanishing tax therefore pushes labour down through the public stock, while depreciation caps how far capital deepening can push it back up. The two cannot be balanced. Appendix A.3 measures the three logged stocks in units that turn the transition into a strict contraction, and reads the conclusion off a linear rate. The argument uses $\delta>0$ twice: depreciation dominates under capital deepening, and there is no AK residual at $\sigma=1$.[^6]

**Corollary (No qualifier at Cobb–Douglas).** At $\sigma=1$ and $\delta\in(0,1]$, neither the bounded-composite and bounded-capital qualifiers of Proposition 3 nor its share hypothesis is needed: a vanishing tax carries capital income down with it whatever the composites do (Proposition 5), and Proposition 10 below puts every assigned-optimal path above a positive constant.

**Corollary (convergent paths).** At $\sigma=1$ and $\delta\in(0,1]$, if an assigned-optimal path satisfies $\tau_t\to\tau_\infty$, then $\tau_\infty\ge\bar\tau>0$, with $\bar\tau$ the constant of Proposition 10. In particular there is no convergent assigned-owner path with a zero long-run capital-income tax.

*Proof.* Proposition 10 gives $\tau_t\ge\bar\tau$ at every date and every state, so any limit lies in $[\bar\tau,1]$. $\square$

The share set can be dropped above the face too, and what replaces it is one number. As capital deepens, $Y/K$ falls towards $\underline A=\theta^{1/\rho_\sigma}e^{a}$ and never below it, so capital reinvested at a vanishing tax returns at least $i\underline A$ per unit whatever labour does. Whether a vanishing tax empties the economy then turns on whether that return covers depreciation.

**Proposition 5$'$ (A small tax empties the economy above Cobb–Douglas exactly when capital cannot replace itself).** Let $\sigma\ge1$, $\delta\in(0,1]$, $\varphi=1$, $\theta,\theta_R,\gamma\in(0,1)$ and $a$ real, and hold the direction $d$ and the investment share $i\in(0,1)$ fixed. Let $\underline A=\lim_{x\to+\infty}Y/K$, so that $\underline A=\theta^{1/\rho_\sigma}e^{a}$ for $\sigma>1$ and $\underline A=0$ at $\sigma=1$.

1.  If $i\underline A<\delta$, there are constants $\tau_c\in(0,1)$ and $\kappa>0$, depending only on the parameters, such that along every feasible path on which $\tau_t\le\tau_c$ from some date on, each of $k_t$, $n_t$ and $g_t$ is at most $c_0-\kappa t$ for a constant $c_0$ of the path, and $E_{C,t}\to-\infty$. No share, composite or capital hypothesis is used.

2.  If $i\underline A\ge\delta$, which requires $\sigma>1$, then along every feasible path with $\sum_t\tau_t<\infty$ capital is bounded away from zero and capital income stays above $\underline AK_t$; and if $i\underline A>\delta$, along every feasible path with $\tau_t\to0$ capital grows at least geometrically and $E_{C,t}\to+\infty$.

So every feasible path with $\tau_t\to0$ empties the economy exactly when $i\underline A<\delta$.

At $\sigma=1$, part (i) is Proposition 5. Above that face the argument of Appendix A.3 goes through once the ratio $\bar X$ is chosen where the reinvested return $ie^{a+h(\bar X)}$ is below $\delta$, which is possible exactly when $i\underline A<\delta$, because $h$ falls towards $\rho_\sigma^{-1}\ln\theta$ without reaching it (Appendix A.3$'$). When $i\underline A\ge\delta$, capital replaces itself out of its own income, and a vanishing tax no longer takes capital income down with it. The Cobb–Douglas face rules that out because there $Y/K\to0$ as capital deepens. The condition bears on the technology and on the fixed investment share, not on the tax: it says how productive capital alone must be before a long-run zero can be sustained. It does not make a vanishing tax assigned-optimal; that question is settled only at $\sigma=1$, by Proposition 10.

Proposition 5 has a relative in the planner literature. Straub and Werning (2020; working-paper version, p. 4) show that in Chamley’s representative-agent economy with recursive, non-additive preferences, a steady state with a zero capital tax and slack tax bounds must carry either zero private wealth, so that the tax base has vanished, or a zero labour tax. Here the zero tax base arrives by another route and needs no restriction on preferences: the revenue rebuilds the labour that capital employs, so a vanishing tax takes capital income down with it.

## Interior policy at a given state

At a given state the owner weighs a tax by what it does to his consumption today and to the state he hands to tomorrow. At Cobb–Douglas, which every result below assumes, his best response exists and is interior at every state: he neither confiscates nor lets the public stock go (Proposition 6). A tax the economy can rest at needs more, a fixed point (Lemma 6 and Proposition 7), and a variation on the public stock shows that no small tax is ever his choice (Proposition 8).

Fix a current state $(K,n,g)$ with $K>0$, and fix $(d,i)$. Write

$$
W(\tau)=(1-\omega)\ln(1-\tau)+(1-\omega)E_C(K,n)+\omega\ln K+\beta V\bigl(K'(\tau),n',g'(\tau)\bigr).
$$

Here $n'$ does not depend on $\tau$.

**Proposition 6 (Interior policy at a given state).** Let $\sigma=1$, $\varphi=1$, $\theta_R\in(0,1)$, $\gamma\in(0,1)$, $\omega\in[0,1)$, $\delta\in(0,1]$ and $i\in(0,1)$. At every finite state $(K,n,g)$, $W$ is continuous on $(0,1)$ and $W(\tau)\to-\infty$ as $\tau\to 1^-$ and as $\tau\to 0^+$. A maximiser $\tau^*(K,n,g)\in(0,1)$ therefore exists.[^7]

Finiteness and Lipschitz continuity of $V$ come from the same non-expansiveness that gives Lemma 7, and the two endpoints are lost for the two reasons the economics names: confiscation destroys the owner’s consumption, and a vanishing rate destroys the stock that reproduces his workforce. Appendix A.4 has the proof.

A stationary peak is a fixed point $\tau=\tau^*(K(\tau),n(\tau),g(\tau))$, where the state is the one induced by the constant policy $\tau$.

**Lemma 6 (Intensive stationarity at $\sigma=1$).** Let $\sigma=1$ and $x:=k-n$. Then
$$
E_W-n=\ln(1-\alpha)+\alpha x,\qquad g-n=\ln(\alpha\tau)+\alpha x,
$$
both independent of scale $n$. Labour stationarity $n'=n$ is
$$
x=x_L(\tau):=-\frac{\theta_R\ln(1-\alpha)+(1-\theta_R)\ln(\alpha\tau)}{\alpha}.
$$
Capital stationarity is
$$
x=x_K(\tau,i,\delta):=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\ln\Bigl(\frac{i(1-\tau)\alpha}{\delta}\Bigr).
$$
A fully intensive-stationary point at fixed $i$ exists only when $x_L(\tau)=x_K(\tau,i,\delta)$. Equivalently $i$ adjusts:
$$
i(\tau,\delta)=\frac{\delta}{\alpha(1-\tau)}\,e^{(1-\alpha)x_L(\tau)}.
$$
The scale $n$ is not pinned. As $\tau\to 0^+$, $x_L(\tau)\to+\infty$. With the constants of these displays $x_L(\tau)>0$ for every $\tau\in(0,1)$, because both logarithms are negative, so $i(\tau,\delta)>\delta/\alpha$: the curve meets the licensed box $i<1$ only if $\delta<\alpha$. At $\delta\ge\alpha$ no stationary point has $i<1$. In particular the constant tax $\tau_C$ of Proposition 2 at $\delta=1$ is a constant policy and not a stationary peak in the sense of this section.

*Proof.* Substitute the Cobb–Douglas composites into $n'=(1-\gamma)n+\gamma\theta_R E_W+\eta g$ and set $n'=n$. Capital stationarity divides by $L$. $\square$

**Proposition 7 (What a fixed point still needs).** Let $\sigma=1$ and maintain the hypotheses of Proposition 6. Lemma 6 does not give a state $(K,n,g)$ for every $\tau$ at fixed $i$. It gives a curve $i=i(\tau,\delta)$ on which intensive stationarity holds; the scale $n$ remains a state. Proposition 6 still gives $\tau^*(K,n,g)\subset(0,1)$ at each given state. A stationary peak is a pair $(\tau,i(\tau,\delta))$ that is a best response at some scale of that intensive point.[^8]

*One-step bound, if $V$ is differentiable.* Raise current $g$ by $\Delta>0$ and hold subsequent instruments fixed. Then $n'$ rises by $\eta\Delta$ and, at $\sigma=1$, $E_C'$ rises by $(1-\alpha)\eta\Delta$. Hence
$$
\frac{V(K,n,g+\Delta)-V(K,n,g)}{\Delta}\ge\beta(1-\omega)(1-\alpha)\eta>0.
$$
If $V$ is differentiable, $V_g\ge\beta(1-\omega)(1-\alpha)\eta$. Proposition 1 then gives
$$
\frac{\tau^*}{1-\tau^*}\ge\frac{\beta^2(1-\omega)(1-\alpha)\eta}{(1-\omega)+\beta I V_K}.
$$
A uniform upper bound on $I V_K$ would produce a positive lower bound on $\tau^*$ that holds at every state. Lemma 7 supplies one, and Proposition 10 does not need $V$ differentiable to use it.

Proposition 6 already settles that the owner taxes. What Proposition 7 leaves open is how little he can be made to tax. A stationary peak is a tax the economy can sit at, and the owner’s investment share has to adjust for it to exist; that is a statement about the resting point, not about his conduct on the way there. The economically useful object is a number he never goes below at any state at all, and the rest of this section produces one.

**Proposition 8 (Discrete variation).** Let $\sigma=1$, $\varphi\in(0,1]$, $\lambda>1$, and fix a current state with finite $E_C$ and investment $I=i(1-\tau)e^{E_C}$. Hold subsequent instruments fixed after the first period. Then
$$
V(K,n,g+\ln\lambda)-V(K,n,g)\ge\beta(1-\omega)(1-\alpha)\eta\ln\lambda>0.
$$
Comparing $W(\lambda\tau)$ with $W(\tau)$ at that state, the public-stock gain is that constant while the current-consumption loss is
$$
(1-\omega)\ln\frac{1-\tau}{1-\lambda\tau}=O(\tau)
$$
as $\tau\to 0^+$, and by Lemma 7 below the capital loss is at most $\beta u/(1-\beta)$ with $u=(\lambda-1)\tau/(1-\lambda\tau)$, which vanishes as $\tau\to 0^+$ at fixed $\lambda$. Hence there exists $\tau_\dagger>0$, depending on $\lambda$ and the parameters but not on the state, such that $W(\lambda\tau)>W(\tau)$ for all $\tau\in(0,\tau_\dagger)$. No maximiser at any state lies in $(0,\tau_\dagger)$.

*Proof.* Raising $g$ by $\ln\lambda$ raises $n'$ by $\eta\ln\lambda$ and $E_C'$ by $(1-\alpha)\eta\ln\lambda$. Next-period log consumption, at unchanged $(\tau',i')$, rises by that amount. That is the displayed lower bound. The consumption loss is the Taylor expansion of $\ln(1-\tau)$; the capital loss is priced by Lemma 7, which is proved independently of this result. The in-kind share enters $g'$ only as the additive level $\ln\varphi$, which cancels in every increment of $g$ used here, so the bound is the same at each fixed $\varphi\in(0,1]$. $\square$

Proposition 8 does not need $V$ differentiable. Combined with Proposition 6, the assigned owner at any finite-$I$ state rejects both $\tau=0$ and every sufficiently small $\tau$. Because Lemma 7 prices the capital loss in logs, that threshold is already uniform in the state; the next two results optimise over $\lambda$ and make it the explicit constant $\bar\tau$.

## A floor that does not move with the state

The emptying argument uses no optimality condition. The floor does. At any state the owner compares a tax with a slightly higher one. The higher tax costs him current consumption, and it costs him some of next period’s machines, because after-tax profit is what buys new machines. It gains him a larger public stock, which raises next period’s labour and, two periods out, his capital income in proportion to labour’s share. The gain from one extra log point of public stock can be bounded below without knowing the future path, and the cost can be bounded above; neither bound depends on how many machines he holds, how many workers there are, how large the public stock already is, or how fast machines wear out. The tax at which the lower bound on the gain still exceeds the upper bound on the cost is a floor below which he never goes.

The quantity Proposition 7 needs is an upper bound on $\beta IV_K$ that holds at every state and every $\delta$. Since $I\le K'$ by construction, it is enough to bound $K'V_{K'}$ — the sensitivity of owner value to capital measured in logs. That bound is a constant.

**Lemma 7 (Value sensitivity in capital).** Let $\sigma\in[1,\infty)$, $\varphi\in(0,1]$, and fix $(d,i)$. For every state $(k,n,g)$ and every $\Delta>0$,

$$
V(k+\Delta,n,g)-V(k,n,g)\le\frac{\Delta}{1-\beta}.
$$

Neither $\delta$, nor $i$, nor the state, nor the elasticity of substitution appears on the right. The same tax path, run from two states that differ only in capital, never lets them drift apart. The gap in every state variable is a weighted average of gaps already present, and each set of weights sums to one. Away from Cobb–Douglas that survives for a reason the three logged composites share: $\ln Y$, $E_C$ and $E_W$ are each of the form $f(k-n)+n$, so each has partial derivatives in $(k,n)$ summing to one, and at $\sigma\ge1$ those derivatives are also non-negative, $(1-s_L/\sigma,\,s_L/\sigma)$ for $E_C$ and $(s_K/\sigma,\,1-s_K/\sigma)$ for $E_W$. The three gaps coincide only at $\sigma=1$; each is at most the largest gap in the state at every $\sigma\ge1$, which is all the argument uses. Appendix A.1 has the proof.

**Proposition 10 (Uniform floor).** Assume a fixed $\varphi\in(0,1]$, $\sigma=1$, $\alpha\in(0,1)$, $\beta\in(0,1)$, $\theta_R\in(0,1)$, $\gamma\in(0,1)$, $\omega\in[0,1)$, and that $V$ is finite at finite states. Put

$$
P:=\beta^2(1-\omega)(1-\alpha)\eta,\qquad
Q:=(1-\omega)+\frac{\beta}{1-\beta},\qquad
c:=\frac{P}{Q},\qquad
\bar\tau:=\frac{c}{1+c}\in(0,1).
$$

Then no maximiser $\tau^*(K,n,g)$ of $W$ lies in $(0,\bar\tau)$ — at any state, for every $\delta\in(0,1]$ and every $i\in(0,1)$.[^9]

The proof is a variation, in Appendix A.2. Raise $\tau$ to $\lambda\tau$ with $\lambda>1$. The public stock rises by $\ln\lambda$ and is worth at least $P\ln\lambda$; consumption and capital fall by at most $Qu$ with $u=(\lambda-1)\tau/(1-\lambda\tau)$. The single step that makes $Q$ a constant is to measure the capital loss in logs rather than in levels: the inherited stock $(1-\delta)K$ sits in the denominator, so the loss is at most $u$ whatever $K$, $i$, $Y$ and $\delta$ are. That is the uniform bound on $\beta IV_K$ Proposition 7 asked for.

The two constants differ in how far they depend on the face. $Q$ is assembled from Lemma 7 and from the payoff, and Lemma 7 holds at every $\sigma\ge1$, so no part of $Q$ is a Cobb–Douglas artefact. $P$ is: labour’s share enters it as the constant $1-\alpha$, which off that face is $s_L/\sigma$ two periods on, and that quantity is not bounded away from zero along a path of unbounded deepening. That is why Proposition 10 is stated at $\sigma=1$, although its uniform ingredient is not confined there.

**Proposition 11 (Durability raises the floor).** Under the hypotheses of Proposition 10, let $\tau^*$ maximise $W$ at some state, and write $\chi^*:=I(\tau^*)/K'(\tau^*)\in(0,1]$ for the share of next period’s capital bought out of this period’s after-tax profit. Then

$$
\frac{\tau^*}{1-\tau^*}\ \ge\ \frac{P}{(1-\omega)+\beta\chi^*/(1-\beta)} .
$$

If $\tau^*$ does not raise the capital stock, $K'(\tau^*)\le K$, then $\chi^*\le\delta$ and

$$
\frac{\tau^*}{1-\tau^*}\ \ge\ c(\delta)
=\frac{\beta^2(1-\omega)(1-\alpha)\gamma(1-\theta_R)}{(1-\omega)+\dfrac{\beta\delta}{1-\beta}},
$$

where $c(\delta)$ is strictly decreasing in $\delta$, with $c(1)=c$ and

$$
\lim_{\delta\to 0^+}c(\delta)=\beta^2(1-\alpha)\gamma(1-\theta_R)>0 .
$$

*Proof.* Write $R:=(1-\delta)K$ and $B:=ie^{E_C}$, so that $I(\tau)=B(1-\tau)$ and $K'(\tau)=R+B(1-\tau)$. Appendix A.2 bounds the capital loss $k'(\tau)-k'(\lambda\tau)$ by $u$. Use instead its exact value $\ln\bigl(1+B(\lambda-1)\tau/(R+B(1-\lambda\tau))\bigr)$, which Lemma 7 prices in the same way and whose derivative in $\lambda$ at $\lambda=1$ is $B\tau/(R+B(1-\tau))=\chi\,\tau/(1-\tau)$ with $\chi=B(1-\tau)/K'(\tau)$. Dividing A.2’s lower bound on $W(\lambda\tau)-W(\tau)$ by $\lambda-1$ and letting $\lambda\downarrow 1$ then gives $P-[(1-\omega)+\beta\chi/(1-\beta)]\,\tau/(1-\tau)$, and a $\tau$ at which this is positive is not a maximiser. For $\delta<1$, $\chi\le\delta$ is equivalent to $B(1-\tau)\le\delta K$, that is to $K'\le K$; at $\delta=1$, $R=0$ and $\chi=1=\delta$. Monotonicity and the limit are read off $c(\delta)$. $\square$

The sharper floor is confined to best responses that do not raise the capital stock, and for a reason. A best response that accumulates capital buys more than a fraction $\delta$ of next period’s stock out of after-tax profit, so the tax touches more of it, and the floor guaranteed there lies between Proposition 10’s and the sharper one.

**Corollary (Conjecture 2, wherever its object exists).** At $\sigma=1$, Conjecture 2 holds in a form that does not wait for the limit: whenever a stationary assigned peak $\tau_C(\delta)$ exists it satisfies $\tau_C(\delta)\ge c(\delta)/(1+c(\delta))\ge\bar\tau>0$, at every $\delta\in(0,1]$ (Proposition 11, then Proposition 10); and along every sequence $\delta_j\downarrow 0$ for which such peaks exist, $\liminf_j\tau_C(\delta_j)\ge c_0/(1+c_0)$ with $c_0=\beta^2(1-\alpha)\gamma(1-\theta_R)$. Existence at a given $\delta$ is Conjecture 1, so the bound is a statement about the peaks there are and not a claim that there is one at every $\delta$. Under its parameter condition, Proposition 13 gives an interval of $\delta$ at which there is none.

**Corollary (paths, second route).** Under the hypotheses of Proposition 10, every assigned-optimal path satisfies $\tau_t\ge\bar\tau$ for all $t$. In particular no assigned-optimal path has $\tau_t\to 0$. Propositions 3 and 5 reach a wider class — every *feasible* path — by a divergence argument, and say what a vanishing tax does along one; Proposition 10 reaches the optimal paths by a variation, and puts a number on how far from zero they stay.

**Corollary (durable long run).** Under the hypotheses of Proposition 10, every assigned-optimal path along which $K_{t+1}/K_t\to1$, in particular one along which capital converges to a positive level, satisfies $\liminf_t\tau_t\ge c(\delta)/(1+c(\delta))$, with $c(\delta)$ the constant of Proposition 11; and along an assigned-optimal path on which capital does not rise from some date on, $\tau_t\ge c(\delta)/(1+c(\delta))$ from that date.

*Proof.* Each $\tau_t$ maximises $W$ at the date-$t$ state, and $\chi_t=1-(1-\delta)K_t/K_{t+1}$, which tends to $\delta$ when $K_{t+1}/K_t\to1$ and is at most $\delta$ when $K_{t+1}\le K_t$. Apply Proposition 11 and use that $t\mapsto t/(1-t)$ is increasing. $\square$

This is the counterpart of Chamley and Judd’s long-run statement, and it does not wait for Conjecture 1: wherever the owner’s own path lets capital settle, the long-run tax is bounded below by the durable floor and not only by $\bar\tau$.

The economics of the constant is the accounting of the two channels. $P$ is what one log point of public stock is worth to the owner: it reaches him after two periods, through the reproduction weight $\eta=\gamma(1-\theta_R)$ and labour’s share $1-\alpha$. $Q$ is what the same log point costs: consumption today, plus the value of the capital it is bought out of. The second half of that cost is the whole of the durability question, and Proposition 11 answers it. As $\delta$ falls, a smaller share of next period’s capital is purchased out of after-tax profit wherever capital is not being accumulated, so the tax bites a smaller part of it and the denominator of $c(\delta)$ falls. Durability does not release the owner from funding the stock. It makes the funding cheaper. The intuition that an owner who inherits most of next period’s machines has less reason to fund the public stock misses the accounting of $G$: durability splits the map from after-tax profit into new machines, not the map from the tax into the public stock, and surviving machines do not remove the loading of $G$ on next period’s labour. At $\alpha=1/3$, $\beta=\gamma=\theta_R=1/2$ and $\omega=0$, the uniform floor is $\bar\tau=1/49$, and as machines become perfectly durable the sharper floor rises towards $1/25$. Neither number is large. Both are positive, explicit and the same at every state.

The floor has a counterpart above. Run the same variation downwards, lowering $\tau$ to $\lambda\tau$ with $\lambda<1$: consumption rises, next period’s capital rises, and the only loss is the public stock.

**Proposition 12 (A ceiling).** Let $\sigma\ge1$, $\varphi\in(0,1]$, $\beta,\gamma,\theta_R\in(0,1)$, $\omega\in[0,1)$, and assume that $V$ is finite at finite states. Put

$$
\bar c:=\frac{\beta^2\gamma(1-\theta_R)}{(1-\beta)(1-\omega)},\qquad \hat\tau:=\frac{\bar c}{1+\bar c}\in(0,1).
$$

Then no maximiser $\tau^*(K,n,g)$ of $W$ lies in $(\hat\tau,1)$ — at any state, for every $\delta\in(0,1]$ and every $i\in(0,1)$.

The owner stops raising the tax where the next log point of public stock is worth less to him than the consumption it costs. That log point reaches labour one period on with weight $\eta$, and by the argument of Lemma 7 no gap it opens in the state ever grows, so it is worth at most $\beta\eta/(1-\beta)$ from then on. Capital can only favour the lower tax. Appendix A.6 has the proof. At $\sigma=1$ the ceiling lies strictly above even the sharpest floor of Proposition 11, so every best response at every state lies in the band $[\bar\tau,\hat\tau]$, and the band depends on the parameters alone.

A stationary peak therefore needs a rest point inside that band, and there need not be one. Write Lemma 6’s curve as $i(\tau,\delta)=\delta j(\tau)$, where $j$ does not depend on $\delta$.

**Proposition 13 (No stationary peak near the top of the curve).** Let $\sigma=1$, $\varphi=1$, $m:=(1-\alpha)(1-\theta_R)/\alpha$ and $\tau_m:=m/(1+m)$. Then $\ln j$ is strictly convex on $(0,1)$ with its minimum at $\tau_m$, and stationary points with $i\in(0,1)$ exist exactly when $\delta<\bar\delta:=1/j(\tau_m)$. If

$$
\alpha\beta^2\gamma<(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)(1-\omega),
$$

then $\hat\tau<\tau_m$ and $\delta_c:=1/j(\hat\tau)<\bar\delta$, and at every $\delta\in[\delta_c,\bar\delta)$ there are stationary points with $i\in(0,1)$ but no stationary assigned peak.

Every rest point needs a tax. The public stock that keeps labour level with capital and the profit that replaces worn capital are paid out of the same capital income, and $\tau_m$ is the tax at which the investment share needed for both is smallest. The condition says the owner’s ceiling lies below $\tau_m$, which happens when he discounts heavily, when labour adjusts slowly or when capital’s share is small; a patient owner with $\beta$ near one does not satisfy it. As $\delta$ approaches $\bar\delta$ the rest points with $i<1$ close in on $\tau_m$, and from $\delta_c$ on all of them lie above his ceiling, so none of them is his. At $\alpha=1/3$, $\beta=\gamma=\theta_R=1/2$ and $\omega=0$, $\hat\tau=1/9$, $\tau_m=1/2$ and the interval is $[16/2187,\,1/54)$; at $\alpha=3/5$, $\beta=4/5$, $\gamma=1/10$, $\theta_R=1/2$ and $\omega=0$ it is $[\delta_c,\bar\delta)$ with $\delta_c\approx 0.16608$ and $\bar\delta\approx 0.17617$. The ceiling says more than that the owner’s path cannot rest there. Write $\iota:=j(\hat\tau)(1-\hat\tau)/(1-\bar\tau)$.

**Proposition 14 (The owner runs the economy down).** Let $\sigma=1$ and $\varphi=1$. If $i<\delta\iota$, there are a constant $C$ and a rate $\mu^*<0$ such that every path whose taxes lie in $[\bar\tau,\hat\tau]$, and in particular every assigned-optimal path, has $\max(k_t,n_t,g_t)\le C+\mu^*t$ for all $t$, so capital, labour, the public stock and capital income vanish geometrically. A constant tax $\tau$ grows the economy exactly when $i>\delta j(\tau)$. Hence if $\iota>j(\tau_m)$, then at every $\delta<\bar\delta$ the investment shares $i\in\bigl(\delta j(\tau_m),\min(1,\delta\iota)\bigr)$ form a non-empty set at which the constant tax $\tau_m$ would grow the economy and every assigned-optimal path empties it; at $\delta\in[1/\iota,\bar\delta)$ that is every $i\in(\delta j(\tau_m),1)$.

The owner can levy a tax that keeps his workforce and his capital growing, and he does not. However he moves between $\bar\tau$ and $\hat\tau$, the path is no better than one that reinvests as if taxed at $\bar\tau$ and funds the public stock as if taxed at $\hat\tau$, and when $i<\delta\iota$ even that combination cannot replace worn capital at the capital–labour ratio at which the public stock it funds holds labour level. Nothing in the argument is about a rest point: it is his own optimal path, from any starting state. At $\alpha=1/3$, $\beta=\gamma=\theta_R=1/2$ and $\omega=0$, $\bar\tau=1/49$, $\iota=3969/32\approx124$ exceeds $j(\tau_m)=54$, and at every $\delta\in[0.0081,\,0.0185)$ this happens at every investment share at which $\tau_m$ would grow the economy. At the second example $\iota\approx5.20$ is below $j(\tau_m)\approx5.68$ and the comparison is empty, so the condition matters.

Conjecture 1 at $\sigma=1$ is therefore a fixed point on the part of Lemma 6’s curve that lies both in the licensed box and in the band $[\bar\tau,\hat\tau]$, not the Theorem of the Maximum on $(0,1)$.

## Stationary first-order condition

At a rest point Proposition 1’s condition takes a stationary form. Three substitutions would turn it into a closed form for the owner’s tax in the rate of wear, and none of them is available.

An interior stationary first-order condition at fixed $(d,i)$ has the shape

$$
\frac{\tau}{1-\tau}
=\frac{\beta^{2}\gamma(1-\theta_R)\,V_n'}{(1-\omega)+\beta I V_K'}
$$

after using $\delta K=I=i(1-\tau)e^{E_C}$, provided current $g$ is a state and $\tau$ enters $g'$. The two discount factors are the two periods the public stock needs to reach the owner: Proposition 1 carries one, and the envelope $V_g=\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)V_n(\cdot')$ carries the other. The one-generation peaks displayed before Proposition 2 are the case $\delta=1$ under log-affine $V$, where $IV_K'=v_k$, the coefficient on $k$ in the owner’s affine value (Appendix A.7), and the identity holds at every state, not only at rest.

The three substitutions would turn that identity into $\tau_C=\mathcal N/(\mathcal D(\delta)+\mathcal N)$ with $\mathcal D(\delta)=(1-\omega)+\beta\omega\delta/(1-\beta(1-\delta))$ and $\mathcal N$ independent of $\delta$:

1.  The envelope $V_K=\omega/K+\beta(1-\delta)V_K'$ drops $\partial E_C/\partial K$. Capital income depends on $K$. The omitted term is $(1-\omega)E_{C,K}$ plus continuation through $K'$ and $n'$.
2.  $\mathcal N=\beta^{2}\gamma(1-\theta_R)V_n'$ independent of $\delta$ treats the labour shadow price as a preference parameter. $V_n$ is an equilibrium object. It moves with $K/L$, and $K/L$ moves with $\delta$.
3.  $\mathcal N$ independent of $\tau$ is affine value in $g$. But $V_n'$ is evaluated at a continuation state that moves with $\tau$, with $g'\to-\infty$ as $\tau\to 0$, and Lemma 2 records that owner value is not log-affine for $\delta<1$.

Above Cobb–Douglas durability also acts through the shares. Under the one-generation expansion, $\tau_C\to 0$ as $s_K\to 1$ when $\sigma>1$. Lowering $\delta$ tends to raise $K$ relative to $L$, which for $\sigma>1$ raises $s_K$ and shrinks the owner’s weight on $L$, while the public-channel coefficient $\gamma(1-\theta_R)$ does not vanish. At $\sigma=1$ shares are fixed and this channel is shut.

## Contact with Chamley and Judd

This section sets the floor against Chamley and Judd’s zero: what the two models differ on, why the floor does not rest on commitment, what a wage tax does to it, and how the two models nearest in motive and in chooser differ.

**Remark (what the contact rests on).** Judd (1985) and Chamley (1986) obtain $\tau_t\to 0$ from a planner problem in which:

1.  Revenue is not an input into $n'$ through $G'=\varphi\tau e^{E_C}$.
2.  The planner is not the assigned owner.
3.  Labour is not reproduced from a public stock that only the capital-income tax fills. At $\delta=1$ the floor needs that restriction: given a wage tax as well, the owner sets the capital tax to zero (Proposition 15).

In this model, by contrast, at $\delta=1$ and $\sigma=1$ the assigned programme is the constant $\tau_C$ from every state (Proposition 2), so its long-run tax is reached at the first date and needs no rest point of the economy; for $\delta<1$ the closure that delivers constancy fails (Lemma 2). Lemma 1 keeps $G$ tied to $\tau$ at every $\delta$. Proposition 1 keeps $V_g$ loaded through $\gamma(1-\theta_R)$ at every $\delta$ on the licensed box. Those facts, Propositions 5 and 5$'$, and above all Proposition 10, are the contact. Each holds the owner’s investment share fixed, so the owner chooses the tax and nothing else; none of them is a statement about a Ramsey plan that also chooses saving.

Commitment turns on a different fact of the Environment: whether any other agent’s current choice reacts to the taxes the owner is expected to set later. Chamley and Judd’s zero is the long-run limit of a plan chosen once, at date zero, by a planner who takes into account how savers respond to the taxes they expect. Such a plan is in general not time-consistent: re-solved later, with capital already in place, it calls for a higher tax on that capital, so it is optimal only if the planner can commit to it. The assigned owner faces no such response. The investment share is held fixed and workers do not save, so no other agent’s current choice depends on the taxes the owner is expected to set later. With a single decision-maker and geometric discounting, the plan the owner would choose at date zero is the plan he carries out when he chooses afresh each period. Proposition 10 is a statement about his choice at every state, so its floor does not rest on commitment. The price of that robustness is scope: holding $i$ fixed shuts the saving margin through which the planner’s commitment problem arises. Judd (1985) himself reaches a positive long-run tax only by removing commitment: when worker-dominated legislatures control only short-term rates, their equilibrium generally carries substantial long-run taxation. The floor here runs the other way, from what the revenue buys, and holds with the owner choosing afresh at every state.

**Proposition 15 (A wage tax takes the floor away at $\delta=1$).** Let $\sigma=1$, $\delta=1$ and $\varphi\in(0,1]$, and give the owner a second instrument: a tax $\tau_w\in[0,1)$ on the wage bill, so that $G'=\varphi(\tau e^{E_C}+\tau_w e^{E_W})$, with the capital tax now ranging over $[0,1)$. Let labour be reproduced out of the net wage,

$$
n'=(1-\gamma)n+\gamma\theta_R\bigl(\ln(1-\tau_w)+E_W\bigr)+\eta g.
$$

The owner’s value is affine in logs, and his unique assigned-optimal plan from every finite state sets $\tau=0$ and $\tau_w=A/(A+N)\in(0,1)$ at every date, where $A$ and $N$ are the discounted values to him of a log point of the public stock and of the net wage (Appendix A.7). If labour were reproduced out of the gross wage, the wage tax would cost him nothing and he would push $\tau_w$ towards one, again with $\tau=0$.[^10]

The owner still funds the public stock at every state; the second instrument changes who pays for it. Each log point by which the capital tax cuts his after-tax income costs him $M=(1-\omega)+\beta v_k$ in consumption and capital, with $v_k$ the value to him of a log point of capital (Appendix A.7), and it is levied on the share $\alpha$ of output. Each log point by which the wage tax cuts the net wage costs him $N$, through the labour he hires, and it is levied on the share $1-\alpha$. Per unit of revenue, the first costs $M/\alpha$ at a zero capital tax and the second $(A+N)/(1-\alpha)$ at the wage tax he chooses, and the first is the larger at every point of the licensed box. This is Judd’s (1999, p. 17) zero reached from the owner’s side: given a wage tax, the stock that reproduces labour is paid for out of wages and not out of capital income. What carries Proposition 10’s floor is therefore the funding instrument.

Jones, Manuelli and Rossi (1997) find the same dependence of one tax rate on the menu of taxes in a planner’s problem: restrictions across tax rates, like pure rents, can make the limiting capital tax positive. Here the rate is the owner’s own, and the restricted menu delivers a floor at every state rather than the sign of a limit. That the menu is not neutral for the capital stock is an old result in this journal: Seidman and Maurer (1982), in a two-class growth model with exogenous class saving rates, convert an income tax into a wage or a consumption tax raising equal steady-state revenue per worker, and find that the conversion raises steady-state capital intensity by shifting disposable income from low to high savers. There nobody chooses the rate and no saving rate responds to it; here the owner chooses, and what the menu moves is his own floor. The owner will not let the stock that reproduces his workforce go unfunded, and when the capital tax is the instrument that funds it, as in Proposition 10, he never sets it below $\bar\tau$.

Galor and Moav (2006), the nearest neighbour on motive, have the owner’s reason to fund workers’ schooling without a floor: their capital depreciates fully in each generation, and their tax is zero until capital per worker passes a critical level. On the Cobb–Douglas face both models share, the threshold gives way to a floor once capital lasts: the owner’s tax never falls below $\bar\tau$, at any state and any rate of depreciation. The reason is how the stock enters. Here it is an essential input into reproducing the workforce, and next period’s labour responds to it with the constant elasticity $\eta=\gamma(1-\theta_R)$ however small it is, so a log point of it is always worth at least $P$ to the owner; it is not an addition to basic skills that workers have without it.

Acemoglu, Gitmez and Shadmehr (2026) share the chooser and the sole instrument. In their model a state run by capital owners, while it redistributes rather than represses, chooses a strictly positive tax whose revenue goes to workers, and a tax on capital alone gives the same results. What differs is what the revenue buys: there it is a transfer that answers a threat of revolt, and here it is an input into the labour the owner hires.

## Conclusion

A tax on capital income that fills a public stock, in an economy where that stock reproduces labour nobody owns, is not the tax Chamley and Judd studied. At Cobb–Douglas, the owner who sets it will not set it to zero. He will not set it below an explicit floor, at any state and any rate of wear. The floor is small, one forty-ninth at the illustration used above, and its size is not what matters: it is positive, explicit, the same at every state, and, wherever his chosen tax is not raising the capital stock, higher the more durable the machines. Along the path he himself chooses, wherever capital settles, the long-run tax obeys that sharper floor. At Cobb–Douglas, and whoever chooses the tax, a tax held below a positive threshold empties the public stock, then the workforce, then capital income; above Cobb–Douglas the same is true exactly when capital at the limit of deepening cannot replace itself out of its own income. The same accounting caps the tax, and under conditions on the parameters the owner’s own path runs down an economy that a constant tax he could have levied would have grown.

He will not, however, insist that capital pay. Given a wage tax, and with machines lasting one period, he funds the same stock out of wages and leaves capital income untaxed.

That the maintenance of the working class is a condition of the reproduction of capital is an old observation. The model makes the condition operational where it might be expected not to bite: durable machines, an owner who sets the tax himself, no planner, no revolt, no risk, and a linear schedule. Three readings follow, in increasing order of ambition.

The narrow reading is an instrument result inside a two-class growth model. If a public stock that reproduces labour can be funded only by a tax on capital income, the owner of capital will not set that tax to zero, and durability does not change his mind. If wages can be taxed too and machines last one period, he will not tax capital. Whether owners oppose capital taxation is therefore not a primitive; it turns on the instruments they are allowed.

The middle reading is about Chamley–Judd as a benchmark. The zero long-run capital tax rests on a planner, a transfer and a saving margin, and durable capital does not rescue it. Change what the revenue buys and who sets the rate, and zero is no longer the owner’s destination; widen the instrument set to wages when machines last one period, and it is again. For teaching Chamley–Judd, that is the reading that matters: say what the revenue is for, and say who is choosing.

The wide reading is about what it means for labour not to be owned. In the standard model human capital is an asset: someone accumulates it, someone earns a return on it, and a planner who taxes that return faces an intertemporal distortion of the usual kind. Here successor labour is the output of a reproduction law that uses a public stock, and nobody holds it as an asset. There is no private Euler equation for labour, only the owner’s reason to keep the public stock from collapsing, and that reason does not die when machines last. Owners who hire labour have a reason, in their own objective, to keep the stock that reproduces that labour from going to zero, provided they cannot send the bill to wages instead. The floor is what it is because of what the revenue buys and because of which instrument is allowed to pay for it.

### A.1 Proof of Lemma 7

Both values are real numbers at every $\sigma\ge1$: step (i) of A.4 uses only that $T_\tau$ is non-decreasing and additively homogeneous and that the period payoff is bounded below by an affine function of $\min(k_t,n_t,g_t)$, and away from Cobb–Douglas the third holds because $h(x)=\ln s_K+\psi-x$ decreases to $\rho_\sigma^{-1}\ln\theta$, so $E_C\ge k+a+\rho_\sigma^{-1}\ln\theta$; the constant there is $\bar M=\max\{\ln(1-\delta+i\theta e^{a}),\ \gamma\theta_R(\ln(1-\theta)+a),\ \ln(\varphi\theta e^{a})\}$. Run a tax path $\{\tau_t\}$ that is $\varepsilon$-optimal at $(k+\Delta,n,g)$ from both states; it is feasible at both, since $\tau_t\in(0,1)$ is unconstrained by $K$. Write $\Delta_t$, $\nu_t$, $\xi_t$ for the gaps in $k$, $n$, $g$, and $D^C_t$, $D^W_t$ for the gaps in the composites $E_{C,t}$ and $E_{W,t}$.

Each composite is a function of the state of the form $f(k-n)+n$: $\ln Y=a+n+\psi(x)$, $E_C=\ln s_K+\psi+a+n$ and $E_W=\ln s_L+\psi+a+n$. The two partial derivatives of such a function sum to one identically, and with $s_K'=\rho_\sigma s_Ks_L$ they are $(1-s_L/\sigma,\,s_L/\sigma)$ for $E_C$ and $(s_K/\sigma,\,1-s_K/\sigma)$ for $E_W$. At $\sigma\ge1$ both pairs are non-negative, since $s_L\le1\le\sigma$ and $s_K\le1\le\sigma$. Integrating along the segment joining the two states therefore gives
$$
D^C_t\le\max(\Delta_t,\nu_t),\qquad D^W_t\le\max(\Delta_t,\nu_t),
$$
and the same for the gap in $\ln Y_t$, whose weights are $(s_K,s_L)$. At $\sigma=1$ the three gaps coincide in the single object $\alpha\Delta_t+(1-\alpha)\nu_t$; for $\sigma>1$ they differ, and only the bound is used below. Since $g'=\ln(\varphi\tau)+E_C$ with the same $\tau_t$ at both states, $\xi_{t+1}=D^C_t$, and

$$
\nu_{t+1}=\rho\nu_t+\gamma\theta_R D^W_t+\eta\xi_t ,
$$

a convex combination because $\rho+\gamma\theta_R+\eta=(1-\gamma)+\gamma\theta_R+\gamma(1-\theta_R)=1$. Capital gives

$$
e^{\Delta_{t+1}}=\frac{(1-\delta)K_t}{K_{t+1}}\,e^{\Delta_t}+\frac{i(1-\tau_t)e^{E_{C,t}}}{K_{t+1}}\,e^{D^C_t},
$$

again a convex combination, since the two weights are non-negative and sum to one by the capital law. Hence $z_t:=\max(\Delta_t,\nu_t,\xi_t)$ satisfies $z_{t+1}\le z_t$, and $z_t\le z_0=\Delta$ for all $t$.

The period-$t$ payoff gap is $(1-\omega)D^C_t+\omega\Delta_t$, since the constants in $\ln C_C=\ln(1-i)+\ln(1-\tau)+E_C$ cancel; it is a convex combination of $D^C_t$ and $\Delta_t$, hence at most $z_t\le\Delta$. Summing with weights $\beta^t$ bounds the value of that path from the lower state by $\Delta/(1-\beta)$ below the value at the upper state. The left-hand side of the lemma is no larger, because the path is $\varepsilon$-optimal above and merely feasible below; letting $\varepsilon\downarrow 0$ gives the stated bound. $\square$

### A.2 Proof of Proposition 10

Fix a state and a $\tau$ with $\tau/(1-\tau)<c$, and take $\lambda>1$ with $\lambda\tau<1$. Write $R:=(1-\delta)K\ge 0$ and $B:=ie^{E_C}>0$, so that $k'(\tau)=\ln(R+B(1-\tau))$ and

$$
k'(\tau)-k'(\lambda\tau)=\ln\Bigl(1+\frac{B(\lambda-1)\tau}{R+B(1-\lambda\tau)}\Bigr)\le\ln(1+u)\le u,
\qquad u:=\frac{(\lambda-1)\tau}{1-\lambda\tau},
$$

the first inequality because $R\ge 0$ only enlarges the denominator, the second because $\ln(1+u)\le u$. Lemma 7 prices that loss at no more than $u/(1-\beta)$, and it does so at the continuation state whose $g$ has already been raised.

The one-period comparison of Proposition 8 prices the gain from $g'(\lambda\tau)-g'(\tau)=\ln\lambda$ at no less than $\beta(1-\omega)(1-\alpha)\eta\ln\lambda$, holding subsequent instruments fixed. Current utility falls by $(1-\omega)\ln\frac{1-\tau}{1-\lambda\tau}\le(1-\omega)u$. Collecting the three terms,

$$
W(\lambda\tau)-W(\tau)\ \ge\ P\ln\lambda-\Bigl[(1-\omega)+\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}\Bigr]u
= P\ln\lambda-Qu .
$$

Divide by $\lambda-1$ and let $\lambda\downarrow 1$. Since $\ln\lambda/(\lambda-1)\to 1$ and $u/(\lambda-1)\to\tau/(1-\tau)$, the right-hand side tends to $P-Q\,\tau/(1-\tau)$, which is strictly positive exactly when $\tau/(1-\tau)<c$. By continuity some $\lambda>1$ has $W(\lambda\tau)>W(\tau)$, so $\tau$ is not a maximiser. Every $\tau$ in $(0,\bar\tau)$ satisfies $\tau/(1-\tau)<c$, since $t\mapsto t/(1-t)$ is increasing and $\bar\tau/(1-\bar\tau)=c$. $\square$

### A.3 Proof of Proposition 5

Fix a finite initial state and write $T_\tau$ for the map carrying $(k,n,g)$ to $(k',n',g')$ at tax $\tau$. Two properties are immediate from the Environment at $\sigma=1$: $T_\tau$ is non-decreasing in each of $k,n,g$, and it is additively homogeneous, $T_\tau(s+c\mathbf1)=T_\tau(s)+c\mathbf1$. Together these give, for any $s$ with $\max_js_j\le z$, $T_\tau(s)\le z\mathbf 1+T_\tau(0)$ componentwise.

Measure the three coordinates in different units. Fix $\bar X$ with $i\alpha e^{-(1-\alpha)\bar X}\le\delta/2$ and
$$
A=\eta^{-1}\bigl[\gamma\theta_R\bigl(\ln(1-\alpha)+\alpha\bar X\bigr)+1\bigr],
\qquad
\tilde z_t=\max\{k_t-\bar X,\ n_t,\ g_t+A\} .
$$
In these units the three components of $T_\tau(0)$ are
$$
\ln\bigl(1-\delta+i\alpha(1-\tau)e^{-(1-\alpha)\bar X}\bigr)\le\ln(1-\delta/2)<0,\qquad
\gamma\theta_R\bigl(\ln(1-\alpha)+\alpha\bar X\bigr)-\eta A=-1,\qquad
\ln\tau+\ln\alpha+\alpha\bar X+A .
$$
The third is at most $-1$ as soon as $\ln\tau\le-1-\ln\alpha-\alpha\bar X-A=:\ln\tau_c$. Hence with $\kappa:=\min\{-\ln(1-\delta/2),1\}>0$,
$$
\tilde z_{t+1}\le\tilde z_t-\kappa\qquad\text{for every }t\text{ with }\tau_t\le\tau_c .
$$
Raising $\bar X$ keeps its defining inequality and raises both $\alpha\bar X$ and $A$, since $\partial A/\partial\bar X=\gamma\theta_R\alpha/\eta>0$, so it lowers $\ln\tau_c$; take $\bar X$ large enough that $\tau_c<1$. The constants $\tau_c$ and $\kappa$ then depend only on $(\alpha,\gamma,\theta_R,\delta,i)$. On a path with $\tau_t\le\tau_c$ for every $t\ge t_0$, $\tilde z_t\le\tilde z_{t_0}-\kappa(t-t_0)$, so each of $k_t-\bar X$, $n_t$ and $g_t+A$ is at most $\tilde z_{t_0}+\kappa t_0-\kappa t$ for $t\ge t_0$; the finitely many earlier dates have finite stocks, so one constant $c_0$ of the path covers every date. Therefore $k_t$, $n_t$ and $g_t$ fall at least linearly, and $E_{C,t}=\ln\alpha+\alpha k_t+(1-\alpha)n_t\to-\infty$: capital, labour, the public stock and capital income go to zero together. A path with $\tau_t\to0$ has $\tau_t\le\tau_c$ from some $t_0$ on. $\square$

The rate is linear, and the argument needs nothing about $x_t$. In particular a constant $\tau\le\tau_c$ empties the economy at the same rate. A constant tax grows the economy exactly when $i>i(\tau,\delta)$, Lemma 6’s curve. Since $\ln i(\tau,\delta)$ is strictly convex in $\tau$ and $i(\tau,\delta)\to\infty$ at both ends of $(0,1)$, that happens on an interval of taxes if at all, and never at a tax close enough to one.[^11]

### A.4 Proof of Proposition 6

Three steps. Write $T_\tau$ for the map carrying $(k,n,g)$ to $(k',n',g')$ at tax $\tau$, and $s$ for a state.

1.  *$V$ is finite at every finite state.* $T_\tau$ is non-decreasing in each of $(k,n,g)$ and satisfies $T_\tau(s+c\mathbf1)=T_\tau(s)+c\mathbf1$. Hence $T_\tau(s)\le\max_j s_j\mathbf1+T_\tau(0)$ componentwise, and since
    $$
    \max_jT_\tau(0)_j\le\bar M:=\max\bigl\{\ln(1-\delta+i\alpha),\ \gamma\theta_R\ln(1-\alpha),\ \ln\alpha\bigr\}
    $$
    uniformly in $\tau$, every plan satisfies $\max(k_t,n_t,g_t)\le\max(k_0,n_0,g_0)+t\bar M^+$. The period payoff is therefore bounded above by an affine function of $t$, and $V<+\infty$. The constant plan $\tau\equiv\frac12$ gives, by the same bound applied to $\min_j$, a payoff bounded below by an affine function of $t$, so $V>-\infty$.

2.  *$V$ is Lipschitz.* Run a plan $\varepsilon$-optimal at $s$ from $s'$ as well. The recursions of A.1 are convex combinations in each coordinate, so the maximal gap is non-increasing and the period payoff gap is at most that maximum; letting $\varepsilon\downarrow 0$ and exchanging $s$ and $s'$ gives $|V(s)-V(s')|\le\lVert s-s'\rVert_\infty/(1-\beta)$. Since $k'(\tau)$ and $g'(\tau)$ are continuous on $(0,1)$, so is $W$.

3.  *Both endpoints.* As $\tau\to 1^-$, $(1-\omega)\ln(1-\tau)\to-\infty$ while, $V$ being non-decreasing in $K$ and in $g$, $V(K'(\tau),n',g'(\tau))\le V(K'(\tau_0),n',E_C)$ for every $\tau\ge\tau_0$. As $\tau\to 0^+$, Proposition 8’s bound read in the other direction gives
    $$
    V(K'(\tau),n',g'(\tau))\le V\bigl((1-\delta)K+ie^{E_C},n',g'(\tau_0)\bigr)-\beta(1-\omega)(1-\alpha)\eta\bigl[g'(\tau_0)-g'(\tau)\bigr]\longrightarrow-\infty .
    $$
    A continuous function diverging to $-\infty$ at both ends of $(0,1)$ attains a maximum in $(0,1)$. $\square$

*Optimal paths exist at $\sigma=1$.* From a finite state $s_0$ choose at each date a maximiser $\tau_t$ of $W$ at $s_t$, which (iii) supplies. With $u_t$ the period payoff, $V(s_t)=u_t+\beta V(s_{t+1})$ at every date, so $V(s_0)=\sum_{t<T}\beta^tu_t+\beta^TV(s_T)$ for every $T$. The payoff and the transitions are additively homogeneous, so $V(s+c\mathbf1)=V(s)+c/(1-\beta)$; with $V$ non-decreasing and the bound of (i), $\beta^TV(s_T)\le\beta^T\bigl[V(0)+(\max_js_{j,0}+T\bar M^+)/(1-\beta)\bigr]\to0$. The path’s payoff, the limit of the partial sums, is therefore at least $V(s_0)$, and the path is optimal.

### A.5 Closure failure at $\delta<1$: the step Lemma 2 needs

Suppose an affine $V=a_kk+a_nn+a_gg+h$ solved the Bellman equation at $\sigma=1$ and some $\delta<1$. All the $\tau$-dependence of the right-hand side sits in
$$
m(k)=\max_{\tau\in(0,1)}\bigl\{(1-\omega)\ln(1-\tau)+\beta a_k\ln\bigl(R+B(1-\tau)\bigr)+\beta a_g\ln\tau\bigr\},
\qquad R=(1-\delta)e^{k},\quad B=i\alpha e^{\alpha k+(1-\alpha)n}.
$$
Three cases dispose of every candidate other than $a_k>0$, $a_g\ge0$. If $a_g<0$, the term $\beta a_g\ln\tau$ sends the right-hand side to $+\infty$ as $\tau\to0^+$. If $a_k=0$, $m$ does not depend on the state, and matching the coefficients of $k$ and $g$ gives $\gamma\theta_Ra_n+a_g=-[(1-\omega)\alpha+\omega]/(\beta\alpha)$ and $a_g=\beta\eta a_n$, hence $a_g<0$, which is the first case. If $a_k<0$, $m(k)/k$ tends to $\beta a_k$ as $k\to+\infty$ and, as $k\to-\infty$, to $\alpha\beta a_k$ when $(1-\omega)+\beta a_k\ge0$ and to $\alpha\beta a_k+(1-\alpha)[(1-\omega)+\beta a_k]$ otherwise; neither equals $\beta a_k$, so $m$ is not affine in $k$. There remains $a_k>0$, $a_g\ge0$. As $k\to+\infty$, $B/R\to 0$ uniformly in $\tau$ and $m(k)/k\to\beta a_k$. As $k\to-\infty$, $\ln B+\ln(1-\tau)\le\ln(R+B(1-\tau))\le\ln B+\ln(1+R/B)$ gives $m(k)/k\to\alpha\beta a_k$. The two limits differ by $\beta a_k(1-\alpha)>0$, so $m$ is not affine in $k$ and neither is the right-hand side — a contradiction. Affine-in-logs closure therefore fails for every $\delta<1$. At $\delta=1$, $R=0$ and both limits are $\alpha\beta a_k$, which is why the closed forms of Proposition 2 and Appendix A.7 exist there and only there.

### A.6 Proof of Propositions 12, 13 and 14

*Proposition 12.* Two facts about $V$ are used. First, $V$ is non-decreasing in $k$: a tax path run from $(k+\Delta,n,g)$ and from $(k,n,g)$ produces states ordered at every date, because the transition is non-decreasing, and the period payoff is non-decreasing in $(k,n)$. Second, for $\Delta>0$,
$$
V(k,n,g+\Delta)-V(k,n,g)\le\frac{\beta\eta\Delta}{1-\beta}.
$$
Run a path that is $\varepsilon$-optimal at $(k,n,g+\Delta)$ from both states and use the notation of A.1. At date 0 the two states share $k$ and $n$, so $D^C_0=D^W_0=0$ and the date-0 payoff gap is zero. The recursions of A.1 give $\Delta_1=0$, $\xi_1=D^C_0=0$ and $\nu_1=\eta\Delta$, so $z_1=\eta\Delta$, and $z_t\le\eta\Delta$ for every $t\ge1$ by the same convex-combination argument. The payoff gap at each $t\ge1$ is at most $z_t$; summing with weights $\beta^t$ and letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ gives the bound.

Fix a state and a $\tau$ with $\tau/(1-\tau)>\bar c$, and take $\lambda\in(0,1)$. Lowering the tax to $\lambda\tau$ leaves $n'$ unchanged, raises $k'=\ln\bigl((1-\delta)K+i(1-\tau)e^{E_C}\bigr)$, which is decreasing in $\tau$, and lowers $g'$ by $-\ln\lambda$, since $\ln\varphi$ cancels in $g'(\lambda\tau)-g'(\tau)$. By the two facts,
$$
W(\lambda\tau)-W(\tau)\ \ge\ (1-\omega)\ln\frac{1-\lambda\tau}{1-\tau}+\frac{\beta^2\eta}{1-\beta}\ln\lambda=:h(\lambda).
$$
Here $h(1)=0$ and $h'(1)=\beta^2\eta/(1-\beta)-(1-\omega)\tau/(1-\tau)=(1-\omega)\bigl(\bar c-\tau/(1-\tau)\bigr)<0$, so $h(\lambda)>0$ for $\lambda<1$ close to $1$, and $\tau$ is not a maximiser. Every $\tau\in(\hat\tau,1)$ has $\tau/(1-\tau)>\bar c$. At $\sigma=1$ the band is non-empty with room to spare: Proposition 11’s $c(\delta)$ is at most its limit $\beta^2(1-\alpha)\eta$ as $\delta\to0$, and $\bar c-\beta^2(1-\alpha)\eta=\beta^2\eta\bigl[1/((1-\beta)(1-\omega))-(1-\alpha)\bigr]>0$. $\square$

*Proposition 13.* Lemma 6 gives $i(\tau,\delta)=\delta j(\tau)$ with
$$
j(\tau)=\alpha^{-1-m}(1-\alpha)^{-(1-\alpha)\theta_R/\alpha}\,\tau^{-m}(1-\tau)^{-1},
$$
so $\ln j$ has derivative $1/(1-\tau)-m/\tau$, which vanishes only at $\tau_m$, and second derivative $m/\tau^2+1/(1-\tau)^2>0$. The set of $\tau$ with $i(\tau,\delta)<1$ is therefore an open interval that contains $\tau_m$ when it is non-empty, and it is non-empty exactly when $\delta j(\tau_m)<1$. Next,
$$
m-\bar c=\frac{(1-\theta_R)\bigl[(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)(1-\omega)-\alpha\beta^2\gamma\bigr]}{\alpha(1-\beta)(1-\omega)},
$$
so the condition is $m>\bar c$, that is $\hat\tau<\tau_m$. Since $\ln j$ is strictly decreasing on $(0,\tau_m)$, $j(\hat\tau)>j(\tau_m)$, so $\delta_c<\bar\delta$, and for $\delta\ge\delta_c$ every $\tau\le\hat\tau$ has $i(\tau,\delta)\ge\delta j(\hat\tau)\ge1$. At $\delta\in[\delta_c,\bar\delta)$ every stationary point with $i\in(0,1)$ therefore has $\tau>\hat\tau$. A stationary assigned peak is such a $\tau$ that maximises $W$ at its own stationary state with $i=i(\tau,\delta)$ held fixed (Proposition 7), and Proposition 12 excludes every $\tau>\hat\tau$ at every state and for every $i\in(0,1)$. $\square$

*Proposition 14.* Write $T_\tau$ for the transition of $(k,n,g)$ at tax $\tau$. For $\tau\in[\bar\tau,\hat\tau]$, $T_\tau(s)\le T^*(s)$ componentwise, where $T^*$ uses $\bar\tau$ in the capital law and $\hat\tau$ in the public-stock law: $k'$ decreases in $\tau$, $g'$ increases in $\tau$, and $n'$ does not contain the current $\tau$. $T^*$ is non-decreasing and additively homogeneous, so $s_t\le T^{*t}(s_0)$ along every path with taxes in the band. $T^*$ has a state $v$ with $T^*(v)=v+\mu^*(1,1,1)$. Normalise $n=0$ and write $x=k$; the labour and public-stock laws then give $g=\ln(\alpha\hat\tau)+\alpha x-\mu$ and
$$
x(\mu)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\Bigl[\frac{\mu(1+\eta)}{\gamma}-\theta_R\ln(1-\alpha)-(1-\theta_R)\ln(\alpha\hat\tau)\Bigr],
$$
while the capital law requires
$$
e^{\mu}=1-\delta+i(1-\bar\tau)\alpha e^{-(1-\alpha)x(\mu)}.
$$
The right side falls strictly from $+\infty$ to $1-\delta$ as $\mu$ rises and the left side rises from $0$ to $\infty$, so there is exactly one root $\mu^*$, and $\mu^*<0$ exactly when the right side at $\mu=0$ is below one. Since $x(0)=x_L(\hat\tau)$, that condition reads $i(1-\bar\tau)\alpha e^{-(1-\alpha)x_L(\hat\tau)}<\delta$, that is $i<\delta\iota$. A non-decreasing, additively homogeneous map does not expand distances in the maximum norm, so $T^{*t}(s_0)\le v+t\mu^*+\|s_0-v\|_\infty$, and $E_C=\ln\alpha+\alpha k+(1-\alpha)n$ inherits the bound. Every assigned-optimal $\tau_t$ maximises $W$ at the date-$t$ state, so it lies in the band by Propositions 10 and 12. With $\bar\tau$ and $\hat\tau$ both replaced by a constant $\tau$ the same construction gives a root $\mu(\tau)$ that is positive exactly when $i>\delta j(\tau)$, and the maximum-norm bound from below, $T_\tau^t(s_0)\ge v+t\mu(\tau)-\|s_0-v\|_\infty$, makes the economy grow at that rate. Finally $\delta j(\tau_m)<1$ exactly when $\delta<\bar\delta$, $\delta j(\tau_m)<\delta\iota$ exactly when $\iota>j(\tau_m)$, and $\delta\iota\ge1$ exactly when $\delta\ge1/\iota$. $\square$

### A.7 The one-generation peaks and Proposition 15

At $\delta=1$ and $\sigma=1$, with $a=0$ and both taxes of Proposition 15 in place,

$$
\begin{aligned}
k'&=\ln i+\ln(1-\tau)+\ln\alpha+\alpha k+(1-\alpha)n,\\
g'&=\ln\varphi+\ln\bigl(\alpha\tau+(1-\alpha)\tau_w\bigr)+\alpha k+(1-\alpha)n,\\
n'&=(1-\gamma)n+\gamma\theta_R\bigl(\ln(1-\tau_w)+\ln(1-\alpha)+\alpha k+(1-\alpha)n\bigr)+\eta g,
\end{aligned}
$$

so the taxes enter every law only through additive constants. Put $V=v_kk+v_nn+v_gg+H$ into the Bellman equation of the owner, whose current payoff is $(1-\omega)\ln C_C+\omega k$. This affine candidate is the owner’s value, as shown below. Matching coefficients gives

$$
v_g=\beta\eta v_n,\qquad v_k=(1-\omega)\alpha+\omega+\alpha\beta S,\qquad \bigl(1-\beta(1-\gamma)\bigr)v_n=(1-\omega)(1-\alpha)+(1-\alpha)\beta S,
$$

with $S:=v_k+\gamma\theta_Rv_n+v_g$. The solution does not depend on either tax, and

$$
v_n=\frac{(1-\alpha)(1-\omega+\beta\omega)}{(1-\beta)D},\qquad D:=1-\alpha\beta+\beta\gamma(1-\theta_R)+\alpha\beta\gamma\theta_R>0.
$$

Each period the owner therefore maximises

$$
F(\tau,\tau_w)=M\ln(1-\tau)+N\ln(1-\tau_w)+A\ln\bigl(\alpha\tau+(1-\alpha)\tau_w\bigr),\qquad M:=(1-\omega)+\beta v_k,\quad N:=\beta\gamma\theta_Rv_n,\quad A:=\beta v_g,
$$

with $M,N,A>0$. $F$ is a positive combination of logarithms of affine functions, and its Hessian has a negative first entry and a positive determinant, so it is strictly concave.

*The candidate is the value.* Write $s=(k,n,g)$. The three laws read $s'=\Gamma s+b(\tau,\tau_w)$ and the period payoff reads $p\cdot s+(1-\omega)\ln(1-\tau)$ plus a constant, where $\Gamma$ has non-negative entries and rows summing to one, $p=\bigl((1-\omega)\alpha+\omega,\,(1-\omega)(1-\alpha),\,0\bigr)$, and $b$ depends on the taxes only through $\ln(1-\tau)$ in its first entry, $\gamma\theta_R\ln(1-\tau_w)$ in its second and $\ln(\alpha\tau+(1-\alpha)\tau_w)$ in its third, each of which is at most zero. Along any plan $s_t=\Gamma^ts_0+\sum_{r<t}\Gamma^{t-1-r}b_r$. The vector $v:=\sum_{j\ge0}\beta^jp\Gamma^j=p(I-\beta\Gamma)^{-1}$ solves $v=p+\beta v\Gamma$, which is the coefficient system above, so $v=(v_k,v_n,v_g)$. Substitute and exchange the order of summation, which is licensed because the terms that carry the taxes are non-positive and the rest are absolutely summable, $\Gamma$ being stochastic and $\beta<1$. The discounted payoff of the plan is then
$$
v\cdot s_0+H_0+\sum_{t\ge0}\beta^tF(\tau_t,\tau_{w,t})\in[-\infty,\infty),
$$
with $H_0$ a constant, and the taxes of each date enter only through $F$ at that date. So the owner’s value is $v\cdot s_0+H_0+\max F/(1-\beta)$, which is the affine candidate with $H=H_0+\max F/(1-\beta)$; a plan is optimal exactly when it maximises $F$ at every date; and at every state $W$ is a constant of the state plus $F$. No transversality condition is needed: the formula holds for every plan, including those whose payoff is $-\infty$. The argument uses only $p\ge0$, so it applies unchanged to a holder of the instrument whose period payoff is $n$, with $p=(0,1,0)$.

*The one-generation peaks.* With $\tau_w\equiv0$ the problem is $\max_\tau M\ln(1-\tau)+A\ln\tau$, whose unique maximiser is $\tau_C=A/(A+M)$; substituting the coefficients gives the display before Proposition 2 at $\sigma=1$. Replacing $\alpha$ and $1-\alpha$ by the partial derivatives of $E_C$ and $E_W$ at the reported ratio, $(1-s_L/\sigma,\,s_L/\sigma)$ and $(s_K/\sigma,\,1-s_K/\sigma)$, and solving the same system gives the display at every $\sigma$. The same computation for a holder of the instrument whose current payoff is $n$, whose coefficients solve the system with $(1-\omega)\alpha+\omega$ and $(1-\omega)(1-\alpha)$ replaced by $0$ and $1$ and whose cost of the tax is $\beta v_k$, gives $\tau_W$.

*Proof of Proposition 15.* At $\tau=0$ and $\tau_w=A/(A+N)$, $\partial F/\partial\tau_w=0$ and

$$
\frac{\partial F}{\partial\tau}=\frac{\alpha(A+N)-(1-\alpha)M}{1-\alpha}.
$$

The equations for $v_k$ and $v_n$ share the term $\beta S$, so $(1-\alpha)(v_k-\omega)=\alpha\bigl(1-\beta(1-\gamma)\bigr)v_n$. Substituting into $M$, $N$ and $A$,

$$
(1-\alpha)M-\alpha(A+N)=(1-\alpha)(1-\omega+\beta\omega)+\alpha\beta(1-\beta)(1-\gamma\theta_R)v_n>0.
$$

The derivative in $\tau$ is negative at the corner, so the Kuhn–Tucker conditions hold at $(0,A/(A+N))$ on $[0,1)^2$, and by strict concavity that point is the unique maximiser. The problem is the same at every state and every date, so the unique optimal plan sets these taxes at every date. With the gross-wage law the coefficients are unchanged and $N$ leaves $F$, which then rises in $\tau_w$; as $\tau_w\to1$, $\partial F/\partial\tau$ at $\tau=0$ tends to $(\alpha A-(1-\alpha)M)/(1-\alpha)<0$ by the same identity. $\square$
