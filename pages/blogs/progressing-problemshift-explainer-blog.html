We Built a Research Programme. Then We Tried to Break It. Here’s What Survived.

Author

Carlos Galindo Escajeda

Published

May 2026

Heterodox economics rarely gets a fair hearing. Its premises are dismissed at the threshold — too political, too unconventional, insufficiently neutral — without anyone pausing to ask whether the body of work has actually been developing in a disciplined and progressive way. The question of whether a heterodox programme is internally rigorous — whether it is generating new content rather than papering over anomalies — is simply never reached.

This paper insists on reaching that question for a specific four-paper sequence on labour power, housing, and value. Not to endorse it. Not to prove it true. To audit it fairly.

1. Four Papers, One Argument

The sequence at stake consists of four pieces that work together as one argument distributed across four different analytical levels.

A theory paper takes the reproduction of labour power — rather than subjective consumer preferences — as the primitive from which value is reckoned, and replaces the standard welfare criterion of Pareto efficiency with one it calls Labor-Power Dominance. A structural paper derives from that criterion a welfare-consistent ceiling on rent, and identifies the mechanisms by which buy-to-let financialization forces rents above that ceiling. An empirical paper tests, across 290 English local authorities and two natural experiments, whether housing benefit is being captured by landlords rather than reaching tenants. A policy paper argues that the standard economic objection to rent regulation is inapplicable to the market Britain actually has.

These are not four separate arguments about related topics. They share a single hard core — the claim that labour power reproduction is the fundamental unit of social value — and address four different implications of that core. Strip out the connecting tissue and each paper becomes weaker. Read together, they constitute a coherent programme.

2. The Lakatosian Test — and Why It’s the Right One

The philosopher of science Imre Lakatos distinguished between two kinds of research programme development. A degenerating programme, when it faces a challenge, adds an untestable auxiliary hypothesis to patch the anomaly — rescuing the core at the cost of making the programme less falsifiable. A progressing programme, by contrast, responds to challenges by revising its protective belt in ways that reduce the programme’s degrees of freedom and increase its explanatory reach. Genuine progress is measured by whether the belt revision restricts the modeller rather than giving them more room.

The paper applies this framework to the four-paper sequence and delivers a deliberately split verdict.

The programme is theoretically progressive. The most important revision in the sequence converted an unproven conjecture about demographic risk — introduced to address a specific anomaly in the formal model — into a signed, closed-form restriction tied to four independently observable parameters. That revision did not add flexibility. It subtracted it. The coefficient that had been a free conjecture became derivable from conditions with empirical content. That is the structural opposite of an ad hoc rescue.

The programme is non-degenerating. Under recorded attacks on the hard core itself — challenges to the most fundamental commitments — the core was preserved through principled refusal and belt revision, not by inventing new untestable claims. The standard of proof required is that the core stays fixed while the belt absorbs and responds to pressure. That standard was met.

But the programme is empirically progressing, not yet empirically progressive. This is the honest part.

3. The Result That Couldn’t Be Claimed — and Why That Matters

The highest standard of Lakatosian progress requires not merely corroboration but corroboration of a novel prediction — a result that the programme predicts and its rivals do not, and that the rivals’ alternative account cannot accommodate after the fact.

The fiscal capture finding — that housing benefit is captured by landlords rather than reaching tenants — looks at first glance like exactly this kind of result. The reproduction framework predicts it structurally, not just behaviorally. The empirical paper confirms it across 26 different analytical specifications with zero sign reversals. That is an unusually robust finding.

The paper’s own methodological discipline required that this result be demoted. The problem is observational equivalence: a simpler behavioral story — landlords follow the LHA rate — predicts the same empirical pattern. The reproduction framework’s prediction is not, in this instance, a genuinely novel prediction that discriminates the programme from its rival. It is shared content. Both the programme and a much simpler story predict fiscal capture.

The paper demotes its own strongest-looking result rather than claim credit for it. This is unusual in academic economics, where the incentive runs powerfully in the other direction.

What remains as the genuine discriminating frontier are the programme’s demographic predictions: that binding areas, with higher concentrations of LHA-dependent households, should show differential household formation rates and population dynamics compared with non-binding areas. Those tests are registered, explicitly falsifiable, and still open.

4. What This Means for the Hiring Package

For a research role at NEF, the methodological point of this paper is also a practical one. The programme demonstrates its credibility not only by accumulating positive results but by its willingness to demote convenient findings when they do not meet the standard it claims to hold. That discipline is the foundation on which policy recommendations can be trusted.

A programme that claims to have proven more than it has is politically useful in the short term and fragile under scrutiny. A programme that is explicit about what it has established, what remains open, and what would refute it is the kind of thing that can sustain serious advocacy. The distinction is exactly what the paper makes.

The registered discriminating tests also create a forward research agenda. If the demographic predictions are confirmed at scale — differential household formation and population growth in high-intensity binding areas — that would be genuine novel corroboration in the Lakatosian sense. It is an empirically tractable question with data that exist. It is waiting to be answered.
