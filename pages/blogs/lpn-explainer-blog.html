We Took Capitalism’s Own Math and Proved It Fails Labor — By Succeeding Perfectly

Author

Julio Huato & Carlos Galindo Escajeda

Published

May 2026

Mainstream economics has a favorite story: competitive markets solve an optimization problem, and the solution is — by the model’s own internal logic — the best of all possible worlds. Resources flow efficiently. Risk is priced correctly. If inequality persists, it is because people started with different endowments, not because the system itself compounds that difference.

We wrote a paper that takes that story’s own formal machinery — Bellman equations, Euler conditions, stochastic discount factors, second-order perturbations — and uses it to prove the story is false. Not in the hand-waving way external critics are usually dismissed for. False by the system’s own derivations, from its own premises, in its own mathematical grammar.

The result is a materialist theory of value, risk, and finance where labor-power reproduction is the fundamental numéraire. In this framework, competitive markets generate structurally necessary violations of labor’s welfare — not despite solving their optimization problem, but precisely because they solve it perfectly.

Here are seven things this paper establishes that mainstream finance theory cannot tell you.

1. Every mainstream growth model is missing a term — and it’s not a technicality

The reproduction technology at the heart of our paper is simple but profound: future labor power depends on both current labor power and current consumption, with exponents that sum to one — constant returns to scale. This is the minimal formal requirement for any society that reproduces itself.

When you take this seriously, a term appears in the Euler equation that has no counterpart in any Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans model: the demographic continuation term. It governs how powerfully today’s labor force carries forward into tomorrow. The higher this term, the more today’s capital inequality compounds into future labor-power deficits.

Prior heterodox formalizations of the materialist hard core either dropped this term or introduced it inconsistently, inadvertently breaking the constant-returns structure that makes the whole framework tractable. Prior stochastic variants dropped it entirely, which quietly destroys the scale invariance of the reproduction technology and voids the derivation. The mainstream never needed to worry about it, because mainstream models don’t treat labor-power reproduction as an objective at all.

This isn’t a minor fix. Without the demographic continuation term, there is no structural risk premium with a signed demographic coefficient, no exact normalization of the value function, and no mathematical object for our welfare criterion to violate. The entire edifice stands or falls with it.

2. We didn’t build a rival model. We turned the mainstream’s apparatus against itself.

Most critiques of neoclassical economics build something different — post-Keynesian, agent-based, or classical input-output models. This paper does something rarer.

We call our strategy methodological immanence: we enter the competitive equilibrium framework on its own terms, accept its Bellman apparatus, and ask what the equilibrium can be shown to prove about itself from its own first-order conditions.

The change this requires is not technical. It is ontological. Mainstream macro asks: how do atomistic individuals optimize their subjective utility? The materialist framework asks: what does the economy require to reproduce the collective capacity to labor? These are not different ways of approaching the same question. They are different theories of what an economy is, who its subjects are, and what value means. The stochastic discount factor, the Bellman equations, and the second-order perturbation technique are all the same. But when labor-power reproduction displaces subjective utility as the fundamental primitive, the demographic continuation term reappears in the Euler equation, the pricing kernel acquires a second structural risk channel, and the welfare criterion that makes visible what competitive equilibria fail to achieve can be stated precisely.

This immanent proof establishes what external critiques cannot. The failure is not contingent on market imperfections, regulatory capture, or financial fragility. It is structurally necessary — a deduction from the competitive equilibrium’s own first-order conditions, on its own ground, against its own standard of proof.

3. The risk premium on capital has two channels — one of them is demographic

Standard consumption-based asset pricing says risky assets earn a premium because they pay off poorly when consumption falls. That channel exists here too.

But our framework reveals a second channel no mainstream model contains: a demographic risk channel. An asset that performs badly precisely when society’s reproductive capacity contracts earns an additional premium. The coefficient on this channel — what we call Omega — is analytically signed positive under two mild, economically interpretable conditions about the curvature of the value function and the savings response to technology shocks.

This is not a free parameter added to fit the data. It emerges directly from the structure of the Bellman problem once the demographic continuation term is present. The pricing kernel responds to reproductive risk because the shadow value of capital that appears in the kernel is the Bellman multiplier governing reproductive capacity itself — not subjective utility. Change the primitive, and the kernel changes.

The macroeconomic implication is stark: when uncertainty about future labor power rises, precautionary demand drives up the price of safe assets and crowds out real investment. Finance does not appear here as a neutral intermediary; it is the institutional form through which the risk of future labor-power deficits is priced and redistributed across dynasties.

4. The risk price cannot be calibrated away

Mainstream asset pricing has a famous flexibility problem: its effective risk-aversion coefficient is a free parameter. It can match almost any observed equity premium simply by turning a dial.

The materialist framework has no such flexibility. The effective risk-aversion coefficient is pinned down by a cross-equation restriction that ties it to four observables:

  • The reproduction elasticity (identifiable from cohort fertility data)
  • The Bellman curvature of the value function (pinned by steady-state conditions)
  • The capital-ratio savings response (recoverable from investment-share dynamics)
  • The normalized consumption response to technology shocks

This makes the materialist framework a testable structural prediction. Standard CCAPM can match any risk premium by turning a dial; this framework cannot. If the observed risk premium is inconsistent with the reproduction technology parameters, the model is falsified — not rescued by recalibration. A theory that cannot be falsified by data is not a scientific theory; this one can be.

5. Perfect markets still fail labor — and fail it persistently

Our welfare criterion replaces Pareto efficiency — and the replacement is not cosmetic.

Pareto efficiency accepts preferences as given and asks whether they can be simultaneously satisfied. But preferences are themselves shaped by class position. A worker’s preference for immediate consumption over deferred investment is not a natural datum; it is a consequence of wage dependence and constrained reproduction under conditions of necessity. A welfare criterion built on such preferences takes the class structure as its own foundation and then evaluates allocations on that basis. It cannot, by design, evaluate whether the structure itself is adequate. It is an apology for a prior inequality dressed in the language of optimality.

LPD does not ask whether preferences are satisfied. It asks whether the physical preconditions for the reproduction of the labor force are met, for every dynasty, under every allocation.

Labor-Power Dominance (LPD): Allocation A dominates Allocation B if every dynasty ends up with at least as much discounted expected labor power, and at least one dynasty is strictly better off. The criterion ranks allocations directly over physical reproductive capacity — not over utility.

Under LPD, competitive markets fail systematically whenever initial capital is unequally distributed. This is not a crisis result or a market-failure result. It holds in the cleanest possible competitive benchmark: complete markets, full insurance against idiosyncratic shocks, and perfectly competitive factor pricing.

The capital-rich dynasty grows its labor force faster — not because it is more productive at reproduction, but because it consumes more, and consumption is an input to reproduction with a positive elasticity. The demographic continuation term turns this into a permanent, multiplicative growth-rate differential: each period of advantage multiplies into the next, so the ratio of labor-power levels between dynasties is strictly increasing over time.

Credit markets, labor markets, and wage equalization all fail to restore LPD. They reorganize the hierarchy; they do not dissolve it.

6. Capitalism turns future human beings into a financial asset class

The paper closes a formal gap that Harvey, Foley, and the circuit-of-capital tradition identified but could not close without dynamic stochastic methods: the derivation of asset prices directly from the conditions of labor-power reproduction.

The safe bond is priced by the same demographic stochastic discount factor that prices risky capital. Its yield is lower precisely because it insures against reproductive-risk variance. Demand for safe assets surges when uncertainty about future labor power rises. The bond aggregates the cost of missing insurance markets across dynasties, and in doing so it prices the future reproductive capacity of society as if it were a financial claim.

This is fictitious capital in its structural form — not in the narrow Marxian sense of capitalized surplus value, but in the broader sense: an asset whose price capitalizes a ranked structure of claims on future social labor through the material conditions of reproduction. The precautionary demand that fills financial balance sheets with “safe” assets is, at its structural foundation, demand for insurance against the risk that society will not reproduce enough labor power to service those claims.

Finance, in this framework, is not an aberration, a parasite, or an external distortion imposed on an otherwise sound productive base. It is constitutive. The capitalist system generates reproductive insecurity through the unequal distribution of the means of subsistence — and then converts that insecurity into yield-bearing claims on future social labor. It produces the condition and sells insurance against it. The terror of reproductive failure is not incidental to capitalist finance; it is its raw material, converted into yield.

7. This is a research program, not a closed result

The paper is theoretical. It establishes the structural restrictions and the welfare violations. The empirical and institutional work follows in a companion research program:

  • Empirical identification: GMM moment conditions confronting the cross-equation restriction with cohort fertility data, normalized consumption series, and asset-pricing moments.
  • Class dynamics: A fully specified heterogeneous-agent model separating liquidity-constrained working dynasties from accumulating capitalist dynasties, to derive the full distributional consequences of the LPD violation.
  • Axiomatics: Formally mapping Labor-Power Dominance against Paretian and utilitarian criteria.

One final point for readers who know the heterodox literature. The stationarity assumption the framework requires — the ergodic structure of dynamic programming — is the price of admission for an immanent critique. It is a mathematical requirement to prove failure from within the mainstream’s own apparatus, not a historical claim that capitalism is actually stable. The stationary pricing kernel is the benchmark that any future extension incorporating crisis dynamics, class transitions, or non-ergodic reproductive trajectories must be consistent with.

Even in its most idealized, efficient, stationary form, capitalism fails labor — not by failing to optimize, but by succeeding.
