Your Housing Benefit Is Going to Your Landlord. We Can Now Measure How Much.

Author

Carlos Galindo Escajeda

Published

May 2026

The UK government spends over £20 billion a year on housing support. The official claim: this money helps low-income people afford their homes. The empirical reality, now measured with government data across 290 English local authorities and two policy experiments, tells a different story. Much of it ends up with landlords.

Not through fraud. Not through individual bad actors. Through a structural mechanism that has been embedded in the design of Local Housing Allowance since its introduction — and that two natural experiments, across 26 different analytical methods, now confirm.

Here is what we found, and what it means.

1. The Floor That Moves the Ceiling

In areas where many tenants rely on housing benefit, the Local Housing Allowance (LHA) does not operate as a helping hand. It operates as a price anchor. Landlords set rents at or near the LHA rate because they know two things: that is the maximum their tenants can pay, and the government will pay it.

The consequence is fiscal capture: the public transfer that is nominally for tenants structurally accrues to property owners. When the LHA goes up, rents follow. When it is frozen, rent growth slows. The benefit does not improve the tenant’s position — it improves the landlord’s revenue.

This is not a new claim. Gibbons and Manning (2006) found that 60–67% of UK housing benefit accrues to landlords rather than tenants, using data from the 1990s reforms. DWP’s own econometric analysis of the 2011–12 LHA cuts confirmed rent reductions in the areas most affected. What has been missing is direct, high-resolution evidence from the most recent policy cycle — and a theory that explains why the mechanism persists even in a nominally competitive rental market.

2. Parliament Accidentally Ran Two Experiments

Governments rarely provide economists with clean natural experiments. The 2020–21 LHA policy cycle is a rare exception.

April 2020: After years of freezing LHA below market rents, the government restored it to the 30th percentile of local market rents — the largest uprating since 2016. If fiscal capture is real, rents in high-benefit areas should have accelerated immediately. Landlords could charge more because their tenants’ budgets had just expanded.

April 2021: LHA was re-frozen at the 2020 cash level, while market rents continued rising. If fiscal capture is real, rent growth in those same areas should have decelerated — the floor was now being held below the rising market, and the yield advantage of binding areas should fade.

Both predictions come true in the data. Areas with higher concentrations of LHA-dependent households saw rent growth accelerate at the 2020 uplift and decelerate at the 2021 re-freeze — exactly as the fiscal capture theory predicts, and exactly opposite to what you would observe if LHA were operating as a neutral income support rather than a price anchor.

3. We Checked 26 Different Ways. The Answer Was Always the Same.

A single finding can always be dismissed: statistical noise, a convenient choice of time window, a sample that was selected to produce the desired result. We therefore ran 26 different versions of the analysis — varying:

  • The time window (different start and end dates, including a version that uses the 2020 uplift period itself as the comparison baseline)
  • Whether to control for post-COVID urban density recovery
  • Whether to restrict the sample to lower-migration areas
  • The bedroom type (1-bed, 2-bed, 3-bed, 4-bed separately)
  • Whether to down-weight large local authorities
  • Whether to treat the treatment variable as continuous or as a binary threshold
  • The clustering of standard errors

Across all 26 analyses — temporal, spatial, compositional, measurement — every single estimated effect points in the same direction. Zero sign reversals. This is not cherry-picking; it is structural consistency. The fiscal capture pattern persists under every analytical lens.

4. It Is Not Displacement

The most common objection to this kind of finding is that the pattern reflects population displacement, not fiscal capture. As LHA is frozen and real incomes fall, lower-income households move out of high-benefit areas. Rents “slow” because the remaining tenants are wealthier — not because landlords are responding to the LHA rate.

We tested this directly. We excluded the 30 English local authorities with the highest rates of net in-migration — the places most likely to be experiencing significant population churn — and re-ran the analysis on the remaining 261 areas. The result did not disappear. It got slightly stronger: the estimated effect is more negative in non-displacement areas than in the full sample.

Fiscal capture is not a displacement artefact. It operates in areas where the population is stable. It is structural, not compositional.

5. The Headline Number Is a Lower Bound

The primary estimate — roughly a 1.7 percentage point reduction in rent growth per unit of LHA intensity — may sound modest. Two features of the study design guarantee it is an underestimate.

First, landlords are reluctant to cut nominal rents even when their LHA floor retreats below the market. This downward nominal rigidity attenuates the measured effect of the re-freeze, particularly in the early quarters after April 2021.

Second, in dense urban areas — which are also high-LHA-intensity areas — post-COVID demand for private renting surged for reasons entirely unrelated to LHA policy. This independent demand recovery in binding areas partially masks the rent deceleration from the freeze.

When we strip out both sources of attenuation — by using the 2020 uplift period as the comparison baseline (which identifies the predicted reversal from accelerated growth at the uplift to decelerated growth at the re-freeze) and controlling explicitly for urban density recovery — the estimated effect is roughly four times larger. That is the fiscal capture signal, isolated from the noise.

6. The Reproduction Trap

The Labor-Power as Numéraire (LPN) framework provides the theoretical grounding for why this mechanism is not merely a market distortion but a structural feature of the UK housing economy.

Housing costs for benefit-dependent households are not just a consumption expenditure — they are inputs to social reproduction. The capacity to work, to form households, to raise children requires that non-housing consumption — food, childcare, transport, heating — remain above a threshold. LHA in high-benefit areas is not primarily a housing supplement; it is a reproduction subsidy maintaining that threshold.

When LHA is frozen and market rents continue rising, the real value of that subsidy falls. The rent deceleration we measure is not merely landlords receiving slightly less revenue — it is the reproduction constraint tightening on the least economically secure households in England. Households do not simply substitute away from housing; they face compressing capacity across all non-housing necessities simultaneously.

The LPN framework predicts that the fiscal capture mechanism should therefore produce demographic consequences — slower household formation, slower population growth — in high-intensity areas after the re-freeze, independently of the rent growth deceleration itself. Testing that prediction is the next step in this research programme.

What Comes Next

The finding is clear: LHA cannot be reformed in isolation. As long as housing benefit can be freely capitalised into rents, raising the benefit raises rents, and the net benefit to tenants approaches zero. The Gibbons–Manning finding from 2006 — that the majority of housing benefit flows to landlords — has now been confirmed with updated, high-resolution data across the most recent policy cycle.

Two changes are necessary together. First, a rent ceiling calibrated to the LHA rate in binding local authorities — not a blanket national freeze, but a binding constraint on LHA-anchored tenancies that prevents landlords from extracting above the benefit in areas where fiscal capture is confirmed. Second, reformed LHA design that supplements tenant consumption directly rather than subsidising landlord revenue flows through the rent mechanism.

Neither change is straightforward. Both are politically contested. But the evidence that they are necessary — 290 local authorities, two natural experiments, 26 empirical tests, consistent direction — is now on the table.

The housing benefit was designed to help people who cannot afford market rents. The data now says it has been, in large part, helping their landlords instead. That is a political choice. It can be made differently.
