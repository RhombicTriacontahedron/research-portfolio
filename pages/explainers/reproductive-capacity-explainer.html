Reproductive Capacity Explainer

Mainstream economics has a favourite story: competitive markets solve an optimization problem, and the solution is — by the model’s own internal logic — the best of all possible worlds. Resources flow efficiently. Risk is priced correctly. If inequality persists, it is because people started with different endowments, not because the system itself compounds that difference.

This paper takes that story’s own formal machinery — Bellman equations, Euler conditions, stochastic discount factors, second-order perturbations — and uses it to show the story is incomplete. Not in the hand-waving way external critiques are usually dismissed for. On the framework’s own derivations, from its own premises, in its own mathematical grammar.

The result is a structural theory of value, risk, and finance in which reproductive capacity — society’s physical capacity to sustain and renew its population — is the fundamental numéraire. In this framework, competitive markets generate structurally necessary shortfalls in the population’s welfare — not despite solving their optimization problem, but precisely because they solve it perfectly.

From that one move, a series of consequences follows that standard finance theory does not reach.

1. Every mainstream growth model is missing a term — and it’s not a technicality

The reproduction technology at the heart of the paper is simple but consequential: future reproductive capacity depends on both current reproductive capacity and current consumption, with exponents that sum to one — constant returns to scale. This is the minimal formal requirement for any economy that reproduces itself.

When you take this seriously, a term appears in the Euler equation that has no counterpart in any Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans model: the demographic continuation term. It governs how powerfully today’s population carries forward into tomorrow. The higher this term, the more today’s capital inequality compounds into future reproductive-capacity deficits.

Prior structural formalizations either dropped this term or introduced it inconsistently, inadvertently breaking the constant-returns structure that makes the framework tractable. Stochastic variants dropped it entirely, which quietly destroys the scale invariance of the reproduction technology and voids the derivation. Mainstream models never needed it, because they don’t treat reproductive capacity as an objective at all.

This isn’t a minor fix. Without the demographic continuation term, there is no structural risk premium with a signed demographic coefficient, no exact normalization of the value function, and no mathematical object for the welfare criterion to register. The entire edifice stands or falls with it.

2. Not a rival model — the mainstream’s apparatus turned on itself

Most critiques of neoclassical economics build something different — post-Keynesian, agent-based, or classical input-output models. This paper does something rarer.

The strategy is methodological immanence: enter the competitive equilibrium framework on its own terms, accept its Bellman apparatus, and ask what the equilibrium can be shown to prove about itself from its own first-order conditions.

The change is not technical but in the primitive. Mainstream macro asks how atomistic individuals optimize subjective utility; this framework asks what an economy requires to sustain the collective capacity of its population. The stochastic discount factor, the Bellman equations, and the second-order perturbation are all the same. But when reproductive capacity displaces subjective utility as the fundamental primitive, the demographic continuation term reappears in the Euler equation, the pricing kernel acquires a second structural risk channel, and a welfare criterion that makes visible what competitive equilibria fail to achieve can be stated precisely.

This immanent result establishes what external critiques cannot. The shortfall is not contingent on market imperfections, regulatory capture, or financial fragility. It is structurally necessary — a deduction from the competitive equilibrium’s own first-order conditions, on its own ground, against its own standard of proof.

3. The risk premium on capital has two channels — one of them demographic

Standard consumption-based asset pricing says risky assets earn a premium because they pay off poorly when consumption falls. That channel exists here too.

But the framework reveals a second channel no mainstream model contains: a demographic risk channel. An asset that performs badly precisely when society’s reproductive capacity contracts earns an additional premium. The coefficient on this channel — what the paper calls Omega — is analytically signed positive under two mild, economically interpretable conditions on the curvature of the value function and the savings response to technology shocks.

This is not a free parameter added to fit the data. It emerges directly from the structure of the Bellman problem once the demographic continuation term is present. The pricing kernel responds to reproductive risk because the shadow value of capital that appears in the kernel is the Bellman multiplier governing reproductive capacity itself — not subjective utility. Change the primitive, and the kernel changes.

The macroeconomic implication is stark: when uncertainty about future reproductive capacity rises, precautionary demand drives up the price of safe assets and crowds out real investment. Finance does not appear here as a neutral intermediary; it is the institutional form through which the risk of future reproductive-capacity deficits is priced and redistributed across dynasties.

4. The risk price cannot be calibrated away

Mainstream asset pricing has a famous flexibility problem: its effective risk-aversion coefficient is a free parameter. It can match almost any observed equity premium simply by turning a dial.

The structural framework has no such flexibility. The effective risk-aversion coefficient is pinned down by a cross-equation restriction that ties it to four observables:

  • The reproduction elasticity (identifiable from cohort fertility data)
  • The Bellman curvature of the value function (pinned by steady-state conditions)
  • The capital-ratio savings response (recoverable from investment-share dynamics)
  • The normalized consumption response to technology shocks

This makes the framework a testable structural prediction. Standard CCAPM can match any risk premium by turning a dial; this framework cannot. If the observed risk premium is inconsistent with the reproduction technology parameters, the model is falsified — not rescued by recalibration. A theory that cannot be falsified by data is not a scientific theory; this one can be.

5. Perfect markets still fall short — and persistently

The welfare criterion replaces Pareto efficiency — and the replacement is not cosmetic.

Pareto efficiency accepts preferences as given and asks whether they can be simultaneously satisfied. By construction, it cannot evaluate whether the underlying distribution of reproductive capacity is itself adequate: it takes the initial structure as a datum and ranks allocations on that basis. A criterion built that way registers efficiency given an unequal starting point, but is silent on whether the starting point leaves every household able to sustain itself.

Reproductive-Capacity Dominance does not ask whether preferences are satisfied. It asks whether the physical preconditions for the reproduction of the population are met, for every dynasty, under every allocation.

Reproductive-Capacity Dominance (RCD): Allocation A dominates Allocation B if every dynasty ends up with at least as much discounted expected reproductive capacity, and at least one dynasty is strictly better off. The criterion ranks allocations directly over physical reproductive capacity — not over utility.

Under RCD, competitive markets fall short systematically whenever initial capital is unequally distributed. This is not a crisis result or a market-failure result. It holds in the cleanest possible competitive benchmark: complete markets, full insurance against idiosyncratic shocks, and perfectly competitive factor pricing.

The capital-rich dynasty grows its population faster — not because it is more productive at reproduction, but because it consumes more, and consumption is an input to reproduction with a positive elasticity. The demographic continuation term turns this into a permanent, multiplicative growth-rate differential: each period of advantage multiplies into the next, so the ratio of reproductive-capacity levels between dynasties is strictly increasing over time.

Credit markets, labour markets, and wage equalization all fail to restore RCD. They reorganize the hierarchy; they do not dissolve it.

6. Future reproductive capacity, priced as a financial claim

The paper closes a formal gap that prior structural-finance work identified but could not close without dynamic stochastic methods: deriving asset prices directly from the conditions of reproduction.

The safe bond is priced by the same demographic stochastic discount factor that prices risky capital. Its yield is lower precisely because it insures against reproductive-risk variance. Demand for safe assets surges when uncertainty about future reproductive capacity rises. The bond aggregates the cost of missing insurance markets across dynasties, and in doing so it prices society’s future reproductive capacity as if it were a financial claim.

This is a capitalised claim in its structural form — an asset whose price capitalises a ranked structure of claims on future income through the conditions of reproduction. The precautionary demand that fills financial balance sheets with “safe” assets is, at its structural foundation, demand for insurance against the risk that society will not reproduce enough capacity to service those claims.

Finance, in this framework, is not an external distortion imposed on an otherwise sound productive base; it is constitutive. The unequal distribution of basic necessities generates reproductive insecurity — and that insecurity is then converted into yield-bearing claims on future income. The system prices the very condition it helps create.

7. A research program, not a closed result

The paper is theoretical. It establishes the structural restrictions and the welfare shortfalls. The empirical and institutional work follows in a companion research program:

  • Empirical identification: GMM moment conditions confronting the cross-equation restriction with cohort fertility data, normalized consumption series, and asset-pricing moments.
  • Group dynamics: A fully specified heterogeneous-agent model separating liquidity-constrained dynasties from accumulating ones, to derive the full distributional consequences of the RCD shortfall.
  • Axiomatics: Formally mapping Reproductive-Capacity Dominance against Paretian and utilitarian criteria.

One final point for readers who know the structural literature. The stationarity assumption the framework requires — the ergodic structure of dynamic programming — is the price of admission for an immanent argument: a mathematical requirement to establish the shortfall from within the mainstream’s own apparatus, not a historical claim that the economy is actually stable. The stationary pricing kernel is the benchmark that any future extension incorporating crisis dynamics, regime transitions, or non-ergodic reproductive trajectories must be consistent with.

Even in its most idealized, efficient, stationary form, the competitive market falls short of sustaining the population — not by failing to optimize, but by optimizing perfectly.
