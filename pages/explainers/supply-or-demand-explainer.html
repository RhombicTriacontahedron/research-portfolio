Was the Inflation Surge ‘Supply’ or ‘Demand’?

When inflation took off after the pandemic, policy had to act on a name — and the name decided who would pay. The central banks first called it transitory: a supply-and-reopening shock that would fade, best looked through. They held that read for the better part of a year and fell behind it, as the rise broadened from goods into services and refused to fade. Then the name flipped to persistent demand, and the brakes slammed — the steepest hiking cycle in decades, a squeeze reaching households through mortgages and rents on top of the prices already climbing. The bill went out late, and all at once. (A third tradition had rejected the choice from the start: firms were using disruption to widen margins, the rate was the wrong instrument, and the fix lay in coordinating fiscal and monetary policy — not in lurching from look-through to crash-stop.)

Both swings rested on one buried claim — that the data themselves can tell supply-driven inflation from demand-driven. So I put it to the test: I took the standard tool economists use, pointed it at the 2021–23 US surge, and ran it two defensible ways — the quick way most research desks reach for, and the more demanding way the theory actually asks for. The answers were not mildly different; they were worlds apart. The data had not changed — only the assumption had, and that is where the whole story turns.

1. The data cannot decide — the assumption does

Here is the uncomfortable truth underneath every “supply versus demand” decomposition: the raw numbers cannot make the split on their own. Prices and output move together for many reasons, and nothing in the data says which movement was supply and which was demand. Before any decomposition exists, the analyst must impose an assumption — a rule for telling the two apart. That assumption does the real work, and it is usually invisible to the reader.

2. The shortcut almost everyone runs — and its known flaw

The most common shortcut is built into the workhorse model many research desks run: put energy prices first in a recursive ordering, which assumes commodity prices cannot react to the wider economy within the same month. By construction, every surprise move in energy then becomes a “supply shock.” It is fast and always available. It also carries a flaw known since the late 2000s: when the world economy booms, oil prices rise with it, inside the month. The shortcut cannot tell an OPEC cut from a demand boom — it files both under supply. It is the workhorse not because it is right but because it is convenient — and that convenience does not save you time so much as quietly choose your answer.

3. The theory-consistent alternative: did it behave like a supply shock?

There is a more structural way, and it asks what theory actually says. A genuine energy-supply disruption raises prices while squeezing output. A demand boom raises prices while output expands. Require only that distinction — nothing more — and keep every reading of the data consistent with it. Then look at how much of the surge energy supply is actually left holding.

4. Same data, same model — two verdicts

Run both rules on the same episode and trace the surge back to its drivers:

  • The recursive shortcut blames energy supply for 33% of the above-baseline surge.
  • Require the behaviour test, and the median energy-supply share collapses to about 3%.

That collapse is narrower than it looks, and the narrowness matters. What falls away is the energy-supply channel specifically — the one thing the shortcut claims to measure. The broader supply forces in the surge — shipping bottlenecks, pandemic shortages — are not refuted; they sit in a large slice of the model deliberately left unlabelled. Nor does the honest method hand back a single number: it returns a whole range of theory-consistent answers, and the spread is the point — the data will not pin the split down on their own. What they will not carry is the confident energy-supply story: across a thousand sampled answers, almost all give that channel a small role, and not one puts it above half.

The data did not change. The model did not change. Yet the headline verdict swung more than tenfold — and nothing but the assumption moved it.

5. The clearest tell: the same oil run-up, two labels

You can see the mislabelling on the sharpest case of all — the oil run-up itself. The shortcut assigns essentially all of crude’s 2021–22 climb to an energy-supply shock, because that is what it is built to do. The behaviour test — asking whether the climb actually behaved like a supply disruption — assigns it a median of only about 8%. Same run-up, relabelled, not re-measured.

6. What this is — and, just as carefully, what it is not

This is a preliminary methods demonstration, offered for discussion — not a finding to bank. Three honesties travel with it:

  • It refutes a label, not a phenomenon: the narrow “energy-supply shock” the shortcut claims to isolate — not the bottlenecks and shortages everyone lived through, which sit untouched in the model’s unlabelled remainder.
  • The structural answer comes with honest uncertainty: the method gives a range, not a single figure, and that spread is itself the finding.
  • Central banks are told to look through supply shocks and to lean against demand, so a wrong label risks the wrong policy. But this cuts against triumphalism in either direction: it is not an argument against the policy rate — where cost pass-through is at work, a single rate is second-best for relative prices yet first-best for anchoring expectations. A rate can break demand, though; it cannot unclog a port or roll back a margin — so where the surge ran through bottlenecks, or (as profit-decomposition work argues) through firms widening margins behind them, the rate reaches the problem only by squeezing the household. Weigh targeted tools alongside the rate, never instead of it — and build the standing capacity to deploy them, authorised in advance and rule-based, rather than default to the one lever already to hand. Diagnosis first, then dosage.

How it was built

The point was to show the method, not to win an argument. The model is a four-variable monthly SVAR — the oil price, US industrial production, consumer prices, and the federal funds rate — identified by how shocks behave rather than by an arbitrary ordering: the sign pattern above, plus a persistence rule fixed before the data were touched, not tuned afterward to taste. It was estimated in two fully independent implementations that agree to thirteen decimal places, far too close for a coding error to hide; the headline medians and bounds were then reproduced from the archived data by an independent reviewer, and the whole thing went through external, adversarial review with the corrections folded in. Every figure traces to public data (FRED) and a regenerable artifact. AI did plenty of the typing; it marked none of its own homework.

The transferable lesson

Next time a study pins a surge on “supply” — or, just as often, on “demand” — ask one question of it: could its method, even in principle, have returned the other answer? If not, the label, not the evidence, may be doing the work. And a confident diagnosis resting on a buried assumption is a thin foundation for the blunt instrument it is made to justify — a single rate set against households’ mortgages and rents. The honest response to a verdict the data will not actually deliver is humility about the diagnosis, and a policy mix — fiscal and monetary, coordinated — that does not stake everyone’s welfare on getting one contested label right. Knowing which is which, and saying so plainly when the data will not say, is the whole job.
