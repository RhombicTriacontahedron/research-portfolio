Rent Channel Explainer

Between 2021 and 2024 the Bank of England raised interest rates from near zero to 5.25 per cent, and private rents rose sharply. A tidy explanation took hold in the commentary: leveraged buy-to-let landlords were facing higher mortgage bills, and they were passing those costs straight on to their tenants. Tightening, on this view, was partly self-defeating — the central bank’s own medicine was feeding the rent inflation it was trying to cure.

It is a plausible story, and it carries a clear policy instruction: if rents are being driven by landlords’ financing costs, then policy should go after those costs. That is a big enough claim to be worth testing rather than repeating. So we tested it — directly, on government data, with the answer written down before we looked.

Here is what we found, and where it points.

1. A popular story with a real policy fork in it

The claim is specific: higher rates raise landlords’ costs, and landlords with pricing power pass them through. If that is what happened, the remedy is to target the cost — relief or limits on buy-to-let financing, pressure on the mortgage channel.

But there is a rival reading in which rates barely touch rents directly, and the pressure on tenants comes from somewhere else entirely. The two stories look the same from a distance — rates up, rents up — but they point at opposite policies. Getting the diagnosis right is not an academic nicety; it decides where scarce political effort goes.

2. We looked exactly where the theory says the effect must be

If higher financing costs are passed into rents, the effect cannot be uniform. It has to be largest where landlords are most exposed to interest rates — the places where house prices are high relative to rents, so that a given rate rise eats a bigger share of the rental income. That is what cost-plus pricing predicts.

So we built a panel of all 290 English local authorities and asked a sharp version of the question: did rents rise faster in the more interest-sensitive areas as financing costs climbed? Crucially, we measured the cost landlords actually paid — the effective rate on their outstanding mortgage debt — not the headline Bank Rate. And, to keep ourselves honest, the design and the decision rules were committed in advance, before any estimate was run.

3. There was nothing there — and “nothing” is the useful part

The interest-sensitive areas did not see faster rent growth. The differential effect is a precise zero.

A null result can sound like a shrug — we couldn’t find anything. This one is the opposite of a shrug, because we can say what it rules out. Restoring landlords’ yields through full textbook pass-through would have required rent increases several times larger than the biggest effect our design could plausibly have missed. The data exclude roughly four-fifths of that textbook benchmark. This is not “we don’t know”; it is “we can rule out the big, scary version of the landlord-cost story.”

4. The landlord paid far less than the headline rate

Part of the reason is hiding in plain sight. While Bank Rate marched to 5.25 per cent, the rate landlords actually paid on their existing debt rose to only about 3.8 per cent by early 2024. Most buy-to-let borrowing is fixed or interest-only, and it rolls off its old deals slowly, over years. The cost shock to sitting landlords was muted and lagged — nothing like the headline number.

That gap between the policy rate and the cost that landlords actually face is itself a fact worth holding onto: monetary tightening reaches the rental sector slowly and incompletely, not with the immediacy the simple story assumes.

5. The scary-looking chart was a mirage

There is a version of the data that looks alarming: plot rent growth against interest-sensitivity and you can find the most exposed areas growing more slowly — a result some would seize on as evidence of something perverse.

It dissolves on inspection. That gradient is a long-running north–south convergence in rents that was already under way well before the rate-rising cycle began — cheaper regions slowly catching up to dearer ones. Hold the pre-2019 period up to the light and the same pattern is already there, with rates flat. It is not a monetary effect at all. A panel design that absorbs the trend makes it vanish, which is exactly why the careful answer and the eyeballed answer disagree.

6. So what did drive it? The floor that didn’t move

If it was not the landlords’ costs, where was the pressure coming from? The binding constraint sat on the tenant side. Rents were pressing against household budgets while the cash value of Local Housing Allowance — the support that low-income private renters actually live on — had been frozen since April 2021.

Read alongside the companion evidence on who bore the squeeze when benefits were frozen, the result is consistent with the harm running through a gap between monetary and fiscal policy — rates going up while the safety-net floor was held down — rather than a clean pipe from rates to rents. It does not prove a coordination failure; it removes the most popular alternative and points firmly at the floor that was allowed to fall behind.

What comes next

The policy reading is the part that matters. If you want to relieve tenants in an episode like this one, going after landlords’ financing costs is aiming at the wrong target. The leverage is on the demand side: a housing safety net that is indexed to prices rather than frozen in cash, and monetary and fiscal policy that are not pulling households in opposite directions at the same time.

There is an honest limit worth stating plainly. Rents are sticky: they do not jump to a new cost target, they drift toward it at the speed the stock reprices. The proper way to capture that is a dynamic model in which this year’s rent leans on last year’s. We do not estimate it here — with only about five years of quarterly data, the standard tools for that kind of model are unreliable, and in any case, if the target the rent is drifting toward shows no exposure-difference, the slow drift toward it stays flat too. It is the right instrument for a 2027 replication, once a longer run of data after the rate peak makes the speed of adjustment something you can actually measure. This is a short-run answer, offered as one.

What it already does is clear the ground. The next time rents climb and the reflex is to blame the landlords’ mortgages, there is now a test on the record that says: look at the floor.
