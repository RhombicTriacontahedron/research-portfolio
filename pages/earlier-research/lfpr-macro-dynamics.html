Long and Short Run Dynamics of the U.S. Macroeconomy (2011)

Earlier, peer-reviewed research. Published in The Business Review, Cambridge, Vol. 18, No. 1, Summer 2011 (ISSN 1553–5827). Co-authored with Dr. Oscar J. Galindo Tijerina (Universidad Autónoma Chapingo, Mexico) and Marina Chernyak. The connection to this site’s main work is in the final section.

In January 2011, the U.S. labour force participation rate hit a 26-year low. People were leaving the labour force faster than at any point since the early 1980s, and the question that followed was not merely statistical: was this exit permanent, or would it reverse?

The answer mattered acutely for monetary policy. If the decline was structural — a genuine, lasting reduction in labour supply — then potential output was lower than the old benchmarks implied, slack was smaller, and a central bank reading the economy against pre-crisis norms risked setting rates too loose. If the decline was mostly cyclical — workers sitting out a bad market who would return when conditions improved — then slack was larger than it looked, and the same central bank risked the opposite error: tightening too soon, choking off a recovery that hadn’t finished.

Getting that distinction wrong has real consequences for real households. This paper tried to get it right.

1. A structural decline with a cyclical interior

The headline picture — fifty years of data, a long fall from a peak above 67 per cent toward 62 per cent — looks like a one-way trend. It is not quite. Embedded inside the secular decline is a procyclical pattern: participation falls in downturns and recovers in expansions. The two movements overlap in the data and can easily be mistaken for one another.

Standard treatments at the time largely took the participation rate as given, or as a simple function of unemployment. We took a different approach: model the participation rate and its major demographic components together with the rest of the macroeconomy inside a multivariate system closely related to the structural models central banks use for conditional forecasting. The question was whether separating the trend from the cycle produced better forecasts — and what the answer implied for where the participation rate, and potential output, would settle.

2. The model had to earn its place

We estimated a collection of unrestricted vector autoregressions fitted to 51 years of quarterly U.S. data (1959 Q1 to 2010 Q4), drawing on publicly available BLS and FRED series. The variable set — output, inflation, investment, consumption, wages, hours, interest rates, unemployment, and the participation rate broken into age cohorts — mirrors the core of the Smets–Wouters structural model that most major central banks now run in some form, with the demographic layer added on top.

The model earned its place empirically. Adding the participation rate and its cohort breakdown significantly improved fit by every standard information criterion. Structural-stability tests that the simpler specification failed were not rejected for the augmented version. Correlations between model-predicted and actual participation for each cohort were consistently above 0.99. The model picks up the dynamics the data contain rather than imposing a theoretical prior on them.

3. Four cohorts, four different stories

The demographic decomposition is where the forecast becomes concrete and where the policy stakes become sharp.

Workers aged 16 to 24 had been withdrawing from the labour force as tuition costs rose and access to financial aid tightened. That part of the trend is real and not obviously cyclical.

Prime-age men (25 to 54) present a more complicated picture. A significant share had exited via disability — applications rose 26 per cent over the two prior years, and those who leave through that route typically do not return. But a portion of the prime-age male decline is genuinely procyclical: discouraged workers who withdrew into inactivity and who will step back in when conditions improve. The model suggests a rebound.

Workers over 55 had been heading in the opposite direction for two decades, and the trend was expected to continue. They are healthier and longer-lived than any previous cohort; changes to retirement ages and to deferred-retirement incentives had extended their labour-market attachment and were not reversing.

Prime-age women are the key uncertain variable. Their procyclicality is strong enough that, if recovery takes hold, the net effect across all four cohorts could imply a meaningful rebound in aggregate participation — not a one-way slide to a new structural floor.

4. What the forecast implied for monetary policy

The model’s central forecast placed the participation rate at roughly 63.7 per cent by the first quarter of 2015 — declining, but at a substantially slower pace than a naive extrapolation of the trend implied, and in line with the earlier Aaronson–Fallick projections that had used different methods to arrive at a similar conclusion.

The long-run picture the model implied was a participation rate settling near 62 per cent, with real GDP growth near 3.6 per cent. That matters because the range between 58 and 66 per cent — the full spread of plausible structural scenarios being debated at the time — is not a narrow band. If potential participation is closer to 62 than to 58, the labour market carries substantially more latent supply than the bottom-of-the-range reading suggests, the output gap is larger, inflationary pressures are more contained, and the case for early monetary tightening is weaker.

The fears that the exit was permanent and would permanently reduce potential output were not well-supported by the evidence. The dynamics embedded in the age-cohort data — particularly the procyclicality of prime-age men and women — pointed instead to an attenuation: a decline that would slow and partly reverse as the business cycle continued, rather than a new, permanently lower ceiling on what the U.S. economy could produce at full employment.

Why this paper is on this site

Three reasons.

The first is methodological lineage. The approach here — multivariate time series, demographic decomposition, explicit separation of cyclical and structural forces — is the foundation the later macro work rests on. The habit of asking whether an apparent trend is really what it looks like, or whether something else is driving it, and then building a model that can distinguish between the two, runs through everything on this site. That habit is partly what this earlier work formed.

The second is the question itself. The paper is asking, in 2011, a version of the same question the later housing and fiscal-capture work asks in 2024: who bears the cost when a macroeconomic force moves, and is the standard account of the transmission mechanism actually correct? Here the force is a rate cycle and the mechanism in question is whether the participation decline reflects genuine structural change or a cyclical withdrawal that policy should not mistake for permanent. Getting it wrong, in either direction, has distributional consequences — for the households whose employment prospects depend on whether the central bank reads the labour market correctly.

The third is the most direct one. A question came in through a collaboration, and I sat down with MATLAB, a FRED and BLS data pull, and the VAR literature and worked it through to a finished paper: model selection done by hand against competing information criteria, the demographic decomposition built from scratch from the cohort series, the forecasts extracted, validated against held-out data, and written up. It went to peer review and was accepted. That collaboration — and the professional relationship it deepened with a practitioner then working at one of the larger global macro funds — is, as directly as career happenstance ever works, how I got my first engagement at a global macro hedge fund the following year. That part of the record is not decorative. It is the evidence that the capacity to pick up an unfamiliar question and carry it through to something rigorous was built early, and stands on its own.

The limitation worth naming plainly: the study does not exploit cointegrating relationships, and the female cohort’s role in the aggregate forecast carries the largest forecast uncertainty. Both are acknowledged in the paper. A result that names its own limits is more reliable than one that doesn’t.
