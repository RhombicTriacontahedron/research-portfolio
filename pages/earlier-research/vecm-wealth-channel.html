The Collapse of the US Wealth–Consumption Channel (2016, pre-AI; updated 2025–2026)

Research completed pre-AI, in two passes: a 2016 research note identifying the structural break using data through 2015Q4; a 2025 working paper delivering the definitive estimate with 72 post-crisis quarters, updated to 2026. This explainer covers both. The full working papers are in the research corpus.

For forty years before 2008, when American households got wealthier — through a rising stock market or climbing house prices — they spent more. About fifteen cents of extra spending for every extra dollar of added wealth, year after year, embedded in the same cointegrating relationship for four decades. Central banks understood this. The Federal Reserve operationalised it: quantitative easing was designed, in Bernanke’s own words, to push up asset prices so that households would “feel richer” and spend more.

That mechanism broke in 2008. I detected the break in 2016 and confirmed it decisively in 2025. The most telling part is not the break itself but what came after it. The Federal Reserve’s own staff researchers have since shown that the wealth-to-spending link is now severely impaired — barely working in the aggregate. Yet the Fed’s own meeting minutes, as recently as March 2026, still credit “gains in household wealth” for resilient consumer spending. The evidence and the official explanation both come from inside the same institution, and they no longer agree. The playbook has not been updated.

1. What the pre-2008 world looked like

The empirical foundation goes back to Lettau and Ludvigson (2001), who showed that real US consumption, real disposable income, and real household net worth form a stable cointegrating trio. In the long run, consumption tracks both — roughly 89 cents of spending per dollar of income, 15 cents per dollar of wealth. Wealth’s 15-cent coefficient was not a rough approximation; it was precisely estimated (t-statistic above 5), stable across every sub-period of the 1961–2007 data, and central to forty years of monetary policy thinking.

The mechanism was direct. When house prices rose, homeowners refinanced, pulled out equity, and spent it. When equity portfolios swelled, shareholders felt permanently richer and consumed accordingly. A central bank that pushed up asset prices was reliably pushing up consumer demand. The long-run wealth elasticity was the transmission mechanism in practice, not just in theory.

2. The first detection, 2016

In 2016, with data through 2015Q4, I ran a Chow likelihood-ratio test on the Johansen VECM specification — the same system Lettau and Ludvigson used, re-estimated on the pre-crisis sub-sample to recover the clean pre-GFC relationship. The test asks: can the same parameter vector that fits 1961–2007 also fit 2008–2015? The answer was decisively no. The likelihood-ratio statistic was 107.1 — nearly three times the 0.1% critical value for the degrees of freedom involved. Parameter constancy across 2008Q1 was overwhelmingly rejected.

What the 2016 detection could not do was estimate the new regime reliably. Thirty-two post-break quarters in a three-equation system is too few: the parameters of the post-crisis relationship were identified only enough to reject the old one, not enough to say what replaced it. The 2016 detection reported the break and the recursive evidence of progressive collapse from roughly 2013, but held off on a definitive post-break estimate for the honest reason that the sample was too short.

The whole analysis was done by hand — specification chosen by information criteria, break date confirmed by a Quandt-Andrews endogenous-break profile, recursive trace estimated step by step. No AI, no automated pipelines.

3. The definitive estimate, 2025 (updated 2026)

By 2025, 72 post-crisis quarters had accumulated. The paper extending the 2016 detection revisited the full system with the longer sample.

The core result: the long-run wealth elasticity had fallen from 0.149 to 0.014 — statistically indistinguishable from zero across Johansen, DOLS, and FMOLS estimators. Pre-crisis and full-sample 95% confidence intervals for the wealth coefficient do not overlap: [0.092, 0.206] before 2008; [−0.056, 0.086] in the full sample. The collapse is unambiguous. Income absorbed the entire long-run anchor: the income coefficient rose from 0.89 to 1.07, crossing unity — the textbook permanent-income result, arrived at not by design but by wealth dying and income picking up the load.

The break statistic roughly doubled as the sample grew — from 107 in 2016 to 223 by 2025. That is the opposite of what you see with a temporary disruption. A transitory shock washes out as the sample lengthens; a structural break persists and its evidence accumulates. The pre-COVID robustness check (stripping the 2020–2022 observations to remove pandemic-era variance) still yields a decisive LR of approximately 103, firmly above any conventional critical value. The break is real, it is durable, and it survives every check run against it.

The mechanism failed on two distinct axes. In the 2008–2019 stagnation decade, the long-run link was, if anything, above its pre-crisis level — but the adjustment speed collapsed to essentially zero: households’ eventual spending target still moved with wealth, but they stopped closing the gap between current spending and that target. Across the full post-crisis sample the pattern reversed: adjustment was brisk, but the long-run link itself fell to zero. Either way the effective channel — the product of a working long-run link and a working behavioural adjustment — fell by more than half. The wealth effect did not merely shrink; its machinery came apart at different joints in different periods.

4. The Fed calibration risk

The Federal Reserve did not merely assume the wealth channel; it built policy around it. Bernanke (2010) named it explicitly when launching QE2 — “higher stock prices will boost consumer wealth and help increase confidence, which can also spur spending.” Powell (2013) confirmed the same logic. The wealth effect was the stated transmission mechanism of the Fed’s signature post-crisis instrument.

Over the following decade, the Fed’s own research quietly dismantled that premise. Its 2025 FEDS Note finds that the aggregate marginal propensity to consume out of wealth fell roughly 20% after 2012, concentrated in the top income quintile where households barely spend incremental gains. The note concludes directly that the decline dampens the transmission of any policy interventions that affect household wealth. The Fed’s researchers had reached, in measured institutional language, essentially the same conclusion: the channel QE was designed to exploit had shrunk to a fraction of its pre-crisis magnitude.

The contradiction in one line. The Fed’s own staff research — the 2025 FEDS Note — finds the wealth-to-spending link severely impaired. The Fed’s own policy record — the March 2026 FOMC minutes — still credits rising household wealth for resilient spending. The institution is calibrating policy through a mechanism its own economists have shown barely works, and is doing so in writing, in the same year.

What makes this a live risk rather than a settled debate is that the Fed’s operating narrative has not caught up with its research. The March 2026 FOMC meeting minutes attribute consumer resilience to “gains in household wealth” — the same framing that animated 2010 policy, applied to an economy the institution’s own economists describe as significantly less responsive to wealth shocks. This is not a failure of communication between outside critics and a defensive institution; it is a research–rhetoric gap inside the same institution. The evidence is internal, the calibration lag is institutional, and the cost of that lag is paid by households whose spending prospects depend on the central bank reading the transmission mechanism correctly.

The post-COVID episode provides a natural test. Equity-driven household wealth surged by roughly $13 trillion through 2021, then unwound almost completely by late 2022. Consumer spending barely responded to either move; the Fed’s own researchers attributed spending resilience to the fiscal-transfer savings stock, not a wealth effect. Wealth surged and collapsed; spending held steady. That is precisely what the structural reorganisation implies: when long-run wealth elasticity is statistically zero, wealth gains and losses leave the long-run level of consumption untouched.

Why this paper is on this site

Three reasons, directly connected to the monetary and distributional research this site carries.

The first is the methodological evidence. The VECM identification strategy — Johansen cointegration, sub-sample Chow testing, endogenous break-date profiling, recursive trace estimation, weak-exogeneity likelihood-ratio tests — is the technical toolkit the later macro and monetary work is built from. This paper is where that toolkit was assembled and stress-tested on a clean identification problem with a decisive result.

The second is the substantive connection. The central question of this paper — does monetary policy transmission actually work the way the institution using it believes it does? — is the same question running through the distributional incidence work on this site. If the wealth channel that QE relied on was severely impaired from 2008, then the distributional costs and benefits of decade-long unconventional policy were being assessed against a broken model of who gains from rising asset prices. Getting the transmission mechanism right is not a technical footnote; it determines who bears the cost of getting it wrong.

The third is the most direct. This was built pre-AI — from data pulled from FRED, every specification checked by hand against information criteria, every recursive step estimated manually. The capacity to take an unfamiliar identification problem and carry it through to a rigorous, defensible result without automated assistance is the same capacity I bring to the questions where that standard matters most.
