Credible Commitment and the Capitalisation Channel — companion note to Breaking the Benchmark

Preliminary working note — please do not cite, quote, or circulate without the author’s permission.

Breaking the Benchmark argues that “any credible commitment to cap \(g_R\) produces an immediate re-pricing of the existing stock” (§5). One word — credible — does load-bearing work there without definition. This note supplies it. Credibility is the condition under which the paper’s capitalisation channel actually fires, and it is exactly where an orthodox economist will press hardest. So the note meets that pressure on its own ground.

The objection

The sharpest orthodox objection is not about price controls; it is about commitment. A cap that compresses the asset price must change what landlords expect future rent growth to be — and expectations are forward-looking. A government cannot simply announce a durable policy and be believed. This is the time-inconsistency problem (Kydland and Prescott 1977): a policymaker free to revise the cap will face continual pressure to do so, rational agents anticipate the revision, and the announced commitment is discounted before it can bind. On this logic the cap’s second job — deflating the speculative premium — would not fire: if landlords expect the cap to lapse, they expect rent growth to revert, and the price holds.

The resolution is a rule, not a stronger promise

Model the market’s belief as a two-state distribution over the permanent growth path: the cap holds, with growth at the ceiling \(g_{\text{cap}}\), with probability \((1-\omega)\); or it is abandoned, reverting to the pre-brake path \(g_{\text{pre}}\), with probability \(\omega\). The asset is then worth the expected present value across the two states, \[ E[P] = \omega\,P(g_{\text{pre}}) + (1-\omega)\,P(g_{\text{cap}}), \] where \(P(g) = R/(r-g)\) is the Gordon-growth present-value relation (a growing perpetuity; finite building life affects the level, not the comparative statics on which the argument turns).

Decompose the reversal probability \(\omega = \omega_d + \omega_s\), treating the discretionary and sovereign reversal channels as distinct: a discretionary component — the day-to-day political revisability the design controls — and a sovereignty component — the irreducible fact that no statute binds a future parliament. A discretionary cap (renewed by ministerial decision, as in the Scottish case the paper discusses in §7) carries a high \(\omega_d\), so \(E[P]\) sits near \(P(g_{\text{pre}})\): the premium is not compressed, and the upstream macro-prudential lever of §3 is blunted. Statutory pre-commitment (legislating the brake, the monitoring regime, and the exit triggers together, as §7 prescribes) lowers \(\omega_d\), pulling \(E[P]\) toward \(P(g_{\text{cap}})\): the §5 re-pricing operates.

This is the orthodox answer to the orthodox objection — the rules-over-discretion logic that disciplined central-bank time-inconsistency. The analogy is to the logic, not the degree: a statute lacks the delegation to an insulated institution that gives an independent central bank its full commitment force, and a visible distributional rule is harder to commit to than a technical inflation target — so legislated triggers are a partial commitment device, raising the political cost of reversal and coordinating expectations without the force of full delegation. The objection specifies and bounds the design; it does not defeat it. (The paper’s §5 already conditions the re-pricing on a credible commitment; this note makes that condition precise — it does not introduce it.)

The re-pricing is proportional — exactly so

The compression — the gap between the no-cap price and the expected price — is \[ P(g_{\text{pre}}) - E[P] = (1-\omega)\,\big[P(g_{\text{pre}}) - P(g_{\text{cap}})\big], \] linear in the credibility \((1-\omega)\) the design achieves. (In a richer hazard model — abandonment at a random time — it is monotone rather than exactly linear; the direction and the size-open discipline are unchanged.) This is the paper’s own discipline made sharp: direction settled, size open (§5, §8) — the per-unit compression \(P(g_{\text{pre}}) - P(g_{\text{cap}})\) awaits the unestimated magnitudes, but its scaling in credibility is exact in this model. A large value confirms the financialisation diagnosis; a small one, a correspondingly small adjustment; the channel operates in the same direction either way.

Two refinements an orthodox reader will demand

First, separate the channels. Capping \(g_R\) compresses the speculative component of the price — the capitalised bet on perpetual growth — while leaving the current cash flow \(R\), which funds maintenance and provision, untouched (the ceiling caps growth, not the level; §5). The discount rate \(r\) is a distinct matter, and it is policy-sensitive: a credible, rule-bound cap lowers the policy-uncertainty component of \(r\) — that is what credibility is — while the restriction may raise a liquidity component. These \(r\)-effects are second-order and their net sign is size-open; the first-order effect is the \(g_R\) compression, downward. So the direction of the re-pricing is robust, and reading any fall as a “risk penalty that destroys value” presupposes the pre-cap price was the asset’s fundamental — which the paper denies (an over-capitalised speculative claim), so any movement is toward fundamental value, not away from it.

Second, the Lucas critique does not bite the direction: \(P = R/(r-g_R)\) is a present-value relation given the parameters, not a fitted regression, so the comparative statics (\(\partial P/\partial g_R > 0\)) are structural; what the critique touches is the size of the expectation revision, which is open.

The sovereignty residual

The sovereignty component \(\omega_s\) cannot be legislated to zero — it is the residual repeal probability that keeps the compression short of complete. But it is not a one-way bet against the cap. The same sovereignty that permits repeal permits a future parliament to tighten or extend; the reversal risk is therefore two-directional, not a directional discount — and the present direction of travel, with the Renters’ Rights Act newly in force, cuts against presuming the only reachable revision is loosening. Nor does §7 let strains accumulate to a forced crisis-repeal: it binds the cap to measurable exit triggers and a glidepath, easing it as conditions improve. And the unregulated status quo carries its own policy-reversal risk in the opposite direction, so the residual is not a cost unique to the brake. What remains is the part no institutional design can remove, in either direction.

What this adds

The note formalises one word in Breaking the Benchmark and draws one connection the paper makes only implicitly: that its credit-channel contribution (§3/§5 premium compression) depends on its sunset-design contribution (§7 statutory commitment). It does not restate the second-best argument, the case-study evidence, or the Scottish design lesson, all of which the paper owns. Its only additions are the expectations decomposition, the credibility-proportional continuum, and the two-sided sovereignty residual. It runs entirely within the paper’s Lipsey-Lancaster frame.

Reference. Kydland, F. E., and E. C. Prescott. 1977. “Rules Rather than Discretion: The Inconsistency of Optimal Plans.” Journal of Political Economy 85 (3): 473–492.
