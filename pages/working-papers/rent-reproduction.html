Rent as Reproduction Cost

Carlos Galindo Escajeda June 2026

Preliminary working paper — please do not cite, quote, or circulate without the author’s permission.

Abstract

For NEF, this paper supplies the positive welfare rationale beneath the Fair Rents brake — not just a defence of it, but a reason it moves the market toward, rather than away from, the social optimum. It treats housing cost not as ordinary consumption but as a deduction from the resources a household needs to reproduce itself, so a rising rent share directly compresses the capacity to sustain and form new households. Embedding this in the Reproductive-Capacity Dominance (RCD) welfare criterion, the paper derives a reproduction-consistent rent ceiling: the rent share a social planner would choose, set at the minimum that still lets landlords cover genuine costs at undistorted asset prices. Conditional on a clearly-stated empirical premise — that post-2008 financialisation pushed buy-to-let prices above their fundamental level — market rents are forced above this ceiling, and a CPI-anchored growth cap of the kind NEF proposes moves the market back toward the optimal path. The paper is disciplined about scope: the overvaluation premise is maintained (its empirical test is delegated to the companion fiscal-capture paper), the within-period result is what is established, and the fuller self-reinforcing rent-and- demography cycle is presented as an interpretive conjecture for future work, not a proven dynamic.

1. Introduction

The UK LHA policy of 2016–24 offers two natural experiments for identifying fiscal capture in housing markets. In April 2016, LHA was frozen at cash levels that held until March 2020. In April 2020, the government temporarily restored LHA to the 30th percentile of local market rents — the first uprating since 2016. From April 2021, LHA was re-frozen at these uplifted cash levels while market rents continued to rise. The Autumn Statement 2023 re-linked LHA to the 30th percentile from April 2024, before freezing it again in 2025. These two events — the 2020 uplift and the 2021 re-freeze — create directionally opposite, testable fiscal capture predictions: if LHA operates as a yield floor for landlords, areas with higher LHA intensity should have seen rent growth accelerate at the uplift (the floor rises) and decelerate at the re-freeze (the floor is held below rising market rents). The two tests are not symmetrically powered: the uplift test (\(\beta_{\text{up}} > 0\)) is the higher-powered identifier because rents adjust readily to upward floor changes, while the re-freeze test (\(\beta_{\text{fr}} < 0\)) is supporting evidence subject to attenuation from downward nominal rigidity and tenant lock-in during periods of general inflation.

The empirical design for testing these predictions is developed in full by the LHA DiD project (Galindo 2026b). The econometric specification exploits pre-period LHA-intensity (housing benefit and Universal Credit housing element households as a fraction of PRS stock) interacted with indicators for the uplift and re-freeze events across local authorities, with region-by-quarter fixed effects absorbing common shocks. The key parameters \(\beta_{\text{up}} > 0\) (uplift accelerates rent growth in binding areas) and \(\beta_{\text{fr}} < 0\) (re-freeze decelerates it) are jointly the fiscal capture test. A clean pre-period — 2019-Q1 to 2020-Q1, before either policy event — provides the parallel trends baseline.

But the DiD design, however well-identified, cannot answer the prior theoretical question: why does fiscal capture occur structurally? Why do landlords price to the LHA rate rather than to a competitive market rate — and what are the welfare implications of a policy that interrupts that pricing?

The standard behavioral explanation is incomplete. It says: landlords in the HB sub-market know the maximum benefit their tenants receive and set rents accordingly. This is empirically documented — DWP qualitative surveys confirm it directly — but it is a description of the mechanism, not a theory of why the mechanism is profit-maximizing. In a genuinely competitive market, landlords would undercut each other to fill vacancies, driving rents below LHA. The persistence of LHA-anchored pricing requires either market power or structural complementarity between LHA rates and the reproduction costs of the benefit-dependent tenant population. We argue it is the latter.

The theoretical foundations we develop rest on three interlocking claims.

Claim 1 (Reproduction compression): Housing costs are not a generic consumption expenditure. They are inputs to the reproduction of the working population. When the rent share \(s_t\) rises — whether from rent increases, benefit freezes, or supply restrictions — the household’s capacity to reproduce is compressed structurally, not merely constrained financially. This follows from embedding housing costs into the RCN reproduction function \(N_{t+1} = Be^{\eta_t}N_t^{1-\gamma}C_t^\gamma\), where \(C_t\) must be understood as non-housing consumption expenditure \((1-s_t)Y_t\).

Claim 2 (BTL overvaluation): In the competitive equilibrium, buy-to-let assets are priced by landlords using their own consumption-based stochastic discount factor. This SDF correctly prices the private risk of BTL investment — fluctuations in rental income and asset values — but cannot reflect the social optimum under the RCN welfare criterion. The RCD-consistent asset price is the highest price at which landlords can earn only the minimum participation rent at fundamental yields. Post-2008 financialization pushed market prices above this level; at overvalued prices, debt service requires rents above the RCD-optimal minimum, compressing tenant reproduction. The market-CE gap is the demographic externality discount — a structural consequence of BTL overvaluation and market incompleteness, not of investor irrationality.

Claim 3 (RCD-optimal rent ceiling): The social planner under the RCD criterion sets the rent share at the minimum consistent with landlord participation at fundamental asset prices. Conditional on the maintained overvaluation premise, the minimum participation rent is inflated above this level, and a self-reinforcing compression cycle is implied: high prices require high rents to service debt, which compress reproduction, which reinforces the demographic dynamics that originally justified the high prices. We establish the static (within-period) comparative statics of this mechanism; its full dynamic characterization — the existence and stability of a compressed steady state — is deferred to the forward programme. A CPI-anchored growth ceiling counteracts the escalating rent-to-income share that the overvaluation dynamic implies. This furnishes a structural welfare foundation for the NEF emergency brake, rather than a purely second-best justification.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the standard fiscal capture account and identifies its three theoretical gaps. Section 3 develops the housing-augmented reproduction function and LHA as reproduction subsidy. Section 4 derives the BTL asset-pricing anomaly and the demographic externality discount. Section 5 synthesizes the two theoretical lenses on the LHA mechanism and their divergent policy implications. Section 6 derives the reproduction-consistent rent ceiling. Section 7 concludes.

2. The Standard Account and Its Theoretical Gaps

2.1 Fiscal Capture: What the Evidence Establishes

The empirical literature on housing benefit capitalization is unusually consistent for housing economics. Gibbons and Manning (2006) exploit the 1996–97 UK housing benefit reforms — which reduced the maximum subsidy payable for new claimants — and find that between 60% and two-thirds of the incidence of the subsidy reduction fell on landlords through reduced rents, with rents paid falling by 6–11%. Their preferred model is a matching framework, not a competitive one: landlords and tenants match bilaterally, and the subsidy affects the rent set in matching rather than a market-clearing price.

DWP’s own econometric analysis of the April 2011 and April 2012 LHA reforms, which reduced LHA from the 50th to the 30th local percentile and severed the automatic uprating link respectively, finds “relatively substantial and statistically significant reductions in rental values” in affected areas. Critically, DWP qualitative surveys of private landlords document the mechanism directly: many landlords in the housing benefit sub-market “used the LHA rates to set their rents, either viewing these as good indicators of market rents, or because they were largely operating within the HB sub-market” (DWP 2011).

The institutional picture is clear. LHA operates in binding areas not merely as a subsidy ceiling but as a price signal: it tells landlords the maximum benefit a tenant can receive, which — under realistic assumptions about tenant budgets and search — also approximates the maximum rent they will pay. Landlords learn this and price to it. We treat the 2021–24 re-freeze — in which LHA was held at April 2020 cash levels while market rents continued to rise, progressively eroding the real value of the benefit — as economically analogous to the 1996–97 nominal benefit reduction studied by Gibbons–Manning: both compress real LHA generosity in binding areas. The symmetry of fiscal capture also predicts a positive incidence at the April 2020 uplift. Both predictions are empirical questions for Galindo 2026b.

2.2 Three Gaps in the Behavioral Account

The behavioral description above is accurate so far as it goes. Its theoretical limitations become apparent when we ask three questions it cannot answer.

Gap 1: Why does the sub-market not competitively erode the LHA anchor? If landlords can earn LHA-anchored rents above competitive equilibrium, entry should occur until rents are competed down. The persistence of LHA anchoring implies either that supply is inelastic (the P1 violation documented in the companion paper, Galindo 2026) or that there is a structural complementarity between LHA rates and landlord willingness to accept benefit-dependent tenants. Both are plausible, but neither is made explicit in the standard fiscal capture account.

Gibbons and Manning’s (2006) preferred framework — bilateral matching rather than competitive price-taking — already provides a structural account: the subsidy affects the rent set in the match, not a market-clearing price, so competitive erosion does not apply directly. The RCN reproduction mechanism is complementary, not competing: it explains why LHA is informationally relevant to both parties in the match (LHA signals the tenant’s effective budget capacity, which is tied to their reproduction constraint), and it adds a welfare criterion — RCD population-path dominance — and a demographic outcome prediction that the matching framework does not supply. The two accounts differ most sharply in their empirical footprint: a matching model predicts rent-level effects only, while the reproduction model additionally predicts differential household formation and population growth in high-intensity binding areas.

Gap 2: Why is capitalization heterogeneous across binding and non-binding areas? A purely behavioral story — landlords follow LHA — would predict capitalization everywhere. But the DiD design specifically tests for heterogeneity: \(\beta_1 < 0\) in binding areas, \(\beta_1 \approx 0\) in non-binding areas. The heterogeneity requires a structural story about why LHA is informative only in areas where it was previously constraining rents. The reproduction framework provides this story.

Gap 3: What is the welfare benchmark for evaluating fiscal capture? The standard framing treats fiscal capture as waste: public funds routing into private yields rather than tenant welfare. This framing implicitly takes the (unsubsidized) market price as the welfare benchmark. But if market prices are themselves distorted — by the financial and supply failures documented in Galindo 2026 — then the welfare comparison becomes indeterminate without a positive theory of what rents should be. LHA as reproduction subsidy, by contrast, provides a positive welfare criterion: rents should be consistent with demographic reproduction, and LHA is part of the mechanism by which the state attempts (imperfectly) to enforce that constraint.

3. Housing Costs in the Reproduction Function

3.1 The Reproduction-Cost Foundation

Within the reproduction-cost framework of the parent theory (Galindo 2026c), the value of labour corresponds to the cost of the wage basket — the goods a worker requires to sustain their capacity to work. Housing is not incidental to this basket; it is constitutive of it. As Huato (forthcoming, B0) documents, urban land rent operates as a deduction from wages — a transfer from wages to economic rent — whenever the cost of shelter exceeds the cost of reproducing the worker’s capacity to work. The rent share \(s_t\) is therefore not merely an affordability problem; it is a claim on wages, operating through the price system to redirect resources from the reproduction process to landlords.

Put structurally, the reproduction of the working population — sustaining the physical, social, and cognitive capacities that workers bring to production — is a material process with real resource requirements. Housing is one of the most significant of those requirements in any advanced economy. When housing costs rise, the real value of the wage basket falls, even if nominal wages are held constant. This is the mechanism through which rising rents function as a real wage cut for tenant households.

The RCN model (Galindo 2026c) provides the formal expression of this insight.

3.2 The RCN Reproduction Function

The RCN model specifies population dynamics as a constant-returns-to-scale reproduction function:

\[N_{t+1} = B e^{\eta_t} N_t^{1-\gamma} C_t^{\gamma} \tag{1}\]

where \(N_t\) is the labor force (working-age population), \(C_t\) is aggregate consumption expenditure, \(B > 0\) is a scale parameter, \(\gamma \in (0,1)\) is the consumption share in reproduction, and \(\eta_t \sim \text{i.i.d.}(0, \sigma_\eta^2)\) is a reproduction shock. The function is CRS in \((N_t, C_t)\): proportional scaling of both inputs produces proportional output. This property is necessary for a balanced growth path to exist and for the value function normalization \(V = N^{1-\sigma}\Psi(k, \theta)\) to hold.

The demographic continuation factor \(\partial N_{t+1}/\partial N_t = (1-\gamma)N_{t+1}/N_t\) is the marginal contribution of current population to next-period reproduction, holding per-capita consumption \(C_t/N_t\) constant. It is the load-bearing term in both the stochastic Euler equation and the asset-pricing result (Theorem 4.1 of RCN): the term \((1-\gamma)\) connecting demographic growth to the pricing kernel.

3.2.1 Minimal Maintained Assumptions from RCN

For self-containment, we state exactly which results of the parent model are imported and maintained here, and the condition under which the housing-augmented function (equation 2) inherits them. These are maintained assumptions sourced to Galindo (2026c), not results re-derived in this paper.

  • (A1) Reproduction technology. \(N_{t+1} = Be^{\eta_t}N_t^{1-\gamma}C_t^{\gamma}\) is constant-returns-to-scale in \((N_t, C_t)\), with \(\gamma \in (0,1)\) (RCN §3).
  • (A2) RCD criterion. Population paths are ranked by the Ramsey–Weizsäcker overtaking criterion (§6.1); the competitive equilibrium is RCD-dominated by the planner’s path under incomplete demographic-insurance markets (RCN §5).
  • (A3) Valuation kernel. The normative SDF \(M^{RCN}\) (equation 7) is the dynastic valuation kernel of RCN (its §2 “Stochastic Discount Factor”; closed form in Appendix A.2).
  • (A4) Calibration. \(\gamma = 0.4\) (RCN §4), used illustratively in §3.3 and §4.3.
  • (A5) Curvature domain. \(\sigma \ge 1\) in the RCN flow index \(u(N)=N^{1-\sigma}/(1-\sigma)\) (RCN §2, Theorem 4.1), under which the RCN asset-pricing results (A3) and the demographic risk-premium sign hold. This domain governs the RCN pricing kernel only; the planner’s rent-share problem of §6 ranks population paths by the RCD overtaking criterion (A2), which is linear in \(N_t\) and therefore does not invoke \(u\) or the sign of \(1-\sigma\) — see §6.1 and Appendix
  • (A6) Value-flow separability. Rent income is a deduction from the reproduction fund \((1-s_t)Y_t\), while the balanced-growth capital stock \(k^*\) is maintained from the accumulation fund; equivalently, landlords’ propensity to reinvest rental income into the productive capital stock is taken to be zero within the model’s horizon (the standing modeling choice that makes the rent-share and capital margins separable in §6.2). This is a sectoral closure: it locates productive accumulation in a distinct capital-owning sector (the holders of \(K\) in the parent RCN general equilibrium), leaving the landlord–tenant rent relation as the object of analysis. §6.2 states the consequence of relaxing it.

Inheritance under the housing augmentation. Equation (2) replaces \(C_t\) in (A1) with the tenant reproduction base \((1-s_t)Y_t\). Under the zero-savings restriction of §3.3 — the subpopulation for whom \((1-s_t)Y_t\) exhausts non-housing consumption — this base is the object \(C_t\) of the parent model, so (A1)–(A2) and (A5) carry over without modification on the tenant subpopulation; the rent share \(s_t\) enters only as a multiplicative compression of the consumption argument, preserving the CRS form and the existence of a BGP in \((N_t, (1-s_t)Y_t)\). The asset-pricing apparatus of §4 operates on the aggregate economy under (A3) and the curvature domain (A5); the relation between the two is the subject of §4.5 and the heterogeneous-household extension of §7.3. We emphasise the division of labour between (A5) and (A2): the RCN pricing results require \(\sigma \ge 1\), but the planner’s rent-ceiling derivation of §6 rests only on the linear RCD criterion (A2) and so is insensitive to the curvature parameter — a point that matters for the boundedness of the planner’s objective in Appendix A.

3.3 Housing as Consumption Compression

In the RCN baseline, \(C_t\) is unrestricted household consumption. In the housing-augmented model, a fraction \(s_t \in [0,1]\) of household income \(Y_t\) is paid in rent. We maintain the assumption — standard in the reproduction literature and empirically defensible for working-age renters — that housing is an inelastic prerequisite: households must pay rent before any other consumption. We further restrict attention to tenant households operating near the hand-to-mouth constraint (savings rate approximately zero), so that non-housing consumption equals the residual \(C_t = (1-s_t)Y_t\). This is a plausible characterisation of benefit-dependent PRS tenants in the bottom income quintile and allows \(Y_t\) to be interpreted as consumption expenditure for this subpopulation.1

Substituting into equation (1):

\[N_{t+1} = B e^{\eta_t} N_t^{1-\gamma} \left[(1-s_t) Y_t\right]^{\gamma} \tag{2}\]

The rent share \(s_t\) compresses the consumption base available for reproduction. Taking logarithms and log-linearizing around the balanced growth path \((s^*, c^*, g^*)\), using \(\ln(1-s_t) \approx \ln(1-s^*) - \frac{s^*}{1-s^*}\hat{s}_t\) for small deviations:

\[\hat{n}_{t+1} = \eta_t + (1-\gamma)\hat{n}_t + \gamma \hat{y}_t - \frac{\gamma s^*}{1-s^*}\hat{s}_t \tag{3}\]

where \(\hat{x}_t \equiv \ln(x_t/x^*)\) denotes log-deviations. The term \(-\frac{\gamma s^*}{1-s^*}\hat{s}_t\) is the rent compression elasticity: a one-unit log-deviation in \(s_t\) reduces next-period log population by \(\gamma s^*/(1-s^*)\).

At the empirically relevant parameters — \(\gamma = 0.4\)2 and \(s^* = 0.30\) (approximately the UK median rent-to-income ratio for PRS working-age tenants, consistent with NEF 2026 data) — the rent compression elasticity is \(0.4 \times 0.30/0.70 \approx 0.17\). A ten-percent real rent increase (from \(s^* = 0.30\) to \(s_t = 0.33\), compressing \(c^{net}\) from \(0.70Y\) to \(0.67Y\) — a fall of \(4.3\%\)) reduces next-period log population by approximately \(0.4 \times 4.3\% \approx 1.7\%\) through the reproduction channel alone, consistent with the log-linearization (\(\hat{s}_t \approx 0.095\); \(-(0.17)(0.095) \approx -0.016\)). This elasticity is local to \(s^* = 0.30\); the underlying coefficient \(\gamma s/(1-s)\) is monotone increasing in \(s\), so the compression effect is larger for households at higher rent shares (at \(s = 0.485\) — the NEF 2026 housing-cost burden for the bottom income quintile, a different population from the PRS working-age median — the elasticity rises to \(0.4 \times 0.485/0.515 \approx 0.38\)). The BGP figure of \(0.17\) is therefore a conservative lower bound for bottom-quintile demographic effects. The demographic consequence of sustained rent-to-income increases of the kind observed in the UK PRS since 2010 is not a second-order welfare effect. It is a first-order demographic outcome.

3.4 LHA as Reproduction Subsidy

For households receiving Housing Benefit or Universal Credit housing element, the LHA transfer \(h_t\) (annual, per qualifying household) augments effective non-housing consumption:

\[C_t^{HB} = (1-s_t) Y_t^{HB} + h_t \tag{4}\]

where \(Y_t^{HB}\) is HB-household income. In reproduction terms, LHA partially offsets the compression effect of \(s_t\): it maintains the consumption base available for reproduction among benefit-dependent households at a level higher than the raw income net of rent would support.

When LHA is frozen in cash terms — as in 2020–24 — \(h_t\) stays constant while market rents \(R_t\) continue to rise. In binding areas (where LHA previously covered a significant fraction of prevailing rent), the real value of the transfer falls: \(h_t/R_t\) declines. For benefit-dependent households, this is equivalent to an increase in the effective \(s_t\): nominal rent absorbs a rising share of the augmented income \((1-s_t)Y_t^{HB} + h_t\). Under equation (3), this raises \(\hat{s}_t\), reducing \(\hat{n}_{t+1}\).

The prediction for the LHA DiD is structural, not behavioral. Areas with higher pre-freeze LHA intensity are areas where a larger share of the tenant population has its reproduction capacity supplemented by LHA. The freeze removes that supplement in real terms. Demographic growth slows in high-intensity binding areas relative to non-binding areas. Because demand for rental housing is tied to population dynamics — new households form as the working-age population grows — rent growth in high-intensity binding areas also slows. The coefficient \(\beta_1 < 0\) is structurally implied by the reproduction framework, not just by the behavioral capitalization story.

Critically, the two accounts — behavioral capitalization and structural reproduction compression — predict the same sign of \(\beta_1\) but assign different welfare interpretations to the result. We return to this divergence in Section 5.

4. Buy-to-Let Asset Pricing and the Demographic Externality

4.1 Standard Housing Asset Pricing

The standard framework for housing asset pricing applies the Consumption CAPM. For an investor holding a BTL property, the relevant stochastic discount factor is:

\[M_{t+1}^{BTL} = \beta \left(\frac{C_{t+1}^{own}}{C_t^{own}}\right)^{-\sigma} \tag{5}\]

where \(C^{own}\) is the consumption of the owner household and \(\sigma\) is the coefficient of relative risk aversion. The no-arbitrage condition gives the risk premium on BTL:

\[E_t[R_{h,t+1}] - R_f = -R_f \cdot \text{Cov}_t\!\left(M_{t+1}^{BTL},\, R_{h,t+1}\right) \tag{6}\]

where \(R_{h,t+1} = (R_{t+1} + P_{t+1})/P_t\) is the total BTL return (rental income plus capital gain). Since landlords receive rent rather than paying it, their consumption \(C^{own}\) is not directly affected by the rent share \(s_t\). In good aggregate conditions, consumption grows (M falls), and BTL returns are also high (rents and prices rise). The covariance is negative: \(\text{Cov}(M^{BTL}, R_h) < 0\), generating a modest positive risk premium on BTL.

Under the capitalization formula \(P = R/(r - g_R)\), the post-2008 compression of the risk-free rate \(R_f\) and the observed rise in expected rent growth \(g_R\) inflated BTL asset prices dramatically — the financialization dynamic documented in Section 1 of the companion paper (Galindo 2026). The standard model explains the level of the boom but says nothing about whether BTL prices reflect social value.

4.2 The RCN Dynastic Valuation Kernel and the Demographic Channel

The benchmark price \(P^{RCD}\) requires a valuation kernel. We take it from the parent model’s dynastic valuation problem — the intertemporal optimality (Euler) condition of a representative fully-insured working dynasty maximising the curved reproductive-capacity index \(u(N_t) = N_t^{1-\sigma}/(1-\sigma)\) with \(\sigma \ge 1\) (assumption A5; RCN §2 and Theorem 4.1) under the CRS reproduction function — which yields the RCN stochastic discount factor [Galindo 2026c, §2 and Appendix A.2]. Two clarifications are essential and are developed in §4.5. First, this is the valuation object of the parent model, not the RCD welfare criterion: the RCD criterion that ranks rent-share paths in §6 is linear in \(N_t\) (it does not pass through \(u\)), whereas \(M^{RCN}\) below is the curved kernel that prices the demographic risk of holding the asset. The two are distinct objects the parent model maintains side by side, and only the valuation object carries curvature. Second, this kernel is a normative construct used solely to define \(P^{RCD}\); it is not a positive claim that the hand-to-mouth tenants of §3.3 solve an Euler equation, nor is it the kernel that clears the decentralized market — equilibrium BTL prices are set by landlords through \(M^{BTL}\) (eq. 5):

\[M_{t+1}^{RCN} = \beta \left(\frac{N_{t+1}}{N_t}\right)^{-\sigma} \frac{\Psi_k(k_{t+1}, \theta_{t+1})}{\Psi_k(k_t, \theta_t)} \tag{7}\]

where \(\Psi_k\) is the marginal value of capital in the intensive-form value function \(V = N^{1-\sigma}\Psi(k, \theta)\). The RCN SDF depends on demographic growth \((N_{t+1}/N_t)^{-\sigma}\): it is high when population growth is low, reflecting the high marginal value of each surviving unit of reproductive capacity when the population is contracting.

Under the housing-augmented reproduction function (equation 2), and writing \(c_t \equiv Y_t/N_t\) for per-capita income — so that \((1-s_t)c_t\) is per-capita non-housing consumption, the per-capita counterpart of the aggregate base \(C_t = (1-s_t)Y_t\) in equation (2): \[\frac{N_{t+1}}{N_t} = B e^{\eta_t}(1-s_t)^{\gamma}(c_t)^{\gamma} \tag{8}\]

the demographic growth rate depends on the rent share \(s_t\). This creates a link between housing market conditions and the social SDF:

\[M_{t+1}^{RCN} \propto \beta \left[B e^{\eta_t}(1-s_t)^{\gamma}(c_t)^{\gamma}\right]^{-\sigma} \tag{9}\]

When \(s_t\) rises (rents increase, benefit is frozen), \((N_{t+1}/N_t)^{-\sigma}\) rises — the RCN SDF increases. At the same time, the BTL return \(R_{h,t+1}\) rises when rents are high. Therefore:

\[\text{Cov}\!\left(M_{t+1}^{RCN},\, R_{h,t+1}\right) > 0 \tag{10}\]

along the rent-share (\(s_t\)) channel, conditional on per-capita income \(c_t\) held fixed. A counteracting income channel runs in the opposite direction: when \(c_t\) is high, demographic growth and housing returns both rise, generating negative covariance between \(M^{RCN}\) and \(R_h\) (the standard CCAPM channel). The net covariance sign is calibration-dependent; in the normative argument of Section 4.3 it is not load-bearing.

Along the rent-share channel, a positive covariance implies a negative risk premium: \(E[R_h]^{RCN} - R_f < 0\) along this channel. BTL housing acts as a demographic hedge — it pays off precisely when demographic outcomes are poor (high \(s_t\) → high \(R_h\) → high \(M^{RCN}\)). This hedge property comes at a social cost: high \(R_h\) is itself the cause of poor demographics. Section 4.3 develops the normative implication.

4.3 Normative Overvaluation and the Demographic Externality Discount

The pricing-kernel analysis of Section 4.2 establishes that BTL acts as a demographic hedge under the RCN SDF. The normative question is distinct: what does the RCD welfare criterion say about the socially consistent asset price?

Defining \(P^{RCD}\). From the planner’s analysis in Section 6.2, the RCD-optimal policy minimizes the rent share \(s_t\) subject to landlord participation at fundamental (undistorted) asset prices. The minimum participation rent \(\underline{R}\) is the per-unit physical and financial cost of housing provision — exogenous to the asset price:

\[\underline{R} = (\delta + m + r_f) K_h\]

where \(\delta\) is depreciation, \(m\) is maintenance cost, \(r_f\) is the risk-free opportunity cost of capital, and \(K_h\) is the replacement cost of the housing unit. Define the RCD-consistent price as the Gordon-Growth capitalization of this exogenously determined minimum participation rent:

\[P^{RCD} = \frac{\underline{R}}{r - g_R}\]

This embeds the standard Gordon-Growth fundamental-pricing condition \(r - g_R = \rho_f\), where \(\rho_f \equiv \underline{R}/P^{RCD}\) is the fundamental yield: at \(P^{RCD}\), the net capitalization rate equals the fundamental yield requirement. \(P^{RCD}\) is a normative benchmark — the BTL price consistent with the planner’s optimal rent share \(s^* = \underline{R}/Y\).

Why \(P^{mkt} > P^{RCD}\). In the CE, post-2008 compression of \(r\) and the supply inelasticity documented in the companion paper (Galindo 2026) inflated BTL market prices substantially above \(P^{RCD}\). The market price \(P^{mkt} = R^{mkt}/(r - g_R)\) where \(R^{mkt} = \rho_{mkt} \cdot P^{mkt}\) is the market rent. The overvaluation condition \(P^{mkt} > P^{RCD}\) is therefore equivalent to \(R^{mkt} > \underline{R}\): overvaluation implies market rents exceed the RCD-consistent minimum. Section 6.3 makes the leverage mechanism explicit: under fixed LTV, total debt service scales linearly with \(P^{mkt}\), so any excess of \(P^{mkt}\) over \(P^{RCD}\) directly raises required rents above \(\underline{R}\).

The demographic externality discount is the capitalized value of this excess:

\[\Delta P \equiv P^{mkt} - P^{RCD} = \frac{R^{mkt} - \underline{R}}{r - g_R} > 0 \tag{11}\]

The incompleteness mechanism. The market incompleteness that sustains \(P^{mkt} > P^{RCD}\) operates through tenant exclusion. Tenant households bear the reproduction cost of \(R^{mkt} > \underline{R}\) but do not hold BTL assets. They cannot discipline landlord pricing through portfolio rebalancing, nor can they capture the hedge value of BTL through asset ownership. The social cost of above-minimum rents is therefore never reflected in the BTL market price.3

The normative results above — the \(P^{RCD}\) benchmark, the externality discount \(\Delta P\) of equation (11), and the rent ceiling of Section 6 — are complete as stated and do not require a calibrated value for the externality. A heuristic bridge to future empirical calibration of the externality’s magnitude, which transfers the RCN demographic risk-premium coefficient to the housing asset by analogy, is deferred to §7.3 to keep the present derivation free of analogy-based steps.

We treat \(P^{mkt} > P^{RCD}\) as a maintained empirical premise for the UK PRS in the post-2008 period, motivated by the financialization and supply-inelasticity evidence in Galindo 2026. Proposition 1 is the conditional implication: given this premise, the CE rent share exceeds the RCD-optimal level.

[!NOTE]

Proposition 1 (Normative BTL Overvaluation). Let \(P^{RCD} = \underline{R}/(r - g_R)\) be the BTL price consistent with the RCD-optimal rent share \(s^* = \underline{R}/Y\), and let \(P^{mkt}\) be the CE market price. Under the maintained empirical premise \(P^{mkt} > P^{RCD}\), market rents \(R^{mkt} = \rho_{mkt} P^{mkt}\) exceed the RCD-consistent minimum \(\underline{R}\), and therefore \(s_t^{mkt} = R^{mkt}/Y_t > s^*\). The CE is reproduction-compressing: the market rent share exceeds the RCD-optimal level, with the excess sustained by financialization and market incompleteness.

Proof. Immediate from the definitions. \(P^{mkt} > P^{RCD}\) with \(P^{RCD} = \underline{R}/(r - g_R)\) implies \(R^{mkt} > \underline{R}\) — the fixed-LTV leverage mapping (15a) of §6.3 makes this rent excess explicit — and hence \(s_t^{mkt} = R^{mkt}/Y_t > \underline{R}/Y_t = s^*\). \(\square\)

4.4 BTL Overvaluation as Structural, Not Behavioral

This result is distinct from the behavioral overvaluation stories in the housing literature (irrational exuberance, momentum trading, disaster myopia). The demographic externality discount is a structural consequence of CE market incompleteness — the same mechanism that drives the RCD failure in the capital market equilibrium of the RCN model. It does not require any irrational behavior by any market participant. Each agent acts optimally given their information and their own SDF. In particular, no individual agent prices the aggregate demographic externality of above-minimum rents — landlords correctly price their own consumption risk via \(M^{BTL}\) (eq. 5); tenants, excluded from BTL ownership, cannot trade against it. The externality is unpriced precisely because no SDF in the market reflects it.

The structural claim is empirically distinguishable from a behavioral account by its demographic prediction (Section 5.3): a purely behavioral overvaluation — irrational exuberance, momentum, or disaster myopia — generates no differential demographic compression in high-intensity binding areas under the LHA freeze. The reproduction framework predicts otherwise. The LHA DiD demographic outcome test (Section 7.3) therefore provides a falsifier for the structural account.

The policy implication is equally structural. A behavioral overvaluation can in principle be corrected by better information, improved price discovery, or investor education. A structural overvaluation driven by market incompleteness requires an institutional correction that forces the market to internalize the externality — a tax, a cap, or a direct regulation of the asset class. Section 6 derives the precise form of that institutional correction.

4.5 Class-Consistent Pricing: Who Prices What

Because the model is explicitly class-divided, it is essential to state which agents price which objects, so that no representative-agent device is smuggled into a class framework. Three distinct roles operate, and they do not collapse into one another:

  1. Landlords price BTL assets in the competitive equilibrium. The market price \(P^{mkt}\) and the market rent \(R^{mkt} = \rho_{mkt}P^{mkt}\) are set by the landlord/investor group using its own consumption-based discount factor \(M^{BTL}\) (eq. 5). Landlords receive rent and hold the asset; their consumption \(C^{own}\) is the only consumption stream entering the equilibrium pricing kernel.

  2. The planner evaluates demographic paths with the RCN kernel. The RCN stochastic discount factor \(M^{RCN}\) (eq. 7) is a normative object: it is used only on the planner’s side to define the RCD-consistent benchmark \(P^{RCD}\) and the demographic externality discount \(\Delta P\) (eq. 11). It is not an equilibrium pricing kernel and is never used to clear the BTL market.

  3. Tenants price nothing. The hand-to-mouth tenants of §3.3 hold no BTL assets, do no intertemporal consumption smoothing on the housing margin, and therefore solve no asset-pricing Euler equation. Their reproduction is compressed by \(R^{mkt}\); they are the excluded party whose interest no market kernel represents — the source of the incompleteness identified in §4.4.

The wedge \(P^{mkt} - P^{RCD}\) is therefore the gap between the price landlords actually set (via \(M^{BTL}\)) and the price a planner valuing the reproduction of the working population would set (via \(M^{RCN}\)). The two kernels are deliberately kept apart; their formal integration within a single heterogeneous-household equilibrium — in which landlord and tenant groups coexist with distinct budget sets — is the aggregation extension deferred to Section 7.3. The present paper’s results do not require that integration: every claim is either a competitive-equilibrium statement priced by \(M^{BTL}\) or a normative statement evaluated by \(M^{RCN}\), and the two never appear on the same side of an equation.

One objection should be met head-on, because it rests on a premise the RCN framework explicitly rejects. A reader trained on the welfare-economics duality between prices and quantities — that the pricing kernel must be the marginal-utility gradient of the objective that ranks allocations — will expect the rent ceiling \(s^*\) and the benchmark price \(P^{RCD}\) to descend from a single objective, and will read the linear-ranking / curved-valuation split as an inconsistency (“two planners”). But that duality is a property of a utilitarian social objective, and RCD is not utilitarian: it is, in the parent model’s own words, “structurally linear (risk-neutral) in expected \(N\),” and “can rank an allocation as dominant that a Paretian welfare function would reject, and vice versa” [Galindo 2026c, §3, Remark on RCD structure]. Severing the price–quantity duality is therefore not an oversight but a defining commitment of the framework: the welfare ranking of population paths (linear, used for \(s^*\)) and the valuation of demographic risk borne by an asset-holder (curved, used for \(P^{RCD}\)) are answers to two different questions and need not share a gradient. Collapsing them into one curved planner — as the duality would require — would reintroduce exactly the variance-penalising Paretian objective that RCD was constructed to displace, and would make this paper inconsistent with its parent rather than more rigorous.

A second separation, distinct from the kernel separation above, must also be kept in view, because it is easily mistaken for an inconsistency. The RCN framework deploys two normative objects, not one, and they have different curvature by construction. The valuation object is the curved dynastic Bellman index \(u(N)=N^{1-\sigma}/(1-\sigma)\) with \(\sigma \ge 1\), whose risk aversion is precisely what generates the demographic risk premium and hence the pricing kernel \(M^{RCN}\) used to define \(P^{RCD}\) (RCN Theorem 4.1). The ranking object is the Reproductive-Capacity Dominance criterion, which RCN states is “structurally linear (risk-neutral) in expected \(N\)” — a discounted Ramsey–Weizsäcker overtaking order over population paths, deliberately not the curved utilitarian sum (Galindo 2026c, §3, Remark on RCD structure). This is not a free choice of convenience but a defining commitment of the parent model: RCD evaluates physical reproductive capacity directly, without penalising variance in \(N\) as such. Consequently the asset price \(P^{RCD}\) (a valuation, curved kernel, \(\sigma \ge 1\)) and the rent ceiling \(s^*\) (a ranking of feasible paths, linear RCD criterion) are derived from the same parent framework using the two objects it already maintains side by side — not from two mutually inconsistent planners. The rent-ceiling derivation of §6 and Appendix A uses only the linear ranking object; the curvature parameter \(\sigma\) enters this paper solely through the pricing apparatus of the present section.

5. Two Lenses on the LHA Mechanism

The reproduction framework and the standard fiscal capture account agree on the empirical prediction: \(\beta_1 < 0\) in binding local authorities. They diverge sharply on the welfare interpretation and the policy implications.

5.1 Lens 1: Fiscal Capture (Standard Account)

Under the fiscal capture account, LHA in binding areas acts as a price floor: landlords set rents at or near the maximum LHA rate, knowing tenants’ budgets are constrained at that level. When LHA is frozen and market rents rise, the floor progressively unbinds from below — LHA can no longer support rents at the market level — and rent growth in binding areas slows relative to non-binding areas.

Welfare interpretation: LHA is distortionary. It transfers public funds from general revenues to private landlord yields rather than to tenant welfare. The DiD finding of \(\beta_1 < 0\) is evidence of that distortion: it shows that LHA was sustaining rents above what the market alone would have delivered. The policy implication is LHA reform: move away from cash subsidies into bricks-and-mortar investment, or redesign LHA to fund social housing acquisition directly, bypassing the landlord capitalization channel.

5.2 Lens 2: Demographic Compression (RCN Account)

Under the reproduction framework, LHA in binding areas acts as a reproduction subsidy: it maintains the non-housing consumption base of benefit-dependent households at a level consistent with demographic reproduction. When LHA is frozen, the effective subsidy falls in real terms, compressing the reproduction capacity of high-intensity areas. Demand for housing — driven by household formation, which is driven by demographic growth — slows in these areas. Rent growth slows not because the yield floor recedes but because the demand base contracts.

Welfare interpretation: LHA is a necessary but insufficient response to housing market failure. The DiD finding of \(\beta_1 < 0\) is evidence that LHA was contributing to demographic reproduction, and its freeze has had adverse demographic consequences in high-intensity areas. The policy implication is not LHA reform alone but rent cap plus LHA augmentation: the rent cap reduces \(s_t\) toward the reproduction-consistent level, while reformed LHA (decoupled from market rents) directly supplements household consumption capacity.

5.3 The Divergence and Its Significance

The two accounts are observationally equivalent at the level of the rent-growth coefficients but distinguishable empirically through their differential predictions on demographic outcomes. The two-event DiD (Galindo 2026b) yields two rent-growth tests: \(\beta_{\text{up}} > 0\) at the April 2020 uplift and \(\beta_{\text{fr}} < 0\) at the April 2021 re-freeze. Both frameworks predict the same rent-growth signs; only the reproduction framework predicts accompanying demographic responses:

The two rent-growth tests carry unequal statistical power. The uplift test (\(\beta_{\text{up}} > 0\)) is the primary identifier: rents in binding areas adjust upward readily when the LHA floor rises. The re-freeze test (\(\beta_{\text{fr}} < 0\)) is supporting evidence; downward nominal rigidity and tenant lock-in attenuate rent-growth deceleration when the floor is held below rising market rents, so \(\beta_{\text{fr}}\) may be smaller in magnitude even when fiscal capture is real. A significant negative \(\beta_{\text{fr}}\) is therefore strong evidence; a near-zero estimate is not diagnostic on its own.

The reproduction framework predicts that the LHA re-freeze should be associated with measurable slowing of household formation and population growth in high-intensity binding areas. This is testable with ONS mid-year population estimates and MHCLG household formation data at the local authority level. If these demographic predictions are confirmed, the fiscal capture story — which says nothing about demographic outcomes — is insufficient, and the full reproduction framework is required.

The welfare divergence is the more fundamental difference. If LHA is merely distortionary (fiscal capture), the correct response is to eliminate the distortion: stop subsidizing private landlords, redirect funds to supply. If LHA is a reproduction subsidy that the market has colonized (reproduction account), the correct response is to protect the reproduction function while eliminating the colonization: cap rents to remove the landlord capitalization channel, and redesign LHA as direct income support uncorrelated with market rent levels.

6. The Reproduction-Consistent Rent Ceiling

6.1 The Planner’s Problem Under RCD

The Reproductive-Capacity Dominance criterion [Galindo 2026c, Section 5] ranks population paths using the Ramsey–Weizsäcker overtaking criterion: path \(\{N_t^A\}\) dominates path \(\{N_t^B\}\) if there exists \(T^*\) such that for all \(T > T^*\):

\[\sum_{t=0}^{T} \beta^t N_t^A > \sum_{t=0}^{T} \beta^t N_t^B\]

The RCN paper establishes that the CE fails RCD: incomplete markets for demographic insurance mean the CE population path is dominated by the social planner’s path.

In the housing context, the planner maximizes the RCD criterion over \(\{s_t\}\), the sequence of rent shares, subject to:

  1. Reproduction: \(N_{t+1} = Be^{\eta_t} N_t^{1-\gamma}[(1-s_t)Y_t]^\gamma\)

  2. Landlord participation: \(s_t Y_t \geq \underline{R}_t\) where \(\underline{R}_t\) is the minimum rent consistent with covering maintenance costs and the opportunity cost of capital at fundamental (undistorted) asset prices \(P_t^{RCD}\).

  3. BGP condition: capital per effective worker \(k_t = k^*\).

6.2 The Optimality Condition

The sign-determining partial derivative with respect to \(s_t\), holding \(Y_t\) fixed and treating the reproduction function as the constraint, is:

\[\frac{\partial N_{t+1}}{\partial s_t} = -\frac{\gamma N_{t+1}}{1-s_t} < 0 \tag{13}\]

Because this partial is strictly negative throughout the interior of the feasible set, the planner’s RCD-maximizing solution lies at the lower boundary of the participation constraint — the corner \(s_t = \underline{R}_t/Y_t\). The result does not require solving the full dynamic program, and — critically — the corner is not undone by the balanced-growth capital constraint \(k_t = k^*\). The reason is structural, not merely technical. In the reproduction ontology of this paper, rent is a deduction from the reproduction fund — the consumption base \((1-s_t)Y_t\) out of which the working population is reproduced — whereas the investment that maintains \(k^*\) is financed from the accumulation fund. This value-flow separability is the standing modeling choice (A6): landlords’ propensity to reinvest rental income into the productive capital stock is taken to be zero within the model’s horizon, so the rent-share and capital-accumulation margins draw on distinct value flows. Lowering \(s_t\) then raises the tenant reproduction base without drawing down the accumulation fund, so it relaxes no capital constraint and tightens none. Two consequences follow. First, because the RCD criterion ranks population paths by the linear overtaking objective \(\sum_t \beta^t N_t\) (A2) — not by the curved utility sum that governs the RCN pricing kernel — the objective is strictly increasing in \(N_t\) at every date with no dependence on the curvature parameter \(\sigma\). Second, \(N_{t+1}\) is strictly decreasing in \(s_t\) (equation 13) at every date, and no channel exists by which a higher \(s_t\) relaxes the participation, reproduction, or BGP-capital constraint. Lowering \(s_t\) therefore raises every term of the objective simultaneously: no intertemporal trade-off arises and the static sign is globally decisive. A compact Lagrangian treatment — verifying that the participation multiplier is strictly positive (the constraint binds) and that the capital and reproduction multipliers do not enter the \(s_t\) first-order condition — is given in Appendix A and delivers the same corner.

The separability (A6) is load-bearing, so its failure mode should be stated. If landlords reinvest a fraction \(\phi > 0\) of rental income into the productive capital stock, then \(k^*\) ceases to be independent of \(s_t\): a higher rent share would relax the capital constraint (iii), the multiplier \(\xi_t\) would enter the \(s_t\) first-order condition with a positive sign, and that term would partially offset the strictly negative reproduction term of equation (13). The corner \(s^* = \underline{R}_t/Y_t\) would then soften into an interior solution trading reproduction compression against accumulation, and the sign of the net effect would become a quantitative question about \(\phi\). Qualitatively, the corner survives as long as the reproduction loss from a marginal rise in \(s_t\) — the strictly negative term \(\lambda_t \gamma N_{t+1}/(1-s_t)\) — dominates the accumulation gain channelled through \(\phi\) and the capital multiplier \(\xi_t\); the corner softens to an interior optimum only once the latter overtakes the former. We do not calibrate that threshold here, because signing it requires the endogenous \(\xi_t\) from the heterogeneous-class equilibrium that is deferred to §7.3; stating the comparison, rather than a manufactured number, is the honest scope. The corner result is thus exact in the \(\phi = 0\) sectoral closure and an upper bound on the optimal rent share for \(\phi > 0\).

The RCD-optimal rent ceiling is therefore the minimum rent share consistent with landlord participation at fundamental asset prices:

\[s^* = \frac{\underline{R}_t}{Y_t} = \frac{\rho_f \cdot P_t^{RCD}}{Y_t} \tag{14}\]

The second equality is an algebraic identity, and it discharges any worry that the curved pricing kernel of §4.2 contaminates the linear rent ceiling. By the definition of the fundamental yield, \(\rho_f \equiv \underline{R}_t/P_t^{RCD}\), so \(\rho_f \cdot P_t^{RCD} = \underline{R}_t\) whatever value the kernel assigns to \(P_t^{RCD}\). The curvature parameter \(\sigma\) enters only through the discount rate \(r\) and hence through \(P_t^{RCD}\) and \(\rho_f\) separately; their product collapses to the physical participation rent \(\underline{R}_t = (\delta + m + r_f)K_h\), which depends on no pricing object. The rent ceiling \(s^* = \underline{R}_t/Y_t\) is therefore invariant to \(\sigma\): it is fixed entirely by the physical reproduction parameters and tenant income. This is the operational content of the valuation/ranking separation of §4.5 — the financial valuation \(P_t^{RCD}\) may carry the demographic risk premium, but the policy instrument \(s^*\) does not inherit it.

where \(\rho_f\) is the fundamental yield requirement (covering maintenance, depreciation, and the risk-free rate at fundamental valuation).4

6.3 The Overvaluation–Compression Cycle

Under BTL overvaluation, \(P_t^{mkt} > P_t^{RCD}\). Highly leveraged BTL investors require debt-service payments on the overvalued asset price, which implies:

\[s_t^{mkt} = \frac{\rho_{mkt} \cdot P_t^{mkt}}{Y_t} > \frac{\rho_f \cdot P_t^{RCD}}{Y_t} = s^* \tag{15}\]

The mechanism linking BTL overvaluation to higher required rents operates through UK BTL leverage structure. With fixed loan-to-value ratio \(L\) and gross mortgage rate \(i_t\), the minimum rent required to service debt on a BTL property worth \(P^{mkt}\) satisfies:

\[R^{min}_t = \left[\rho_e + (i_t - \rho_e)L\right] P^{mkt}_t \tag{15a}\]

where \(\rho_e\) is the equity yield on the unlevered asset. This expression scales linearly with \(P^{mkt}\): a 10% increase in the market price raises the minimum required rent by exactly 10% at fixed LTV. Under overvaluation (\(P^{mkt} > P^{RCD}\)), \(R^{min}_t > \rho_f P^{RCD} = \underline{R}\), directly establishing the market rent excess of equation (11). The yield-compression objection — that overvaluation compresses \(\rho_{mkt} = R/P^{mkt}\), potentially lowering required rents — conflates the ex-post yield on the overvalued asset with the ex-ante minimum rent required to service the purchase price. At UK BTL LTV ratios of 60–75%, the leveraged minimum rent rises faster than the yield compresses.

The market rent share therefore exceeds the RCD-optimal ceiling: this within-period comparison is the formally established result. Reproduction is compressed below the planner’s path. The full dynamic characterization of the cycle — existence, uniqueness, and stability of a fixed point \(s^{cycle} > s^*\) — requires off-BGP analysis beyond the log-linearization and is deferred to the forward programme (Section 7.3); the account that follows is therefore an interpretive conjecture about the dynamics, not an established theorem.

Conjecturally, this would constitute a self-reinforcing cycle: overvalued assets require high rents to service, high rents compress reproduction, compressed reproduction reduces household formation, reduced household formation slows organic demand growth, but demographic compression also validates BTL as a demographic hedge (its worst periods coincide with poor demographic outcomes), which sustains investor demand for BTL as a hedge, potentially preventing the price from correcting to fundamental even as the physical demand for housing slows. On this reading the overvaluation would be self-perpetuating precisely because it generates the demographic conditions that justify the demographic hedge premium. Establishing whether this fixed point exists and is stable is the central task of the deferred dynamic analysis.

6.4 The CPI-Anchored Growth Ceiling as RCD Approximation

The NEF Fair Rents proposal (NEF 2026) caps annual rent increases at the lower of CPI inflation or 2%. Under the reproduction framework, this approximates the RCD-optimal path in the following sense.

On the BGP, the RCD-optimal rent share \(s^* = \underline{R}/Y\) is constant if rents and income grow at the same rate. CPI inflation is approximately equal to nominal income growth at the BGP (if real wages are roughly stable). A ceiling of CPI therefore maintains the rent-to-income ratio at its current level — neither compressing nor expanding it.

The 2% cap does more: it forces the rent-to-income ratio downward whenever CPI exceeds 2%, which has been the UK experience since 2021. During periods of above-2%-inflation real wage growth, the cap allows rents to rise in real terms and thus does not force \(s_t\) below \(s^*\). During periods of rent inflation above 2% (the current UK situation), the cap prevents \(s_t\) from rising further above \(s^*\).

More precisely, the CPI/2% ceiling approximates the RCD path under the following sufficient condition: current market rents are already above the RCD-optimal level \(s^*\), so any cap that holds or reduces the rent-to-income share moves the economy toward the planner’s optimum. This is satisfied whenever:

\[s_t^{mkt} > s^* \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \frac{P_t^{mkt}}{P_t^{RCD}} > 1 \tag{16}\]

which is exactly the BTL overvaluation condition of Proposition 1. The NEF ceiling is therefore not merely an emergency instrument justified by second-best welfare analysis (though it is that, as established in Galindo 2026). It is the first-order RCD-optimal policy response to a structurally overvalued BTL market.

[!NOTE]

Proposition 2 (CPI Ceiling as RCD Approximation — within-period, conditional). Maintain the value-flow separability (A6) and transition-path feasibility (holding or reducing \(s_t\) leaves the participation floor \(\underline{R}_t\) and fundamental yields unchanged within the period). If the CE BTL market exhibits the demographic externality discount of Proposition 1 (i.e., \(P^{mkt} > P^{RCD}\) and therefore \(s_t^{mkt} > s^*\)), then any rent growth ceiling that prevents \(s_t\) from rising further moves the economy toward the RCD-optimal demographic path. A CPI-anchored growth ceiling satisfies this condition whenever actual rent growth would otherwise exceed CPI — the case throughout the UK PRS since 2021.

Proof. By Proposition 1, the overvaluation premise (condition (16)) gives \(s_t^{mkt} > s^*\). A ceiling that holds or reduces the rent-to-income share moves \(s_t\) weakly toward \(s^*\); since \(N_{t+1}\) is strictly decreasing in \(s_t\) (equation 13), every such step weakly raises each term of the RCD objective. The CPI/2% anchor enforces a non-increasing \(s_t\) precisely when counterfactual rent growth exceeds CPI. \(\square\)

The proposition is a within-period comparative-static result: it establishes the direction of movement under the ceiling, holding the participation floor \(\underline{R}_t\) and fundamental yields fixed. It assumes transition-path feasibility — that holding or reducing the rent-to-income share does not itself trigger capital destruction, abrupt landlord exit, or supply shocks that would shift \(\underline{R}_t\) and so violate the ceteris-paribus conditions of equation (13). The full off-BGP transition dynamics, including any such feedback, belong to the deferred dynamic analysis of §6.3 and §7.3.

6.5 The No-Vacancy-Decontrol Requirement

Proposition 2 applies only if the ceiling is binding at the intensive margin and there is no decontrol mechanism at tenancy turnover. Diamond, McQuade, and Qian (2019) show that the San Francisco 1994 rent control, which permitted full rent reset at vacancy, was evaded through the very mechanism the ceiling was designed to block: eviction and re-letting reset the rent-to-income share back to market levels.

Under the reproduction framework, vacancy decontrol has a specific additional cost. Each eviction displaces a household, interrupting the tenure continuity that supports household formation and stable reproduction. Frequent disruption increases \(\sigma_\eta\) (the reproduction shock variance) even if the expected rent level is controlled. This is a consistency argument: the RCN model does not formally parameterize tenure disruption as a \(\sigma_\eta\) shifter, and a full derivation of the variance effect on RCD dominance would require the heterogeneous-household extension of RCN (Section 7.3). The structural intuition — that higher shock variance raises uncertainty around the planner’s path and attenuates the RCD-dominance result in expectation — is consistent with the model’s logic, and provides a second, independent design motivation for no-vacancy-decontrol.

The NEF no-vacancy-decontrol design is therefore not merely an anti-evasion device. It is, under the reproduction framework, a variance-reduction instrument that keeps \(\sigma_\eta\) low by maintaining household tenure stability. This is a second, independent reason why no-vacancy-decontrol is the correct design from RCD first principles — supporting the design argument already made on second-best grounds in Galindo 2026.

7. Conclusion

7.1 What This Paper Establishes

This paper provides three results that complement the second-best welfare analysis of the NEF emergency brake in the companion paper (Galindo 2026).

First, fiscal capture is structurally necessary, not merely behaviorally contingent. Housing benefit capitalization into private rents arises because housing costs enter the reproduction function for the working population, and LHA compensates for the reproduction-compressing effect of above-subsistence rents. Landlords price to LHA in binding areas not only because they can but because LHA is the signal of effective household budget capacity in markets where tenant incomes are insufficient to sustain the reproduction-consistent rent. The DiD prediction of \(\beta_1 < 0\) follows from this structural account without invoking any behavioral assumption about rent-setting. As §5.3 stresses, the rent-growth signs alone do not discriminate the structural account from behavioral capitalization — both predict the same \(\beta_1 < 0\); the discriminating evidence is the demographic response in binding areas, which only the reproduction account predicts and which is deferred to the companion empirical paper.

Second, conditional on the maintained empirical premise that post-2008 financialization pushed BTL market prices above their fundamental level, buy-to-let assets are overvalued relative to the RCD social optimum. The RCD-consistent price is the highest price at which landlords earn only the minimum participation rent at fundamental yields. Given that premise, market rents are forced above the RCD-optimal minimum, compressing tenant reproduction. Tenant households, excluded from BTL ownership, cannot internalize the demographic externality through market participation. The gap — the demographic externality discount — is a structural consequence of BTL overvaluation and market incompleteness, not of investor irrationality.

Third, the reproduction-consistent rent ceiling is the RCD-optimal policy instrument. The planner minimizes the rent share subject to landlord participation at fundamental (unovervalued) asset prices, under the value-flow separability (A6). BTL overvaluation forces market rents above this minimum. The within-period compression is established here; its full self-reinforcing dynamic form is advanced as a conjecture pending the off-BGP analysis (Section 6.3). Conditional on the overvaluation premise of Proposition 1 and the within-period feasibility conditions of Proposition 2, a CPI-anchored growth ceiling moves the rent share toward the RCD-optimal path whenever current market rents exceed the fundamental level — which, under that premise, they do.

7.2 Relation to Prior Work

The results formalize, within the parent theory’s reproduction-cost framework (Galindo 2026c; Huato forthcoming, B0), the insight that housing is a cost of reproducing the working population: rent as a deduction from wages finds its formal expression in the housing-augmented reproduction function (equation 2). Huato’s (forthcoming, B0) analysis of urban land rent as a capitalised claim — a claim on future income discounted to a present price — corresponds formally to the BTL capitalization formula \(P = R/(r - g_R)\), and his identification of the deduction mechanism corresponds to the demographic externality discount \(\Delta P\) of equation (11).

The RCN model is not built on that tradition’s formal apparatus (it is a stochastic dynamic optimization model with rational agents), but its welfare criterion — RCD — captures the concern with the reproduction of the working population as a social end, not merely an input to production. The RCD result that CE markets fail to achieve the planner’s demographic path is the formal counterpart of the argument that unconstrained accumulation can undermine the conditions of its own reproduction.

7.3 The Forward Empirical Programme

This paper is theoretical. Its empirical counterpart requires four components, summarised here as the forward programme; no estimation is undertaken in this paper.

The first component — the rent-side fiscal-capture test (\(\beta_{\text{up}} > 0\) at the 2020 uplift, \(\beta_{\text{fr}} < 0\) at the 2021 re-freeze) — is the subject of the companion empirical paper (Galindo 2026b), to which all estimation, specifications, and inference are referred. The present paper supplies only the structural prediction that the companion’s difference-in-differences design is built to test; its results are reported there, not here.

The remaining components are open. The distinguishing test — whether high-intensity areas also show differential slowing of household formation and population growth — can be pursued with ONS mid-year estimates and MHCLG data at the same LA geography.

The demographic externality discount \(\delta_{demog}\) requires estimation of \(\text{Cov}(\Delta n, R_h)\) at the local authority level: the partial correlation between demographic growth rates and housing returns. The ONS Price Index of Private Rents (PIPR), which succeeded VOA PRMS following its discontinuation in March 2024, provides the housing return series; ONS mid-year population estimates provide the demographic series. Constructing this panel would allow a first empirical test of whether binding housing markets exhibit the negative demographic–return covariance that the overvaluation result requires.

The RCN structural parameter \((1-\gamma)\Omega\) — the coefficient linking demographic growth covariance to the asset risk premium in Theorem 4.1 — is a GMM-identifiable object using cohort fertility data and matched asset-pricing factor data, as outlined in Galindo 2026c Section 7. Estimating this parameter would transform the demographic externality discount from a theoretical prediction to a calibrated magnitude.

A natural calibration heuristic (optional, and not part of any result in the body of this paper) would express the demographic externality cost per unit of BTL return as

\[\delta_{demog} \approx (1-\gamma)\Omega \cdot \frac{\text{Cov}(\Delta n, R_h)}{\sigma_{R_h}^2} \tag{12}\]

where \((1-\gamma)\Omega\) is the demographic risk-premium coefficient from RCN Theorem 4.1 (Galindo 2026c) and \(\text{Cov}(\Delta n, R_h)/\sigma_{R_h}^2\) is the regression loading of demographic growth on housing returns. RCN Theorem 4.1 was derived for the capital asset \(K\) in the RCN general equilibrium, not for a housing asset. Equation (12) transfers the coefficient by analogy; the transfer presumes that landlords price housing risk with the same demographic-risk loading that prices aggregate capital — i.e. sufficient cross-sectoral capital mobility that no housing-specific risk wedge survives. Under the incomplete-markets premise of this paper that assumption is not innocuous, which is why the transfer is confined to this forward-programme note rather than the derivation; a formal derivation of the housing-asset coefficient is itself part of the programme. At illustrative parameters (\(\gamma = 0.4\), \(\Omega = 0.6\), \(\text{Cov}(\Delta n, R_h) < 0\) in binding markets), \(\delta_{demog} < 0\), consistent with BTL’s demographic hedge property, but its magnitude is an empirical question.

A fourth, theoretical extension is also required: a heterogeneous-household RCN model in which the tenant subpopulation’s reproduction shock variance \(\sigma_\eta\) responds to tenure stability and in which the RCD-dominance comparison can be computed across heterogeneous demographic paths. This extension would also formalize the off-BGP dynamics of the §6.3 overvaluation cycle — in particular, the existence and stability of a fixed-point rent share \(s^{cycle} > s^*\) — and would resolve the aggregation gap between the zero-savings tenant model (§3) and the full RCN asset-pricing setup (§4).

Together, these four contributions would allow the reproduction-consistent rent ceiling \(s^*\) to be estimated from data, rather than inferred from the RCD optimality condition alone. That would give policymakers — and advocates for the NEF Fair Rents programme — a quantitative counterpart to the structural argument: not just that market rents exceed the RCD-optimal level, but by how much, and what rent ceiling would restore the optimal path.

7.4 Closing Remarks

The standard case against rent control rests on the presumption that market prices reflect competitive conditions and that interfering with them creates deadweight loss. Both the companion paper (Galindo 2026) and this paper show why that presumption fails in the UK PRS.

The companion paper shows it fails on institutional grounds: the UK PRS is a quadruple-object market (shelter, asset, collateral, fiscal conduit) that violates the foundational conditions of competitive welfare analysis at three simultaneous margins. This paper shows it fails on structural welfare grounds: even if the market were institutionally competitive, the CE would fail to achieve the RCD-optimal demographic path because BTL pricing omits the demographic externality, and the missing externality generates a structural reproduction compression cycle that no competitive equilibrium can internalize.

The rent ceiling derived here is not a departure from economic reasoning. It is the economic reasoning applied consistently — inclusive of the reproduction function that standard models ignore — and it arrives at a clear policy conclusion: a no-vacancy-decontrol, CPI-anchored growth ceiling is the first-order RCD-optimal instrument for the overvalued, reproduction-compressing BTL market that UK housing policy has constructed. Britain’s renters do not merely deserve this protection on humanitarian grounds, though they do. They can be shown to deserve it on the strongest available economic foundations.

Appendix A: Proof of the Corner Solution (§6.2)

We verify the corner solution under the value-flow separability assumption stated in (A6) and the linear RCD ranking criterion (A2); the curved pricing index of §4.2 plays no role here (see §4.5 on why the valuation and ranking objects differ in curvature by construction of the parent model).

The planner ranks rent-share sequences \(\{s_t\}_{t\ge0}\) by the RCD overtaking criterion of RCN (Galindo 2026c, §3 and Appendix A.3), which is linear in the population path: sequence \(A\) dominates \(B\) if \(\liminf_{T\to\infty}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\beta^t\big(N_t^A - N_t^B\big) \ge 0\), with strict inequality somewhere. This is the criterion (A2); crucially, it is defined directly over \(N_t\) and does not pass through the curved flow index \(u(N)=N^{1-\sigma}/(1-\sigma)\) that governs the RCN pricing kernel. The choice variables are subject, at every date \(t\), to:

    1. the reproduction technology \(N_{t+1} = Be^{\eta_t}N_t^{1-\gamma}[(1-s_t)Y_t]^{\gamma}\);
    1. the landlord participation constraint \(s_t Y_t \ge \underline{R}_t\);
    1. the balanced-growth capital condition \(k_t = k^*\).

Monotonicity in the path. Because the objective weights are \(\beta^t > 0\) and every \(N_t\) enters with a positive coefficient, the criterion is strictly increasing in each \(N_t\). By (i), raising \(N_{t+1}\) requires raising the reproduction base \((1-s_t)Y_t\) for some \(t\), i.e. lowering \(s_t\); and by the recursion in (i) a higher \(N_{t+1}\) weakly raises every \(N_{t+\tau}\), \(\tau \ge 1\). No curvature or sign restriction on \(1-\sigma\) is used: the ranking is linear in \(N\).

First-order condition in \(s_t\). Attach multipliers \(\lambda_t\) to (i), \(\mu_t \ge 0\) to (ii), and \(\xi_t\) to (iii). Only constraints (i) and (ii) contain \(s_t\); constraint (iii) and the marginal value of capital do not — by value-flow separability (A6), rent redistributes within the reproduction fund while \(k^*\) is maintained from the separate accumulation fund (§6.2). Hence

\[\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial s_t} = -\lambda_t\,\gamma\,\frac{N_{t+1}}{1-s_t} + \mu_t Y_t .\]

Sign of \(\lambda_t\). The multiplier \(\lambda_t\) is the shadow value of \(N_{t+1}\) in the overtaking sum. Since the criterion is strictly increasing in every \(N_t\) and \(N_{t+1}\) propagates into all later dates through (i), \(\lambda_t > 0\) at any candidate optimum. The first term is therefore strictly negative for every interior \(s_t \in (\underline{R}_t/Y_t,\,1)\).

Complementary slackness. With \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial s_t < 0\) throughout the interior, stationarity can hold only with \(\mu_t > 0\): the participation constraint binds, pinning

\[s_t = \frac{\underline{R}_t}{Y_t} = s^* \qquad \text{for every } t.\]

The capital multiplier \(\xi_t\) does not enter the \(s_t\) first-order condition, confirming that constraint (iii) is slack in \(s_t\): the corner is determined entirely by the reproduction objective and the participation floor, with no intertemporal trade-off — the formal counterpart of the value-flow separability (A6) argued in §6.2.

Remark (no growth-bound is required, by construction of the criterion). A reader accustomed to a curved discounted objective \(\sum_t \beta^t u(N_t)\) would here demand a boundedness condition such as \(\beta(1+g^*)^{1-\sigma} < 1\) evaluated at the growth-maximizing corner. The RCD criterion is constructed precisely to avoid that demand. As RCN states (Appendix A.3), “no spectral-radius restriction on \(\beta g_N\) is imposed … the criterion is adopted precisely to handle the case where \(N_t\) grows along the equilibrium path”: the overtaking order ranks paths by the \(\liminf\) of finite partial sums and is well defined even when \(\sum_t \beta^t N_t\) diverges. In contrast to a conventional discounted-utility objective \(\sum_t \beta^t u(N_t)\), which would require a boundedness condition evaluated at the maximised growth rate to keep the infinite sum finite, the RCD criterion needs no such restriction. The corner \(s_t = s^*\) delivers the pointwise maximal \(N_t\) at every date (each finite partial sum is maximized term-by-term), so it overtakes every feasible alternative regardless of convergence; no parameter restriction on the maximized growth rate \(g^*_{\max}\) is needed for admissibility. (The separate pricing-side boundedness condition — the RCN contraction bound on the value function used to derive the SDF, which does involve \(1-\sigma\) — applies to §4’s asset-pricing apparatus, not to this rent-share program.) The corner \(s_t = \underline{R}_t/Y_t\) thus satisfies the first-order conditions, complementary slackness (\(\mu_t > 0\)), and the overtaking dominance comparison at every date, and is the unique RCD optimum. \(\square\)
