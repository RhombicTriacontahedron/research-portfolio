Fiscal Capture in the UK PRS

Carlos Galindo Escajeda June 4, 2026

Abstract

This is the empirical pillar of the case NEF makes: evidence that the state’s reliance on Local Housing Allowance (LHA) partly subsidises landlords rather than protecting tenants. Using a difference-in-differences design across 290 English local authorities (2019–2024) — built from ONS private-rent indices, DWP Stat-Xplore caseloads, MHCLG dwelling-stock estimates and NOMIS wage and claimant data — it exploits two natural experiments: the April 2020 LHA uplift to the 30th percentile and the April 2021 re-freeze. If LHA acts as a binding yield floor, rent growth in high-LHA-intensity areas should accelerate at the uplift and decelerate at the re-freeze. Across 24 specifications the directional signature is consistent with capture in every case, with zero sign reversals. The paper is candid about magnitude: downward nominal rigidity and tenant lock-in attenuate the re-freeze response, so the headline estimate is reported deliberately as a conservative lower bound, with larger effects in the windows that isolate the mechanism more cleanly. For NEF’s advocacy the implication is concrete: because a real share of any LHA increase is absorbed into rents, raising or unfreezing LHA without a rent ceiling partly funds landlord yields — so a ceiling is a necessary complement to benefit reform, not an alternative to it. The distinguishing demographic test (whether captured areas also show slower household formation) is identified as the next empirical step, not claimed here.

JEL Classification: C23, R21, R38, H53

Keywords: fiscal capture; Local Housing Allowance; housing benefit; private rented sector; difference-in-differences; Labor-Power as Numéraire; yield floor; UK housing policy

1. Introduction

The UK Local Housing Allowance transfers public funds to low-income households to help cover private rents. Whether that transfer reaches tenants or is instead captured by landlords through rent inflation — the benefit-incidence question — is the central empirical question of this paper, and its answer has direct implications for how LHA expenditure should be evaluated as fiscal policy. Prior work establishes that landlord capture is substantial. Gibbons and Manning (2006) find that 60–67% of UK housing benefit accrues to landlords rather than tenants; Brewer et al. (2014) confirm rent reductions in binding local authorities following the 2011–12 LHA reforms. The international incidence literature establishes the same pattern: Susin (2002) finds US rent vouchers raise rents for all low-income households; Fack (2006) estimates that 78 cents of every euro of French housing benefit is absorbed in higher rents; Collinson and Ganong (2018) show that uniform voucher generosity transmits to landlords rather than tenants. Yet the standard account — landlords know their tenants’ maximum benefit and price accordingly — does not explain why this behaviour persists in the face of competition. The persistence of LHA-anchored pricing requires a structural mechanism linking LHA rates to the reproduction costs of the benefit-dependent tenant class.

The UK LHA policy of 2016–24 generates two exogenous policy events that allow direct identification of fiscal capture. In April 2016, LHA was frozen at 2015–16 cash levels, progressively unbinding the floor as market rents rose. In April 2020, the government temporarily restored LHA to the 30th percentile of local market rents. In April 2021, LHA was re-frozen at these uplifted levels while market rents continued to rise. These events create directionally opposite, testable predictions: if LHA operates as a binding yield floor, areas with higher LHA intensity should see rent growth accelerate at the uplift (the floor rises) and decelerate at the re-freeze (the floor is held below rising market rents). The two tests are not symmetrically powered. The uplift test (\(\hat{\beta}_{\text{up}} > 0\)) is the higher-powered identifier because rents adjust readily to upward floor changes. The re-freeze test (\(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}} < 0\)) is subject to two attenuation mechanisms — downward nominal rigidity and tenant lock-in — so a near-zero estimate is consistent with genuine fiscal capture; a significantly negative estimate constitutes strong evidence.

We quantify the LHA incidence across 290 England local authorities, exploiting both events in a difference-in-differences design. The treatment variable — LHA intensity, measured as housing benefit and Universal Credit private rented sector caseload as a share of private rental stock in 2018–19 — is pre-period and predetermined with respect to both policy events. The design uses region-by-quarter fixed effects to absorb common shocks, entity fixed effects to absorb time-invariant LA characteristics, and the NOMIS claimant count and ASHE 10th-percentile wage as time-varying controls. The 10th-percentile wage is structurally required under the LPN framework as the outside option of the benefit-dependent tenant class; we use the 10th percentile rather than the median wage for this reason.

The primary inferential claim is robust directional consistency: across 24 fiscal capture specifications varying the estimation window, spatial controls, bedroom composition, and outlier treatment, every point estimate for \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}}\) is negative with zero sign reversals. No single methodological choice — window, density control, bedroom specification, or outlier treatment — reverses the sign. This pattern, spanning four methodologically distinct classes of specification choice, constitutes structural triangulation rather than frequentist multiplicity. The COVID-window restriction (2020-Q2–2024-Q1), which identifies the predicted fiscal-capture reversal by using the uplift period as the comparison baseline, yields \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}} = -0.0498\) (\(p = 0.0033\)); after additionally stripping post-COVID urban demand recovery via a density control, the mechanistic isolator yields \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}} = -0.0675\) (\(p = 0.0001\)). The baseline primary specification yields \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}} = -0.0174\) (\(p = 0.142\)), attenuated by construction (§2.2); it provides a lower bound. The R4 placebo (\(p = 0.562\), 2015–2019) supports parallel trends. A conservative sample restriction excluding high net in-migration local authorities yields \(\hat{\beta} = -0.0195\) (\(p = 0.119\), 261 LAs), confirming the mechanism is not purely compositional.

Two features of the design are worth noting upfront. First, the primary specification is underpowered by construction: downward nominal rigidity and tenant lock-in attenuate \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}}\) precisely when fiscal capture is real, so the primary spec provides a lower bound on the effect. The COVID-window restriction and density-controlled estimates are better-powered. Second, the 24 specifications share the same 290-LA cross-section and are therefore not statistically independent. We interpret the zero-sign-reversal pattern as specification robustness — evidence that the negative sign is not an artefact of any particular window, control set, or functional form — rather than as 24 independent frequentist tests.

Beyond the fiscal capture evidence, the paper contributes a structural interpretation grounded in the Labor-Power as Numéraire (LPN) framework of Huato and Galindo (2026c). Under LPN, LHA functions as a reproduction subsidy maintaining the non-housing consumption base of benefit-dependent households along the labor-power balanced growth path. Its freeze is therefore not merely a policy choice; it is a structural compression of the demographic reproduction capacity of high-intensity local authorities. This interpretation generates an additional, distinguishable prediction: binding LAs should show not only decelerated rent growth but also slower household formation and population growth relative to non-binding areas. Testing those demographic predictions is outside the scope of this paper and is the subject of ongoing work (Galindo 2026a).

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 describes the institutional background. Section 3 describes the data. Section 4 presents the empirical design. Section 5 reports the main results. Section 6 presents robustness specifications. Section 7 develops the LPN structural interpretation. Section 8 discusses limitations. Section 9 concludes.

2. Institutional Background

2.1 The LHA Policy Timeline

Date Event Direction
April 2016 LHA frozen at 2015–16 cash levels Freeze
April 2020 LHA raised to 30th percentile of local market rents Uplift
April 2021 LHA re-frozen at April 2020 cash levels Re-freeze
April 2024 LHA re-linked to 30th percentile Re-uplift
April 2025 LHA frozen again Re-freeze

The 2020 uplift and 2021 re-freeze create directionally opposite, testable fiscal capture predictions. If LHA operates as a binding yield floor, areas with higher LHA intensity should see rent growth accelerate at the uplift (floor rises) and decelerate at the re-freeze (floor held below rising market rents).

The mechanism through which LHA anchors rents operates at the level of the 152 Broad Rental Market Areas (BRMAs) into which England is divided. Within each BRMA, LHA is set at (or, during freeze periods, below) the 30th percentile of local market rents for each bedroom category, with the intention of giving benefit-dependent households access to at least the lower third of local rental supply. When LHA reaches the 30th percentile it anchors the lower bound of the active letting market: landlords with properties at or near the BRMA-level LHA rate face a soft yield floor when renting to housing benefit or Universal Credit claimants, because tenants’ housing cost allowances cap at that level. Properties priced above the LHA rate are effectively inaccessible to benefit-dependent households; properties at or below it constitute the relevant sub-market for this tenant class. The LHA rate therefore functions as a local price signal — not a legal ceiling, but a hard constraint on the budgets of a substantial share of private renters in binding BRMAs.

Figure 1 makes the receding floor concrete. Because the allowance is reset to the 30th percentile only at each uplift and then held in cash terms, it falls progressively below that percentile as market rents rise between uplifts. Through the 2021–24 re-freeze the 30th percentile of two-bedroom market rents grew at close to five per cent a year1 — peaking near seven per cent in 2024 — while the allowance grew not at all, opening a gap of approximately £106 per month. By 2024 the allowance had, on its own statutory terms, ceased to do what the rule describes: legislated to reach the 30th percentile of local market rents, it had slipped to roughly the 11th percentile of the national two-bedroom distribution shown here — reaching the cheapest ninth of the active letting market rather than the cheapest third the rule is designed to span.2 This is a descriptive comparison of the policy rule with observed market rents and carries no causal content; it identifies the policy-induced wedge whose incidence the remainder of the paper estimates.

The same compression bears unevenly across household sizes. Because the allowance is reset and then frozen in cash terms, the shortfall accumulated over a freeze scales with the rent level: Figure 2 shows that by 2024 the frozen allowance had fallen £88 per month below the 30th percentile for a one-bedroom home but £217 for a four-bedroom home — 2.5 times larger for family-sized accommodation, though the proportional erosion (about 15 per cent) was similar across sizes.

Benefit-dependent households are concentrated at the lower end of the private rental stock. In our sample, LHA intensity — housing benefit and Universal Credit private rented sector caseload as a share of the private rental stock — averages 4.4% across 294 local authorities, but is distributed unevenly: the top decile reaches intensities above 8%, concentrated in coastal towns, post-industrial northern districts, and selected inner-London boroughs outside the highest-rent BRMAs. In high-intensity local authorities, the LHA anchor price represents a larger fraction of the active letting market and landlords face stronger incentives to price at or near the maximum allowance. The expected fiscal capture signal — differential rent growth between high-intensity and low-intensity local authorities, correlated with the LHA policy cycle — is therefore most visible at the upper end of the lha_intensity distribution, and it is precisely this contrast that the difference-in-differences design exploits.

Prior evidence on LHA incidence establishes the underlying phenomenon but is limited in one specific respect. Gibbons and Manning (2006), examining the 1990s restructuring of UK housing benefit, and Brewer et al. (2014), examining the 2011–12 LHA reforms that introduced per-room rates, extended the Shared Accommodation Rate to the under-35s, and capped allowances at the 30th percentile for the first time, both identify substantial landlord capture. Both studies test only the downward rent response to a cut or freeze; neither has access to a subsequent uplift event. This paper addresses that gap by using the April 2020 uplift as a quasi-experimental baseline for the April 2021 re-freeze, permitting identification of the directional reversal — accelerated rent growth at the uplift followed by decelerated rent growth at the re-freeze in high-intensity local authorities — that is the definitive test of the yield-floor mechanism.

2.2 Why the Re-freeze Coefficient is Attenuated

The re-freeze test (\(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}} < 0\)) is subject to two attenuation mechanisms absent from the uplift test. First, downward nominal rigidity: landlords resist cutting rents below existing levels even when the LHA yield floor recedes. Second, tenant lock-in: sitting tenants in high-LHA-intensity areas face moving costs that suppress re-negotiation during periods of general rent inflation. A near-zero \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}}\) is therefore consistent with genuine fiscal capture; a significantly negative estimate constitutes strong evidence.

These attenuation mechanisms have a direct implication for the specification hierarchy in this paper. The primary specification (R1), which uses the full 2019-Q1 to 2024-Q1 window with the entire pre-2021-Q3 period as the comparison baseline, is most severely affected: the comparison window includes the 2019–2020 pre-uplift period, during which rent dynamics in high-intensity areas were already shaped by the ongoing 2016 freeze, compressing the measured contrast. The COVID-window restriction (R3) mitigates this by opening the window at 2020-Q2 — the start of the uplift — so that the comparison baseline captures rent growth during the uplift period itself. This identifies the reversal from accelerated rent growth at the uplift to decelerated growth at the re-freeze, partially bypassing both attenuation mechanisms: landlords who adjusted rents upward at the uplift do not face the same downward nominal rigidity when the floor is subsequently held fixed while market rents continue rising. R3 is therefore the better-powered specification for the fiscal capture reversal test; R1 provides a conservative lower bound. The full specification hierarchy is discussed in §5 and §6.

3. Data

3.1 Rent Outcome Variable

ONS Price Index of Private Rents (PIPR): LA-level median private rents (2-bedroom), 294 England local authorities, January 2015 – March 2024 (Q1 2015 – Q1 2024). Source: ONS. Succeeded VOA PRMS following its discontinuation in March 2024.

3.2 LHA Intensity (Treatment Exposure)

lha_intensity = (HB households + UC-PRS households) / PRS stock, by LA, averaged 2018–19.

  • HB caseload: DWP Stat-Xplore, 296 England LAs, Jan 2015–Mar 2024, CHD-harmonised.
  • UC-PRS caseload: DWP Stat-Xplore, 294 LAs, Aug 2015–Mar 2024.
  • PRS stock denominator: MHCLG Live Table 100, 294 LAs, 2018–19 average. Dwelling stock denominator (not household count); addresses size confound.

3.3 Controls

  • NOMIS Claimant Count: Quarterly unemployment rate by LA, 290 LAs.
  • ASHE 10th Percentile Wages (p10_weekly_wage): Annual ASHE item=6, expanded to quarterly via regional-year median. Benefit-dependent private renters are concentrated in the p10–p25 range of local wage distributions; the 10th-percentile wage therefore targets the labour-market conditions of the relevant tenant class more precisely than the median. The LPN framework formalises this targeting decision: the 10th percentile is the structural outside option of the benefit-dependent tenant class — a theoretical primitive, not a post-hoc empirical choice. Robustness without this control (R21) shows the fiscal capture coefficient changes by 0.5% when p10 is excluded (§6.3). Limitations of the annual frequency are noted in §8.
  • BRMA boundaries: 152 England BRMAs; used for BRMA-level clustering sensitivity (R22).

3.4 Migration Restriction Sample (R7)

ONS MYE Components of Population Change (MYEB3): LAD-level net internal migration, 2018 and 2019. Top-decile excluded: 30 LAs with net in-migration \(\geq\) +1,703 persons/yr. Conservative sample restriction: 261 LAs remain.

4. Empirical Design

4.1 Specification

The primary specification is a within-LA difference-in-differences estimator:

\[\Delta\log(\text{rent})_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \beta_1 (\text{lha\_intensity}_i \times \text{freeze}_t) + \mathbf{X}_{it}'\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \varepsilon_{it}\]

where \(\text{freeze}_t = \mathbf{1}[t \geq \text{2021-Q3}]\), \(\alpha_i\) are LA fixed effects, \(\lambda_t\) are region-by-quarter fixed effects absorbing common shocks, and \(\mathbf{X}_{it}\) includes the quarterly unemployment rate and log 10th-percentile weekly wage. Standard errors clustered at the LA level.

The treatment variable lha_intensity is pre-period (2018–19 average), eliminating reverse-causality concerns. The freeze indicator is interacted with continuous lha_intensity rather than a binary binding indicator to exploit full intensity variation.

4.2 Two-Event Specification

The two-event specification simultaneously estimates the uplift and re-freeze effects:

\[\Delta\log(\text{rent})_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \beta_{\text{up}} (\text{lha\_intensity}_i \times \text{uplift}_t) + \beta_{\text{fr}} (\text{lha\_intensity}_i \times \text{freeze}_t) + \mathbf{X}_{it}'\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \varepsilon_{it}\]

where \(\text{uplift}_t = \mathbf{1}[2020\text{-Q2} \leq t \leq 2021\text{-Q2}]\) covers the 2020 restoration period and \(\text{freeze}_t = \mathbf{1}[t \geq 2021\text{-Q3}]\) is the re-freeze indicator from §4.1. Fiscal capture predicts \(\beta_{\text{up}} > 0\) (rent acceleration when the floor rises) and \(\beta_{\text{fr}} < 0\) (rent deceleration when the floor is held fixed below rising market rents). The single-event specification in §4.1 omits the uplift dummy, folding the 2020-Q2–2021-Q2 uplift quarters into the comparison baseline.

4.3 Identification Assumptions

Parallel trends: In the absence of the LHA policy events, rent growth in high-intensity and low-intensity LAs would have followed parallel trends. The R4 placebo test (\(\hat{\beta}_1\), 2015–2019, fake treatment 2018-Q1: \(p=0.562\)) supports this assumption.

Pre-treatment intensity exogeneity: lha_intensity is measured in 2018–19, before the April 2020 uplift, and is predetermined with respect to the treatment. It is also substantively interpretable (Figure 3): intensity loads strongly on local labour-market weakness — the claimant unemployment rate (\(r = 0.75\)) — while being uncorrelated with the level of local rents (\(r = 0.02\)). Exposure therefore concentrates in the areas where benefit-dependence, and hence the reproduction constraint of §7, is most acute; and because intensity is orthogonal to rent levels, the rent-growth effect cannot be an artefact of high- or low-rent areas reverting to a common trend. The design exploits variation in the benefit-dependent share of the local market, not in its price.

No anticipation: LHA re-freeze announced at Autumn Statement 2020; the 2021-Q3 indicator incorporates a one-quarter implementation lag.

5. Results

5.1 Primary Specification

Table 2 reports the re-freeze coefficient under the two analytical choices that most affect power: the estimation window (the full panel, or the reversal window that takes the 2020 uplift as its baseline) and whether a density \(\times\) freeze control is included. The primary specification (R1) is the conservative, fully-saturated lower bound; each remaining cell removes one pre-identified attenuator and R20 removes both (§2.2, §7.4). The placebo (R4: a fake 2018-Q1 treatment estimated on 2015–2019) yields \(\hat{\beta} = +0.0085\) (SE 0.0146, \(p = 0.562\)), as the design requires. The full 24-specification directional sweep is in Appendix A (Table 4).

5.2 Two-Event Specification

\(\hat{\beta}_{\text{up}} = +0.0205\) (SE=0.0151, \(p=0.174\)); \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}} = -0.0095\) (SE=0.0138, \(p=0.492\)). Joint Wald \(\chi^2(2) = 4.35\) (\(p=0.114\), \(H_0: \beta_{\text{fr}}=0\ \text{and}\ \beta_{\text{up}}=0\)). Both signs correct; jointly suggestive.

The uplift coefficient is directionally consistent (\(\beta_{\text{up}} > 0\)) but imprecise in the 4-quarter window. The re-freeze coefficient is attenuated (see §2.2) but survives in the COVID-window restriction (R3) and density-controlled specifications (R15, R20).

5.3 The Mechanistic Isolator (R20)

R20 combines the COVID-window restriction (R3: uses the 2020-Q2 uplift period as baseline, providing the cleanest identification of the re-freeze reversal) with the density control (R15: strips urban demand recovery attenuation). The result, \(\hat{\beta} = -0.0675\) (\(p=0.0001\)), represents the density-adjusted, uplift-baseline estimate. It is a post-hoc, exploratory specification — not part of the pre-specified analysis and not the primary specification. It demonstrates the R3 result is density-robust by a wide margin.

5.4 Event-Study Dynamics

Figure 4 reports the dynamic analogue of the difference-in-differences estimate: the coefficient on lha_intensity interacted with each quarter, under the fixed-effect structure of the primary specification and normalised to the quarter before the re-freeze.3 The static \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}}\) is the post-2021-Q3 average of this sequence.

The path is consistent with the yield-floor mechanism: once the allowance is frozen while market rents continue to rise, rent growth in high-intensity authorities decelerates relative to elsewhere. The coefficients are statistically indistinguishable from zero across the 2020 uplift and turn negative only after the re-freeze, reaching \(-0.075\) in 2022-Q1; the deceleration concentrates in the year following the freeze and then attenuates through 2023–24 as growth rates re-converge and the April 2024 re-linking is anticipated and implemented. We read the late-window recovery as the dynamic counterpart of the policy reversal rather than as evidence of a transient effect, while noting that the post-freeze window is too short to separate the two.

The pre-uplift coefficients are not flat: high-intensity authorities show slower rent growth through 2019. We read this as the imprint of the preceding freeze — LHA was held at 2015–16 cash terms from April 2016 — rather than a failure of the design, since the mechanism under test should itself have depressed rent growth in high-intensity areas during that episode. The reading bears directly on the specification hierarchy: because the 2019 baseline is itself a freeze period, the primary specification, which averages against the full pre-2021 window, compresses the measured contrast, whereas the COVID-window specification (R3), which takes the 2020 uplift as its comparison, removes the contaminated baseline and recovers a larger coefficient — the pattern the event study makes visible. We therefore do not rest the parallel-trends claim on visual flatness, which the LHA policy history renders implausible over any window spanning a prior freeze; the formal support is the R4 placebo (§4.3). The coefficients are imprecise throughout — only the 2022 quarters are individually distinguishable from zero — so the figure corroborates the sign and timing of the static estimate rather than supplying an independent test of significance.

6. Robustness

6.1 Specification Robustness — Directional Consistency

Across the 24 fiscal capture specifications — varying the estimation window (Temporal), density controls and geographic clustering (Spatial), bedroom composition (Compositional), and outlier treatment and measurement (Measurement) — \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}}\) is negative with zero sign reversals. The Appendix A table reports 28 rows; four are excluded from this directional count. R4 is the identification check (positive coefficient expected and confirmed) and R12a is a binary binding threshold (near-zero by construction — an absorption diagnostic, not a fiscal capture test). The remaining two — R8 and R9 — coincide with R1 by construction rather than as independent estimates, because their coverage criteria (drop thin-market and high-HMO local authorities) are satisfied by zero local authorities and so reproduce the primary coefficient exactly. The 24 counted specifications are distinct estimates. R2, which replaces the saturated Region×Quarter fixed effects with region-specific linear trends plus quarter fixed effects, is among the specifications that recover a nominally significant negative coefficient (\(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}} = -0.0231\), \(p = 0.043\)) once the heavy time-absorption of the primary is relaxed; R1 retains the more conservative absorption and remains the primary.

Because the design tests a directional prediction — a sign reversal, not a two-sided difference — the appropriate per-specification test is one-sided (\(H_0: \hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}} \geq 0\)). Figure 5, reported in Appendix A, plots the one-sided 95% upper confidence bound accordingly. Every point estimate lies below zero, no single analytical choice — temporal window, density control, clustering, bedroom composition, or outlier treatment — moves it across the line, and 7 of the 24 specifications are individually significant at the one-sided 5% level, concentrated as expected in the better-powered isolators (R3, R15, R19, R20, R22) together with R2 and the winsorised R10. The two-sided intervals reported in the Appendix table are more conservative and are not the relevant test for a signed hypothesis.

This directional consistency demonstrates the result is not an artifact of any single methodological choice. The 24 fiscal capture specifications are methodologically orthogonal (spanning four distinct classes of specification choice) but not statistically independent (all share the 290-LA cross-section and therefore share unobserved spatial error structure). We interpret the sweep as specification robustness, not as 24 independent pieces of frequentist evidence. The robustness that carries weight is not the breadth of the sweep but its structure: the estimate strengthens monotonically as two pre-identified confounds are removed — the isolator bracket of Table 2 (§5.1), read structurally in §7.4.

Statistical disclosure (FWER): The pre-specified family of 16 tests (R1–R15 plus R7) is reported for completeness. At \(\alpha/16 = 0.003125\), R3 (\(p = 0.0033\)) narrowly fails strict Bonferroni correction. We do not claim frequentist significance for any individual specification and rest the primary inference on the 24-specification directional sweep.

6.2 Conservative Sample Restriction (R7)

Excluding the top decile of net in-migration LAs (30 LAs; threshold \(\geq\) +1,703 persons/yr) yields \(\hat{\beta} = -0.0195\) (SE=0.0125, \(p=0.119\)) on 261 LAs — more negative than R1. Fiscal capture is not purely compositional; the effect operates in non-displacement areas. Under the LPN framework, displacement is the dominant mechanism in high-migration areas; R7 survival confirms the yield-floor mechanism operates independently.

6.3 Wage Control Sensitivity (R21)

Excluding p10 wages: \(\hat{\beta} = -0.0182\) vs R1 \(\hat{\beta} = -0.0174\) (\(\Delta\beta = 0.0008\), 0.5%). The fiscal capture coefficient absorbs virtually nothing from the wage control. Under the LPN framework, the 10th-percentile wage is a theoretical primitive — the outside option of the benefit-dependent tenant class — and its inclusion is axiomatic, not empirically contingent. A 0.5% coefficient shift is inconsistent with meaningful endogeneity: if p10 were absorbing the LHA policy shock as a mediator or collider, the divergence between R1 and R21 would need to be substantial relative to the main coefficient. The empirical insulation confirms that the theoretical endogeneity concern is inert at the scale of the 290-LA cross-section.

6.4 Bedroom Stability (R17)

All four bedroom categories (1–4 bed) yield \(\hat{\beta} \approx -0.016\) to \(-0.019\), sign-consistent and nearly identical in magnitude. The 2-bedroom primary specification is not cherry-picked.

7. Structural Interpretation

7.1 Two Lenses on the Same Coefficient

The standard fiscal capture account and the LPN reproduction account agree on the empirical prediction — \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}} < 0\) in binding local authorities — but diverge on the mechanism, the welfare interpretation, and the policy prescription.

Lens 1 — Standard fiscal capture. Under the standard account, LHA in binding areas acts as a price floor: landlords in the housing benefit sub-market set rents at or near the maximum allowance, knowing tenants’ budgets are constrained at that level. When LHA is frozen and market rents rise, the floor progressively unbinds from below — LHA can no longer support rents at the market level — and rent growth in binding areas slows relative to non-binding areas. Welfare interpretation: LHA is distortionary; it transfers public funds from general revenues to private landlord yields rather than to tenant welfare. The DiD finding of \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}} < 0\) is evidence of that distortion. Policy implication: redirect LHA funding away from cash subsidies and toward bricks-and-mortar supply investment that bypasses the capitalization channel.

Lens 2 — LPN reproduction compression. Under the LPN framework of Huato and Galindo (2026c), housing costs are not a generic consumption expenditure; they are inputs to the reproduction of labor power. The reproduction function \(N_{t+1} = Be^{\eta_t}N_t^{1-\gamma}C_t^{\gamma}\) requires that \(C_t\) be interpreted as non-housing consumption \((1 - s_t)Y_t\), where \(s_t\) is the rent share. LHA in binding areas acts as a reproduction subsidy: it maintains the non-housing consumption base \(C_t\) of benefit-dependent households at a level consistent with demographic reproduction along the balanced growth path. When LHA is frozen, the effective subsidy falls in real terms as market rents rise above the frozen level, compressing household reproduction capacity. Rent growth slows in binding areas not only because the yield floor recedes but because the demand base — household formation, driven by demographic reproduction — contracts. Welfare interpretation: LHA is necessary but insufficient. Its freeze has adverse demographic consequences in high-intensity areas. Policy implication: a reproduction-consistent rent ceiling plus reformed LHA: the ceiling reduces the rent share \(s_t\) toward the LPD-optimal minimum; reformed LHA (decoupled from market rents) supplements household consumption capacity directly.

Table 3 summarises the divergence. The two accounts make the same rent-growth prediction — which is why the coefficient alone cannot adjudicate between them — but part company on welfare interpretation, on the test that would distinguish them, and on what policy follows.

7.2 Why the Standard Account is Insufficient

The two lenses are observationally equivalent at the level of the rent-growth coefficient. The DiD finding of \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}} < 0\) is consistent with both. They diverge sharply in their predictions on outcomes outside rent growth.

The standard fiscal capture story predicts only rent-level effects. It has no prediction for population growth, household formation, or landlord exit rates conditional on rent levels. The LPN account predicts all three: high-LHA-intensity local authorities should show slower household formation and population growth relative to non-binding areas after the 2021 re-freeze, independently of the rent growth deceleration. Testing these demographic predictions requires the ONS mid-year population estimates and MHCLG household formation data at the local authority level, matched to the same 290-LA panel. That test is outside the scope of this paper (see Galindo 2026a for the theoretical derivation).

7.3 The R7 Evidence Under Both Lenses

The conservative sample restriction (R7) is informative about the mechanism even without the demographic test. Excluding the top decile of net in-migration local authorities — areas where LPN displacement is the dominant mechanism — the coefficient strengthens: \(\hat{\beta} = -0.0195\) versus \(\hat{\beta} = -0.0174\) in the full sample. Under the standard account, this is unremarkable: displacement-prone and non-displacement areas should show equally strong fiscal capture if the mechanism is purely a yield floor. Under the LPN account, R7 survival means the yield-floor mechanism operates independently of displacement in low-migration areas — fiscal capture is systemic, not merely a compositional artefact of tenant turnover. R7 failure would have implied that the mechanism operates primarily through the displacement channel. We observe survival: the yield-floor mechanism is structurally embedded in the non-displacement subsample.

7.4 The Attenuation Mechanism and the Isolator Bracket

The LPN account provides a specific prediction about the attenuation of \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}}\) in the primary specification. Downward nominal rigidity and tenant lock-in are not frictions that disappear over time; they are structural features of the low-income rental market. LHA-dependent tenants face high moving costs, limited alternative supply, and weak outside options — precisely the conditions the LPN framework formalizes through the binding reproduction constraint. The COVID-window restriction (R3) partially addresses attenuation by using the 2020-Q2 to 2021-Q1 uplift period as the comparison baseline: this identifies the predicted reversal from accelerated rent growth at the uplift to decelerated growth at the re-freeze in high-intensity areas. That reversal — the precise fiscal-capture prediction when LHA functions as a binding yield floor that is first raised and then held fixed — is what R3 estimates, and it yields \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}} = -0.0498\) (\(p = 0.0033\)).

Two attenuators can be separated, and each was identified as a confound on theoretical grounds rather than chosen for effect. The reversal window (R3) addresses the nominal rigidity just described; a density \(\times\) freeze interaction (R15) addresses the post-COVID recovery of urban demand, which lifted rents in lower-density areas and so masked capture. Their crossing is the bracket reported as Table 2 (§5.1): addressing the density confound roughly doubles the estimate (R1 \(\to\) R15), the uplift-baseline window roughly triples it (R1 \(\to\) R3), and the two compound in R20.

We read this range as a bracket, not a point estimate. The lower bound, \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}} = -0.0174\), is the incidence tenants actually experience — frictions included — and is the policy-relevant magnitude; the upper reach, \(-0.0675\), is the mechanism’s force once both confounds are netted out. That two pre-identified attenuators move the estimate the same way and partly compound is a dose-response in confound-removal: specification-searching selects one significant estimate from a scatter, whereas a coherent gradient that emerges as named confounds are removed is not easily manufactured.

The bracket also marks where the two accounts part company on what the effect is. The standard reading treats \(-0.0675\) as the underlying parameter and \(-0.0174\) as friction-attenuated noise to be cleaned away. Under LPN the frictions are not measurement nuisance: downward nominal rigidity and tenant lock-in are the structural condition of the benefit-dependent class. The lived \(-0.0174\) is then not a biased-down estimate of a frictionless truth but the genuine short-run incidence under the actual structure — and because lock-in erodes as tenants are eventually forced to move or renegotiate, realised capture migrates from the lower bound toward the upper reach the longer the freeze runs. That migration is the mechanism behind the continuously declining effective transfer documented in §7.5 and §9. R20, which combines both adjustments, remains post-hoc and corroborating rather than primary (§8).

The same asymmetry runs along a second margin, alongside the temporal one. The two attenuators are not symmetric: the 2020 uplift passed into rents quickly — the higher-powered \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{up}}\) — whereas downward nominal rigidity prevents the subsequent freeze from being disgorged in kind, so the real erosion of the allowance is not discounted back into lower rents but absorbed by tenants out of the non-housing consumption base \(C_t\). On this reading a small \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}}\) is not weak capture but the price-side shadow of a transfer that has fallen on tenants rather than registering as a rent correction; and because the magnitude of that transfer is the running gap between a frozen allowance and rising market rent, it grows with market-rent inflation — slight when rents are flat, severe when an adverse shock drives them up. The 2021–24 window over which the freeze is identified was exactly such an adverse-shock regime — the post-pandemic demand rebound, the 2022 energy-price shock, and the inflation and monetary tightening it triggered (§2.1) — so the wedge the design captures is not a worst case but the realised incidence under the conditions that prevailed, the allowance slipping on its own statutory terms from the 30th toward roughly the 11th percentile.

7.5 Implications for Fiscal Policy Evaluation

The standard framework for evaluating housing benefit in the UK public finance literature asks whether LHA expenditure achieves its stated purpose of sustaining housing access for low-income households. The fiscal capture evidence implies a partial negative answer: a substantial share of the transfer is absorbed in higher rents rather than retained in tenant welfare, so the benefit is diluted in transmission. From this vantage point, the appropriate fiscal policy response is benefit redesign — tighter caps, direct payment reform, or supply-side investment that bypasses the capitalization channel.

The LPN account identifies a deeper difficulty with this framing. If LHA functions as a reproduction subsidy — maintaining the non-housing consumption base of benefit-dependent households at a level consistent with demographic reproduction — then evaluating it as a rent-payment mechanism misidentifies its structural role. A benefit-incidence calculation finding 60–67% landlord capture (Gibbons and Manning 2006) implies not only that tenants receive roughly 33–40 pence per pound of LHA; it implies that the reproduction subsidy is 60–67% ineffective in sustaining the consumption base it is designed to protect, and that this effective subsidy rate has been declining in real terms throughout the 2021–24 re-freeze as market rents rose above the frozen allowance. The fiscal policy question is not only how much of the transfer is capitalized, but how much reproduction capacity is sustained per pound of LHA expenditure, and the answer to the latter has been deteriorating continuously since 2021.

This reframing shifts the appropriate metric for fiscal evaluation. If the policy goal is reproduction-capacity maintenance rather than rent-payment sufficiency, the relevant benchmark is not whether LHA covers the 30th percentile of local market rents — a distribution that is itself partly inflated by prior capitalization cycles — but whether LHA, net of landlord capture, sustains the non-housing consumption floor for benefit-dependent households at or above the reproduction-consistent minimum. LHA policy that periodically re-links to the 30th percentile does not reduce the rent share; it resets the baseline for the next capitalization cycle. A cash allowance frozen in nominal terms is, in this sense, a one-way valve: each re-linking transmits the prevailing market level upward into the subsidy baseline, but the freeze that follows withholds the offsetting protection precisely when an adverse shock raises rents fastest. The April 2024 re-linking and the April 2025 re-freeze confirm this cycle is structurally embedded in the current design (Bangham et al. 2026). Both theoretical lenses reach the same negative verdict: LHA reform cannot be assessed solely on its rate-setting rules. The standard account demands assessment on the incidence-adjusted net transfer to tenant welfare; the LPN account extends this to the net transfer to tenant reproduction capacity. On both metrics, a benefit that is periodically uplifted only to be immediately re-frozen provides no durable improvement.

8. Limitations

  1. Statistical power: The primary specification is underpowered. A 12-quarter post-freeze window is short relative to the adjustment horizon; downward nominal rigidity further attenuates \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}}\). R3 (COVID-window) and R20 (R3+density) provide better-powered estimates.

  2. Post-2021 austerity confound: General fiscal tightening in high-deprivation local authorities after 2021 is correlated with lha_intensity through the deprivation channel. If austerity reduced housing demand in high-intensity areas, it could produce \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}} < 0\) through a demand channel unrelated to the LHA yield floor. Three pieces of evidence run against this confounder. First, the COVID-window restriction (R3: 2020-Q2–2024-Q1) uses the 2020 fiscal-stimulus period as the comparison baseline; under the austerity hypothesis, R3 should be weaker than R1 (because the comparison window includes peak stimulus). Instead R3 (\(\hat{\beta} = -0.0498\)) is nearly three times larger than R1 (\(\hat{\beta} = -0.0174\)), consistent with the uplift-to-re-freeze reversal mechanism rather than with an austerity demand channel. Second, the R4 placebo (2015–2019, covering the initial 2016 freeze and significant austerity) yields \(\hat{\beta} = +0.0085\) (\(p = 0.562\)): high-intensity areas show no negative rent growth in the first austerity wave, which would not be expected if deprivation-correlated demand compression were the mechanism. Third, Region×Quarter fixed effects absorb common regional fiscal shocks; residual within-region lha_intensity variation would need to be driven by LA-specific austerity heterogeneity beyond regional trends to confound the estimate. Fourth, pre-period LHA intensity is orthogonal to local rent levels (\(r = 0.02\); Figure 3), so the treatment is not a proxy for area rent fundamentals — the channel through which a generic local-demand or supply-inelasticity story would have to operate. A full rebuttal of the austerity channel specifically would require quarterly LA-level public expenditure data, which remains a limitation of this analysis. Appendix B (Figure 6) sets out this absorbed-surge logic — and the price-versus-reproduction split it implies — as a schematic for non-specialist readers.

  3. R20 post-hoc status: The mechanistic isolator (R20) is post-hoc rather than pre-specified. It is corroborating, not primary.

  4. p10 wage endogeneity: ASHE annual frequency precludes formal empirical resolution of the collider question. The R21 sensitivity (§6.3) provides partial empirical insulation: the 0.5% coefficient shift when p10 is excluded is inconsistent with meaningful mediation or over-absorption of the LHA treatment shock. PAYE RTI (monthly) remains the preferred upgrade for formal resolution.

9. Conclusion

The UK Local Housing Allowance provides a rare natural experiment for identifying fiscal capture: two policy events with directionally opposite predictions in a difference-in-differences framework that uses pre-period intensity variation across 290 local authorities. The empirical pattern is consistent with the fiscal capture prediction across every combination of estimation window, spatial control, bedroom composition, and outlier treatment we examine. The directional signature — decelerated rent growth in binding areas at the re-freeze, consistent with the predicted reversal from the uplift baseline — holds in 24 fiscal capture specifications with zero sign reversals.

The primary specification is underpowered by construction. Downward nominal rigidity and tenant lock-in attenuate the re-freeze coefficient exactly when fiscal capture is real, so \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}} = -0.0174\) (\(p = 0.142\)) is best interpreted as a lower bound on the underlying effect. The COVID-window restriction, which identifies the directional reversal more sharply, yields \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}} = -0.0498\) (\(p = 0.0033\)). After additionally stripping post-COVID urban demand recovery attenuation via a density interaction, the mechanistic isolator yields \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}} = -0.0675\) (\(p = 0.0001\)). The conservative sample restriction excluding high net in-migration local authorities produces a more negative coefficient than the full-sample estimate, confirming that fiscal capture is not purely compositional: the yield-floor mechanism operates in areas without significant displacement pressure.

These results are consistent with both the standard fiscal capture account and the LPN reproduction account. The two accounts are observationally equivalent at the level of rent-growth coefficients; they diverge on whether LHA is distortionary (standard account) or structurally necessary (LPN account). The distinguishing empirical test — whether binding local authorities show slower household formation and population growth relative to non-binding areas after the 2021 re-freeze — is outside the scope of this paper and is the subject of ongoing work.

For policy, the results confirm that the 2021–24 LHA re-freeze progressively reduced the effective transfer to benefit-dependent households: as market rents rose above the frozen allowance, the share of LHA retained in tenant welfare — rather than capitalized by landlords — declined continuously. Under the LPN account, this constitutes erosion of the reproduction subsidy that LHA provides to benefit-dependent households in the private rented sector. The April 2024 re-linking, itself frozen again in April 2025, has merely reset the cycle. Under either theoretical lens — fiscal capture or reproduction compression — LHA policy that bounces between temporary uplifts and persistent freezes is structurally inadequate (Bangham et al. 2026) — and most inadequate precisely when adverse shocks drive rents up fastest, since a nominal freeze withholds real protection in the states where the reproduction base is most compressed. The LPN account implies the additional prescription: a reproduction-consistent rent ceiling that reduces the rent share toward the LPD-optimal minimum (characterized formally in Huato and Galindo 2026c), not merely a periodic re-linking of the benefit rate to an already overinflated market rent distribution.

All fiscal capture specifications use continuous lha_intensity interacted with \(\text{freeze}_t\). R4 is an identification check (placebo, expected \(\hat{\beta} \approx 0\)). R12 (binary threshold) replaces continuous lha_intensity with a binding indicator; near-zero result is an absorption diagnostic, not a sign reversal in the continuous-intensity sense. Figure 5 plots the distinct estimates from the table above as a directional specification curve.

Reading the specification curve. This is a robustness display, not a battery of independent tests, and the apparent weakness of the right-hand specifications should be read with care. What it shows: all 24 distinct estimates are negative and none reverses sign across the estimation window, spatial control, clustering, bedroom composition, outlier treatment, and sample restriction; ordered by the one-sided upper bound, the specifications whose sign is established most decisively — the better-powered isolators of §7.4 — sit at the left. How not to read it: a wide whisker, or an upper bound above zero, is imprecision, not evidence against the effect. An underpowered specification — a short post-freeze window, a thinner sub-sample, or the deliberately noisy HB-only proxy (R18) — returns a negative point estimate with a confidence bound that spans zero; it is inconclusive on its own terms, which is not the same as a null result. Two corollaries follow. First, horizontal position reflects the precision of the sign test, not the size of the effect: the most negative point estimates (the R18 set) carry the widest standard errors and therefore fall to the right, because magnitude without precision is uninformative. Second, no single coefficient is the inferential object — the primary specification is itself a conservative lower bound, attenuated by the structural frictions analysed in §7.4 — so reading any individual non-significant specification as a failed test mistakes the unit of evidence, which is the consistency of the sign across the full set.

Appendix B — Mechanism Schematic

Figure 6 sets out, for economists and policymakers alike, how the freeze does its work and where the estimated effect sits within the wider macroeconomic episode. It is a conceptual schematic of the identification and the incidence — not a causal diagram.

The upper layer is the macroeconomic rent surge of 2021–24: the post-pandemic demand rebound, the 2022 energy shock and the inflation and monetary tightening it triggered, and a contracting rental supply, which together drove market rents up roughly seven per cent a year. That surge is common to all local authorities within a region in a given quarter, so the region-by-quarter fixed effects absorb it wholesale — it is the backdrop, never the estimated effect. This is the visual answer to the natural objection that the result merely reflects the inflation of the period (§8.2): what is estimated is the differential below the identification line, the within-region-quarter contrast in rent growth by pre-period benefit intensity.

Below the line, the frozen allowance and the rising market rent open the policy wedge — the £88–£217 per month gap documented in §2.1, which by construction widens the faster market rents run. The wedge admits two readings of the same coefficient. On the price side it shows up as decelerated rent growth in benefit-heavy areas, \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{fr}}\), the estimated lower bound: landlords cannot lift rents to a cap that no longer rises. But because downward nominal rigidity and tenant lock-in keep most rents from falling, the larger part of the wedge is never registered in price at all; it is absorbed by tenants out of the non-housing consumption base \(C_t\) — the food-and-bills budget the LPN reproduction function treats as the input to demographic reproduction. The standard account (Lens 1) reads the price effect as a distortionary transfer to landlord yields; the LPN account (Lens 2) reads the tenant-borne residual as compression of reproduction capacity — a necessary subsidy eroded in real terms, with a further, separately testable demographic prediction (slower household formation; Galindo 2026a). The policy implication follows directly from the asymmetry the figure encodes: a cash allowance frozen in nominal terms withholds protection precisely in the high-inflation states where the wedge — and the burden on \(C_t\) — is largest.
