Rent as Sustenance Cost

Carlos Galindo Escajeda June 2026

Preliminary working paper — please do not cite, quote, or circulate without the author’s permission.

Abstract

For NEF, this paper supplies the positive welfare rationale beneath the Fair Rents brake — not just a defence of it, but a reason it moves the market toward, rather than away from, the social optimum. It treats housing cost not as ordinary consumption but as a deduction from the resources a household needs to sustain itself, so a rising rent share directly compresses the capacity to sustain and form new households. Embedding this in the Sustenance-Capacity Dominance (SCD) welfare criterion, the paper derives a sustenance-consistent rent ceiling: the rent share a social planner would choose, set at the minimum that still lets landlords cover genuine costs at undistorted asset prices. Conditional on a clearly-stated empirical premise — that post-2008 financialisation pushed buy-to-let prices above their fundamental level — market rents are forced above this ceiling, and a CPI-anchored growth cap of the kind NEF proposes moves the market back toward the optimal path. The paper is disciplined about scope: the overvaluation premise is maintained (its empirical test is delegated to the companion fiscal-capture paper), the within-period result is what is established, and the fuller self-reinforcing rent-and- demography cycle is presented as an interpretive conjecture for future work, not a proven dynamic.

1. Introduction

The UK LHA policy of 2016–24 offers two natural experiments for identifying fiscal capture in housing markets. In April 2016, LHA was frozen at cash levels that held until March 2020. In April 2020, the government temporarily restored LHA to the 30th percentile of local market rents — the first uprating since 2016. From April 2021, LHA was re-frozen at these uplifted cash levels while market rents continued to rise. The Autumn Statement 2023 re-linked LHA to the 30th percentile from April 2024, before freezing it again in 2025. These two events — the 2020 uplift and the 2021 re-freeze — create directionally opposite, testable fiscal capture predictions: if LHA operates as a yield floor for landlords, areas with higher LHA intensity should have seen rent growth accelerate at the uplift (the floor rises) and decelerate at the re-freeze (the floor is held below rising market rents). The two tests are not symmetrically powered: the uplift test (\(\beta_{\text{up}} > 0\)) is the higher-powered identifier because rents adjust readily to upward floor changes, while the re-freeze test (\(\beta_{\text{fr}} < 0\)) is supporting evidence subject to attenuation from downward nominal rigidity and tenant lock-in during periods of general inflation.

The empirical design for testing these predictions is developed in full by the LHA DiD project (Galindo 2026b). The econometric specification exploits pre-period LHA-intensity (housing benefit and Universal Credit housing element households as a fraction of PRS stock) interacted with indicators for the uplift and re-freeze events across local authorities, with region-by-quarter fixed effects absorbing common shocks. The key parameters \(\beta_{\text{up}} > 0\) (uplift accelerates rent growth in binding areas) and \(\beta_{\text{fr}} < 0\) (re-freeze decelerates it) are jointly the fiscal capture test. A clean pre-period — 2019-Q1 to 2020-Q1, before either policy event — provides the parallel trends baseline.

But the DiD design, however well-identified, cannot answer the prior theoretical question: why does fiscal capture occur structurally? Why do landlords price to the LHA rate rather than to a competitive market rate — and what are the welfare implications of a policy that interrupts that pricing?

The standard behavioral explanation is incomplete. It says: landlords in the HB sub-market know the maximum benefit their tenants receive and set rents accordingly. This is empirically documented — DWP qualitative surveys confirm it directly — but it is a description of the mechanism, not a theory of why the mechanism is profit-maximizing. In a genuinely competitive market, landlords would undercut each other to fill vacancies, driving rents below LHA. The persistence of LHA-anchored pricing requires either market power or structural complementarity between LHA rates and the sustenance costs of the benefit-dependent tenant population. We argue it is the latter.

The theoretical foundations we develop rest on three interlocking claims.

Claim 1 (Sustenance compression): Housing costs are not a generic consumption expenditure. They are inputs to the sustenance of the working population. When the rent share \(s_t\) rises — whether from rent increases, benefit freezes, or supply restrictions — the household’s capacity to sustain itself is compressed structurally, not merely constrained financially. This follows from embedding housing costs into the SCN sustenance function \(N_{t+1} = Be^{\eta_t}N_t^{1-\gamma}C_t^\gamma\), where \(C_t\) must be understood as non-housing consumption expenditure \((1-s_t)Y_t\).

Claim 2 (BTL overvaluation): In the competitive equilibrium, buy-to-let assets are priced by landlords using their own consumption-based stochastic discount factor. This SDF correctly prices the private risk of BTL investment — fluctuations in rental income and asset values — but cannot reflect the social optimum under the SCN welfare criterion. The SCD-consistent asset price is the highest price at which landlords can earn only the minimum participation rent at fundamental yields. Post-2008 financialization pushed market prices above this level; at overvalued prices, debt service requires rents above the SCD-optimal minimum, compressing tenant sustenance. The market-CE gap is the demographic externality discount — a structural consequence of BTL overvaluation and market incompleteness, not of investor irrationality.

Claim 3 (SCD-optimal rent ceiling): The social planner under the SCD criterion sets the rent share at the minimum consistent with landlord participation at fundamental asset prices. Conditional on the maintained overvaluation premise, the minimum participation rent is inflated above this level, and a self-reinforcing compression cycle is implied: high prices require high rents to service debt, which compress sustenance, which reinforces the demographic dynamics that originally justified the high prices. We establish the static (within-period) comparative statics of this mechanism; its full dynamic characterization — the existence and stability of a compressed steady state — is deferred to the forward programme. A CPI-anchored growth ceiling counteracts the escalating rent-to-income share that the overvaluation dynamic implies. This furnishes a structural, sustenance-grounded welfare reading for the NEF emergency brake, complementing the second-best justification.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the standard fiscal capture account and identifies its three theoretical gaps. Section 3 develops the housing-augmented sustenance function and LHA as sustenance subsidy. Section 4 derives the BTL asset-pricing anomaly and the demographic externality discount. Section 5 synthesizes the two theoretical lenses on the LHA mechanism and their divergent policy implications. Section 6 derives the sustenance-consistent rent ceiling. Section 7 concludes.

2. The Standard Account and Its Theoretical Gaps

2.1 Fiscal Capture: What the Evidence Establishes

The empirical literature on housing benefit capitalization is unusually consistent for housing economics. Gibbons and Manning (2006) exploit the 1996–97 UK housing benefit reforms — which reduced the maximum subsidy payable for new claimants — and find that between 60% and two-thirds of the incidence of the subsidy reduction fell on landlords through reduced rents, with rents paid falling by 6–11%. Their preferred model is a matching framework, not a competitive one: landlords and tenants match bilaterally, and the subsidy affects the rent set in matching rather than a market-clearing price.

DWP’s own econometric analysis (DWP 2012) of the April 2011 and April 2012 LHA reforms, which reduced LHA from the 50th to the 30th local percentile and severed the automatic uprating link respectively, finds “relatively substantial and statistically significant reductions in rental values” in affected areas. Critically, DWP qualitative surveys of private landlords document the mechanism directly: many landlords in the housing benefit sub-market “used the LHA rates to set their rents, either viewing these as good indicators of market rents, or because they were largely operating within the HB sub-market” (DWP 2011).

The institutional picture is clear. LHA operates in binding areas not merely as a subsidy ceiling but as a price signal: it tells landlords the maximum benefit a tenant can receive, which — under realistic assumptions about tenant budgets and search — also approximates the maximum rent they will pay. Landlords learn this and price to it. We treat the 2021–24 re-freeze — in which LHA was held at April 2020 cash levels while market rents continued to rise, progressively eroding the real value of the benefit — as economically analogous to the 1996–97 nominal benefit reduction studied by Gibbons–Manning: both compress real LHA generosity in binding areas. The symmetry of fiscal capture also predicts a positive incidence at the April 2020 uplift. Both predictions are empirical questions for Galindo 2026b.

2.2 Three Gaps in the Behavioral Account

The behavioral description above is accurate so far as it goes. Its theoretical limitations become apparent when we ask three questions it cannot answer.

Gap 1: Why does the sub-market not competitively erode the LHA anchor? If landlords can earn LHA-anchored rents above competitive equilibrium, entry should occur until rents are competed down. The persistence of LHA anchoring implies either that supply is inelastic (the supply-elasticity (P1) violation documented in the companion paper, Galindo 2026d) or that there is a structural complementarity between LHA rates and landlord willingness to accept benefit-dependent tenants. Both are plausible, but neither is made explicit in the standard fiscal capture account.

Gibbons and Manning’s (2006) preferred framework — bilateral matching rather than competitive price-taking — already provides a structural account: the subsidy affects the rent set in the match, not a market-clearing price, so competitive erosion does not apply directly. The SCN sustenance mechanism is complementary, not competing: it explains why LHA is informationally relevant to both parties in the match (LHA signals the tenant’s effective budget capacity, which is tied to their sustenance constraint), and it adds a welfare criterion — SCD population-path dominance — that the matching framework does not supply. The two accounts are, however, observationally close in their first-moment footprint: a sophisticated matching or income-effect account also predicts slower household formation in binding areas, so that prediction alone does not separate them. The sustenance model’s front-line empirical signature is instead the sustenance response — after-housing residual-income compression coupled to the location-rent wedge (§5.3) — with the demographic path a slow, confounded corroborating channel, not the sharp differentiator.

Gap 2: Why is capitalization heterogeneous across binding and non-binding areas? A purely behavioral story — landlords follow LHA — would predict capitalization everywhere. But the DiD design specifically tests for heterogeneity: \(\beta_1 < 0\) in binding areas, \(\beta_1 \approx 0\) in non-binding areas. The heterogeneity requires a structural story about why LHA is informative only in areas where it was previously constraining rents. The sustenance framework provides this story.

Gap 3: What is the welfare benchmark for evaluating fiscal capture? The standard framing treats fiscal capture as waste: public funds routing into private yields rather than tenant welfare. This framing implicitly takes the (unsubsidized) market price as the welfare benchmark. But if market prices are themselves distorted — by the financial and supply failures documented in Galindo 2026d — then the welfare comparison becomes indeterminate without a positive theory of what rents should be. LHA as sustenance subsidy, by contrast, provides a positive welfare criterion: rents should be consistent with population renewal, and LHA is part of the mechanism by which the state attempts (imperfectly) to enforce that constraint.

3. Housing Costs in the Sustenance Function

3.1 The Sustenance-Cost Foundation

Within the sustenance-cost framework of the parent theory (Galindo 2026c), the value of labour corresponds to the cost of the wage basket — the goods a worker requires to sustain their capacity to work. Housing is not incidental to this basket; it is constitutive of it. As Huato (forthcoming, B0) documents, urban land rent operates as a deduction from wages — a transfer from wages to economic rent — whenever the cost of shelter exceeds the cost of reproducing the worker’s capacity to work. The rent share \(s_t\) is therefore not merely an affordability problem; it is a claim on wages, operating through the price system to redirect resources from the sustenance process to landlords.

Put structurally, the sustenance of the working population — maintaining the physical, social, and cognitive capacities that workers bring to production — is a material process with real resource requirements. Housing is one of the most significant of those requirements in any advanced economy. When housing costs rise, the real value of the wage basket falls, even if nominal wages are held constant. This is the mechanism through which rising rents function as a real wage cut for tenant households.

The SCN model (Galindo 2026c) provides the formal expression of this insight.

3.2 The SCN Sustenance Function

The SCN model specifies population dynamics as a constant-returns-to-scale sustenance function:

\[N_{t+1} = B e^{\eta_t} N_t^{1-\gamma} C_t^{\gamma} \tag{1}\]

where \(N_t\) is the labor force (working-age population), \(C_t\) is aggregate consumption expenditure, \(B > 0\) is a scale parameter, \(\gamma \in (0,1)\) is the consumption share in sustenance, and \(\eta_t \sim \text{i.i.d.}(0, \sigma_\eta^2)\) is a sustenance shock. The function is CRS in \((N_t, C_t)\): proportional scaling of both inputs produces proportional output. This property is necessary for a balanced growth path to exist and for the value function normalization \(V = N^{1-\sigma}\Psi(k, \theta)\) to hold.

The demographic continuation factor \(\partial N_{t+1}/\partial N_t = (1-\gamma)N_{t+1}/N_t\) is the marginal contribution of current population to next-period sustenance, holding per-capita consumption \(C_t/N_t\) constant. It is the load-bearing term in both the stochastic Euler equation and the asset-pricing result (Theorem 4.1 of SCN): the term \((1-\gamma)\) connecting demographic growth to the pricing kernel.

3.2.1 Minimal Maintained Assumptions from SCN

For self-containment, we state exactly which results of the parent model are imported and maintained here, and the condition under which the housing-augmented function (equation 2) inherits them. These are maintained assumptions sourced to Galindo (2026c), not results re-derived in this paper.

  • (A1) Sustenance technology. \(N_{t+1} = Be^{\eta_t}N_t^{1-\gamma}C_t^{\gamma}\) is constant-returns-to-scale in \((N_t, C_t)\), with \(\gamma \in (0,1)\) (SCN §3).
  • (A2) SCD criterion. Population paths are ranked by the Ramsey–Weizsäcker overtaking criterion (§6.1); the competitive equilibrium is SCD-dominated by the planner’s path under incomplete demographic-insurance markets (SCN §5).
  • (A3) Valuation kernel. The normative SDF \(M^{SCN}\) (Appendix B, eq. B.1) is the dynastic valuation kernel of SCN (its §2 “Stochastic Discount Factor”; closed form in SCN Appendix A.2).
  • (A4) Calibration. \(\gamma = 0.4\) (SCN §4), used illustratively in §3.3 and §4.3.
  • (A5) Curvature domain. \(\sigma \ge 1\) in the SCN flow index \(u(N)=N^{1-\sigma}/(1-\sigma)\) (SCN §2, Theorem 4.1), under which the SCN asset-pricing results (A3) and the demographic risk-premium sign hold. This domain governs the SCN pricing kernel only; the planner’s rent-share problem of §6 ranks population paths by the SCD overtaking criterion (A2), which is linear in \(N_t\) and therefore does not invoke \(u\) or the sign of \(1-\sigma\) — see §6.1 and Appendix
  • (A6) Value-flow separability. Rent income is a charge on the sustenance fund \((1-s_t)Y_t\), while the balanced-growth capital stock \(k^*\) is maintained from the accumulation fund; equivalently, landlords’ propensity to reinvest rental income into the productive capital stock is taken to be zero within the model’s horizon (the standing modeling choice that makes the rent-share and capital margins separable in §6.2). This is a sectoral closure: it locates productive accumulation in a distinct capital-owning sector (the holders of \(K\) in the parent SCN general equilibrium), leaving the landlord–tenant rent relation as the object of analysis. §6.2 states the consequence of relaxing it.

Inheritance under the housing augmentation. Equation (2) replaces \(C_t\) in (A1) with the tenant sustenance base \((1-s_t)Y_t\). Under the zero-savings restriction of §3.3 — the subpopulation for whom \((1-s_t)Y_t\) exhausts non-housing consumption — this base is the object \(C_t\) of the parent model, so (A1)–(A2) and (A5) carry over without modification on the tenant subpopulation; the rent share \(s_t\) enters only as a multiplicative compression of the consumption argument, preserving the CRS form and the existence of a BGP in \((N_t, (1-s_t)Y_t)\). The asset-pricing apparatus of §4 operates on the aggregate economy under (A3) and the curvature domain (A5); the relation between the two is the subject of §4.5 and the heterogeneous-household extension of §7.3. We emphasise the division of labour between (A5) and (A2): the SCN pricing results require \(\sigma \ge 1\), but the planner’s rent-ceiling derivation of §6 rests only on the linear SCD criterion (A2) and so is insensitive to the curvature parameter — a point that matters for the boundedness of the planner’s objective in Appendix A.

3.3 Housing as Consumption Compression

In the SCN baseline, \(C_t\) is unrestricted household consumption. In the housing-augmented model, a fraction \(s_t \in [0,1]\) of household income \(Y_t\) is paid in rent. We maintain the assumption — standard in after-housing-costs poverty measurement and empirically defensible for working-age renters — that housing is an inelastic prerequisite: households must pay rent before any other consumption. We further restrict attention to tenant households operating near the hand-to-mouth constraint (savings rate approximately zero), so that non-housing consumption equals the residual \(C_t = (1-s_t)Y_t\). This is a plausible characterisation of benefit-dependent PRS tenants in the bottom income quintile and allows \(Y_t\) to be interpreted as consumption expenditure for this subpopulation.1

Substituting into equation (1):

\[N_{t+1} = B e^{\eta_t} N_t^{1-\gamma} \left[(1-s_t) Y_t\right]^{\gamma} \tag{2}\]

The rent share \(s_t\) compresses the consumption base available for sustenance. Taking logarithms and log-linearizing around the balanced growth path \((s^*, c^*, g^*)\), using \(\ln(1-s_t) \approx \ln(1-s^*) - \frac{s^*}{1-s^*}\hat{s}_t\) for small deviations:

\[\hat{n}_{t+1} = \eta_t + (1-\gamma)\hat{n}_t + \gamma \hat{y}_t - \frac{\gamma s^*}{1-s^*}\hat{s}_t \tag{3}\]

where \(\hat{x}_t \equiv \ln(x_t/x^*)\) denotes log-deviations. The term \(-\frac{\gamma s^*}{1-s^*}\hat{s}_t\) is the rent compression elasticity: a one-unit log-deviation in \(s_t\) reduces next-period log population by \(\gamma s^*/(1-s^*)\).

At the empirically relevant parameters — \(\gamma = 0.4\)2 and \(s^* = 0.30\) (approximately the UK median rent-to-income ratio for PRS working-age tenants, consistent with NEF 2026 data) — the rent compression elasticity is \(0.4 \times 0.30/0.70 \approx 0.17\). A ten-percent real rent increase (from \(s^* = 0.30\) to \(s_t = 0.33\), compressing \(c^{net}\) from \(0.70Y\) to \(0.67Y\) — a fall of \(4.3\%\)) reduces next-period log population by approximately \(0.4 \times 4.3\% \approx 1.7\%\) through the sustenance channel alone, consistent with the log-linearization (\(\hat{s}_t \approx 0.095\); \(-(0.17)(0.095) \approx -0.016\)). This elasticity is local to \(s^* = 0.30\); the underlying coefficient \(\gamma s/(1-s)\) is monotone increasing in \(s\), so the compression effect is larger for households at higher rent shares (at \(s = 0.485\) — the NEF 2026 housing-cost burden for the bottom income quintile, a different population from the PRS working-age median — the elasticity rises to \(0.4 \times 0.485/0.515 \approx 0.38\)). The BGP figure of \(0.17\) is therefore a conservative lower bound for bottom-quintile demographic effects. The demographic consequence of sustained rent-to-income increases of the kind observed in the UK PRS since 2010 is not a second-order welfare effect. It is a first-order demographic outcome.

3.4 LHA as Sustenance Subsidy

For households receiving Housing Benefit or Universal Credit housing element, the LHA transfer \(h_t\) (annual, per qualifying household) augments effective non-housing consumption:

\[C_t^{HB} = (1-s_t) Y_t^{HB} + h_t \tag{4}\]

where \(Y_t^{HB}\) is HB-household income. In sustenance terms, LHA partially offsets the compression effect of \(s_t\): it maintains the consumption base available for sustenance among benefit-dependent households at a level higher than the raw income net of rent would support.

When LHA is frozen in cash terms — as in 2020–24 — \(h_t\) stays constant while market rents \(R_t\) continue to rise. In binding areas (where LHA previously covered a significant fraction of prevailing rent), the real value of the transfer falls: \(h_t/R_t\) declines. For benefit-dependent households, this is equivalent to an increase in the effective \(s_t\): nominal rent absorbs a rising share of the augmented income \((1-s_t)Y_t^{HB} + h_t\). Under equation (3), this raises \(\hat{s}_t\), reducing \(\hat{n}_{t+1}\).

The prediction for the LHA DiD is structural, not behavioral. Areas with higher pre-freeze LHA intensity are areas where a larger share of the tenant population has its sustenance capacity supplemented by LHA. The freeze removes that supplement in real terms. Demographic growth slows in high-intensity binding areas relative to non-binding areas. Because demand for rental housing is tied to population dynamics — new households form as the working-age population grows — rent growth in high-intensity binding areas also slows. The coefficient \(\beta_1 < 0\) is structurally implied by the sustenance framework, not just by the behavioral capitalization story.

Critically, the two accounts — behavioral capitalization and structural sustenance compression — predict the same sign of \(\beta_1\) but assign different welfare interpretations to the result. We return to this divergence in Section 5.

4. Buy-to-Let Asset Pricing and the Demographic Externality

4.1 Standard Housing Asset Pricing

The standard framework for housing asset pricing applies the Consumption CAPM. For an investor holding a BTL property, the relevant stochastic discount factor is:

\[M_{t+1}^{BTL} = \beta \left(\frac{C_{t+1}^{own}}{C_t^{own}}\right)^{-\sigma} \tag{5}\]

where \(C^{own}\) is the consumption of the owner household and \(\sigma\) is the coefficient of relative risk aversion. The no-arbitrage condition gives the risk premium on BTL:

\[E_t[R_{h,t+1}] - R_f = -R_f \cdot \text{Cov}_t\!\left(M_{t+1}^{BTL},\, R_{h,t+1}\right) \tag{6}\]

where \(R_{h,t+1} = (R_{t+1} + P_{t+1})/P_t\) is the total BTL return (rental income plus capital gain). Since landlords receive rent rather than paying it, their consumption \(C^{own}\) is not directly affected by the rent share \(s_t\). In good aggregate conditions, consumption grows (M falls), and BTL returns are also high (rents and prices rise). The covariance is negative: \(\text{Cov}(M^{BTL}, R_h) < 0\), generating a modest positive risk premium on BTL.

Under the capitalization formula \(P = R/(r - g_R)\), the post-2008 compression of the risk-free rate \(R_f\) and the observed rise in expected rent growth \(g_R\) inflated BTL asset prices dramatically — the financialization dynamic documented in Section 1 of the companion paper (Galindo 2026d). The standard model explains the level of the boom but says nothing about whether BTL prices reflect social value.

4.2 The Benchmark Price and the Sustenance-Grounded Rate

The benchmark price \(P^{SCD}\) capitalises the provision rent \(\underline R\) at the rate the parent model’s sustenance problem supplies. Its dynastic valuation kernel — the Euler condition of a representative fully-insured working dynasty valuing sustenance capacity \(u(N_t)=N_t^{1-\sigma}/(1-\sigma)\), \(\sigma\ge1\), under the CRS sustenance function — reduces, at the deterministic steady state, to the constant-growth capitalisation \(P=D/(r^{*}-g_R)\) with \(r^{*}=\bar g^{\sigma}/\beta-1\) [Galindo 2026c, §2, Appendix A.2, Prop 6.2]. The kernel’s full stochastic structure — the housing-augmented demographic-growth channel and the covariance that gives BTL its demographic-hedge character (it pays off precisely when sustenance outcomes are poor) — is set out, self-containedly, in Appendix B. It is not reproduced in the main text because the overvaluation result (Proposition 1) and the rent ceiling (§6) rest only on this capitalisation and the linear SCD criterion, not on the kernel’s curvature.

Two points are essential and are developed in §4.5. First, this valuation rate is not the SCD welfare criterion: the criterion that ranks rent-share paths in §6 is linear in \(N_t\), whereas the rate that prices the asset descends from the parent model’s curved valuation of demographic risk — two distinct objects the parent model maintains side by side, and only the valuation object carries curvature. Second, the kernel is a normative construct used solely to define \(P^{SCD}\); it is not a positive claim that the hand-to-mouth tenants of §3.3 solve an Euler equation, nor the rate that clears the decentralised market — equilibrium BTL prices are set by landlords through their own consumption-based discount factor \(M^{BTL}\) (eq. 5).

4.3 Normative Overvaluation and the Demographic Externality Discount

The normative question is distinct from the asset’s risk character (the demographic-hedge property set out in Appendix B): what does the SCD welfare criterion say about the socially consistent asset price?

Defining \(P^{SCD}\). From the planner’s analysis in Section 6.2, the SCD-optimal policy minimizes the rent share \(s_t\) subject to landlord participation at fundamental (undistorted) asset prices. The minimum participation rent \(\underline{R}\) is the per-unit physical and financial cost of housing provision — exogenous to the asset price:

\[\underline{R} = (\delta + m + r_f) K_h\]

where \(\delta\) is depreciation, \(m\) is maintenance cost, \(r_f\) is the risk-free opportunity cost of capital, and \(K_h\) is the replacement (rebuild) cost of the structure — a produced asset. Non-produced land is excluded from \(K_h\) by construction: it is neither depreciated, maintained, nor reproducible, so the rent required to sustain provision is a return on the produced structure alone. This is the exact reading of a replacement cost, and it is what makes the wedge \(R^{mkt} - \underline{R}\) interpretable as the location rent on land (below). Define the SCD-consistent price as the capitalization of this exogenously determined minimum participation rent under the SCN valuation kernel \(M^{SCN}\) (Appendix B, eq. B.1):

\[P^{SCD} = \frac{\underline{R}}{r - g_R}\]

This is not an imported Gordon–Growth identity but the steady-state reduction of the kernel \(M^{SCN}\) applied to the rent stream (SCN Proposition 6.2): pricing \(\underline R\) by no-arbitrage, \(P_t = \mathbb{E}_t[M^{SCN}_{t+1}(\underline R_{t+1} + P_{t+1})]\), and iterating forward to the deterministic steady state, the discount rate is the kernel’s own, \(r = r^{*} = \bar g^{\sigma}/\beta - 1\) (convergent for \(r > g_R\)). Here \(g_R\) is the structure-rent’s long-run trend — replacement/construction-cost growth. Because \(r^{*}\) is pinned to the sustenance base (\(\bar g \approx 1\), near-stationary population) while \(g_R\) tracks construction cost and per-capita earnings, \(g_R\) can meet or exceed \(r^{*}\), and the Gordon level \(\underline{R}/(r^{*}-g_R)\) then diverges. That divergence is, however, an artifact of capitalising a reproducible asset: the structure is a produced good (above), so by free entry its fundamental value is simply its replacement cost \(K_h\) — the Gordon form merely re-expresses \(K_h\) when the no-arbitrage identity \((\delta+m+r_f)=(r^{*}-g_R)\) holds, and signals departure from the reproducible-structure equilibrium (binding land scarcity, capitalised into non-produced land) when it does not. The benchmark structure value is therefore \(K_h\), not an infinite-horizon sum. What this means for the results below: they hold the rate fixed to isolate the rent channel — a decomposition, not a claim that the market discounts at \(r^{*}\) — so the overvaluation and ceiling results are rate-cancelling (\(P^{mkt}/P^{SCD}=R^{mkt}/\underline{R}\)), the capitalised land/scarcity rent, and survive at any common rate even where \(r^{*}\approx g_R\) renders the Gordon level ill-posed. The fundamental yield \(\rho_f \equiv \underline{R}/P^{SCD} = r - g_R\) is therefore sustenance-grounded — its components are the sustenance-capacity growth \(\bar g\), the curvature \(\sigma\) of the objective over \(N\), and the discount \(\beta\) over sustenance capacity, not a posited time-preference.3 \(P^{SCD}\) is a normative benchmark — the BTL price consistent with the planner’s optimal rent share \(s^* = \underline{R}/Y\).

Why \(P^{mkt} > P^{SCD}\). Both prices are capitalised at the same sustenance-grounded rate \(r = r^{*}\): \(P^{mkt} = R^{mkt}/(r^{*} - g_R)\) (with \(R^{mkt} = \rho_{mkt} \cdot P^{mkt}\) the market rent) and \(P^{SCD} = \underline{R}/(r^{*} - g_R)\). Holding the rate fixed isolates the channel this paper formalises: the gap runs entirely through the rent, so the overvaluation condition \(P^{mkt} > P^{SCD}\) is exactly equivalent to \(R^{mkt} > \underline{R}\) — market rents exceed the SCD-consistent minimum. The supply inelasticity documented in the companion paper (Galindo 2026d), together with the leverage structure of Section 6.3, sustains \(R^{mkt}\) above \(\underline{R}\): under fixed LTV, total debt service scales linearly with \(P^{mkt}\), so any excess of \(P^{mkt}\) over \(P^{SCD}\) directly raises required rents above \(\underline{R}\). (A distinct rate-channel account — a compressed market yield rather than an elevated rent — is treated separately below and is not the mechanism of \(\Delta P\).)

The demographic externality discount is the capitalized value of this excess:

\[\Delta P \equiv P^{mkt} - P^{SCD} = \frac{R^{mkt} - \underline{R}}{r - g_R} > 0 \tag{11}\]

The incompleteness mechanism. The market incompleteness that sustains \(P^{mkt} > P^{SCD}\) operates through tenant exclusion. Tenant households bear the sustenance cost of \(R^{mkt} > \underline{R}\) but do not hold BTL assets. They cannot discipline landlord pricing through portfolio rebalancing, nor can they capture the hedge value of BTL through asset ownership. The social cost of above-minimum rents is therefore never reflected in the BTL market price.4

The normative results above — the \(P^{SCD}\) benchmark, the externality discount \(\Delta P\) of equation (11), and the rent ceiling of Section 6 — are complete as stated and do not require a calibrated value for the externality. A heuristic bridge to future empirical calibration of the externality’s magnitude, which transfers the SCN demographic risk-premium coefficient to the housing asset by analogy, is deferred to §7.3 to keep the present derivation free of analogy-based steps.

Motivating the premise on UK data. We treat \(P^{mkt} > P^{SCD}\) — equivalently \(R^{mkt} > \underline{R}\) — as a maintained empirical premise: its magnitude is the companion paper’s empirical object, but its sign is not assumed blind, and the comparison is rent-against-rent, needing no income denominator. The participation rent \(\underline{R} = (\delta+m+r_f)K_h\) prices only the produced structure. At a structure rebuild cost of roughly £1,750 per square metre (BCIS rebuilding-cost index, 2026) and the private-rented mean floor area of 75 square metres (English Housing Survey 2023–24), \(K_h \approx\) £130k (£110–150k across the rebuild-cost band); with \(\delta \approx 2\%\) (the ~50-year service life ONS applies to dwellings; ONS 2021), maintenance and management \(m \approx 1\)–\(1.5\%\) of structure value, and a real risk-free rate \(r_f \approx 1.7\%\) (ten-year index-linked gilt, DMO), the user-cost rate is about \(4.7\)–\(5.2\%\) at these central values (widening to \(4\)–\(6.5\%\) across plausible parameter ranges), and \(\underline{R} \approx\) £440–£710 per month. The ONS mean private rent is £1,368 per month (Price Index of Private Rents, December 2025) — above the entire \(\underline{R}\) band; the larger all-stock mean floor area of 96 square metres would raise \(\underline{R}\) only to £560–£910, still well below it. So \(R^{mkt} > \underline{R}\) holds for the UK mean across the plausible parameter range, the wedge widest in high-rent regions.

The wedge is decisive in interpretation, not only in arithmetic. Because \(\underline{R}\) by construction prices only the produced structure, the residual \(R^{mkt} - \underline{R}\) is the location rent on non-produced land — and by the ONS national balance sheet (ONS 2022), land underlying dwellings rose to over 70% of dwelling value by 2016 (from a roughly even split in the mid-1990s) and stood at £5.4 trillion in 2020. With land already the majority of dwelling value, that the wedge is positive is close to definitional for the UK PRS; it is the size of the overvaluation, not its sign, that the companion paper estimates. On current UK figures the premise is thus not a free modelling assumption but a quantitative presumption the data already favour.

This excess-rent route — \(R^{mkt} > \underline{R}\), the wedge \(\Delta P\) of equation (11), the rent varying at the fixed sustenance-grounded rate — is the channel the paper formalises, and the figures above bear on it. It must be kept apart from the rate channel of the financialisation literature, in which a compressed market yield rather than an elevated rent lifts the price: on the construction here the price-to-rent ratio is \(P^{mkt}/R^{mkt} = 1/(r^{*}-g_R)\), a pure function of the discount rate that is silent on whether \(R^{mkt}\) exceeds \(\underline{R}\). That literature documents an elevated UK market through this distinct channel — the OECD’s whole-market standardised price-to-rent ratio places UK prices about 32% above their long-run average in 2025, which the Bank of England attributes largely to the secular fall in the real risk-free rate (Bank of England 2019) — but it is the rate-wedge decomposition of SCN Proposition 6.3 (price varying with the rate at fixed cash flow), not the excess-rent wedge \(\Delta P\) (rent varying at the fixed rate). The two are complementary; conflating them would misattribute the source of the gap. Proposition 1 rests on the excess-rent channel, which the UK provision-cost and land-share evidence motivates.

The participation floor and the capitalised land value. A natural objection is that real landlords bought the land, so a floor set at the structure-only \(\underline{R}\) leaves them unable to service the land-acquisition debt: a cap at \(\underline{R}\) would then imply not the mere loss of excess yield but insolvency. The objection is correct as accounting, and it is precisely the point. In the sustenance ontology land is a non-produced condition; the part of the purchase price that capitalised the location rent is a claim by title, not a payment for produced provision. A ceiling at \(\underline{R}\) therefore writes down the capitalised land value on landlord balance sheets — it does not destroy a produced asset or interrupt the provision of housing services. This is the incidence the historical record displays under regulation: the binding rent, when capped, is borne as a write-down of the capitalised asset rather than as a charge for a service — the discriminator of the companion history paper (Galindo 2026e, §6). The transition is consequently a balance-sheet event for holders who bought at the overvalued price — a redistribution from capitalised title back to the sustenance fund, not a deadweight loss of housing services — whose management (the pace of the write-down, the treatment of leveraged holders) is a matter of transition design and funding, taken up in the companion policy paper (Galindo 2026d) — a genuine open question of incidence (how the write-down distributes across leverage bands, and its fiscal treatment), not a refutation of the SCD-optimal floor.

Proposition 1 is the conditional implication: given this premise, the CE rent share exceeds the SCD-optimal level.

[!NOTE]

Proposition 1 (Normative BTL Overvaluation). Let \(P^{SCD} = \underline{R}/(r - g_R)\) be the BTL price consistent with the SCD-optimal rent share \(s^* = \underline{R}/Y\), and let \(P^{mkt}\) be the CE market price. Under the maintained empirical premise \(P^{mkt} > P^{SCD}\), market rents \(R^{mkt} = \rho_{mkt} P^{mkt}\) exceed the SCD-consistent minimum \(\underline{R}\), and therefore \(s_t^{mkt} = R^{mkt}/Y_t > s^*\). The CE is sustenance-compressing: the market rent share exceeds the SCD-optimal level, with the excess sustained by financialization and market incompleteness.

Proof. Immediate from the definitions. \(P^{mkt} > P^{SCD}\) with \(P^{SCD} = \underline{R}/(r - g_R)\) implies \(R^{mkt} > \underline{R}\) — the fixed-LTV leverage mapping (15a) of §6.3 makes this rent excess explicit — and hence \(s_t^{mkt} = R^{mkt}/Y_t > \underline{R}/Y_t = s^*\). \(\square\)

4.4 BTL Overvaluation as Structural, Not Behavioral

This result is distinct from the behavioral overvaluation stories in the housing literature (irrational exuberance, momentum trading, disaster myopia). The demographic externality discount is a structural consequence of CE market incompleteness — the same mechanism that drives the SCD failure in the capital market equilibrium of the SCN model. It does not require any irrational behavior by any market participant. Each agent acts optimally given their information and their own SDF. In particular, no individual agent prices the aggregate demographic externality of above-minimum rents — landlords correctly price their own consumption risk via \(M^{BTL}\) (eq. 5); tenants, excluded from BTL ownership, cannot trade against it. The externality is unpriced precisely because no SDF in the market reflects it.

The structural claim is distinguishable from a behavioral asset-overvaluation account — irrational exuberance, momentum, disaster myopia — by its sustenance predictions: a purely behavioral overvaluation generates no compression of the tenant sustenance fund in high-intensity binding areas under the LHA freeze, whereas the structural account does. As §5.3 sets out, the front-line discriminator is the sustenance response (after-housing residual income and in-work poverty, coupled to the location-rent wedge), not the demographic response, which is a slow, confounded corroborating channel whose non-appearance does not falsify the thesis. Against behavioral asset overvaluation specifically, even the demographic compression distinguishes — the budget-side squeeze being shared with orthodox budget economics — but it is a weak test, not a clean falsifier.

The policy implication is equally structural. A behavioral overvaluation can in principle be corrected by better information, improved price discovery, or investor education. A structural overvaluation driven by market incompleteness requires an institutional correction that forces the market to internalize the externality — a tax, a cap, or a direct regulation of the asset class. Section 6 derives the precise form of that institutional correction.

4.5 Class-Consistent Pricing: Who Prices What

Because the model is explicitly class-divided, it is essential to state which agents price which objects, so that no representative-agent device is smuggled into a class framework. Three distinct roles operate, and they do not collapse into one another:

  1. Landlords price BTL assets in the competitive equilibrium. The market price \(P^{mkt}\) and the market rent \(R^{mkt} = \rho_{mkt}P^{mkt}\) are set by the landlord/investor group using its own consumption-based discount factor \(M^{BTL}\) (eq. 5). Landlords receive rent and hold the asset; their consumption \(C^{own}\) is the only consumption stream entering the equilibrium pricing kernel.

  2. The planner evaluates demographic paths with the SCN kernel. The SCN stochastic discount factor \(M^{SCN}\) (Appendix B) is a normative object: it is used only on the planner’s side to define the SCD-consistent benchmark \(P^{SCD}\) and the demographic externality discount \(\Delta P\) (eq. 11). It is not an equilibrium pricing kernel and is never used to clear the BTL market.

  3. Tenants price nothing. The hand-to-mouth tenants of §3.3 hold no BTL assets, do no intertemporal consumption smoothing on the housing margin, and therefore solve no asset-pricing Euler equation. Their sustenance is compressed by \(R^{mkt}\); they are the excluded party whose interest no market kernel represents — the source of the incompleteness identified in §4.4.

The wedge \(P^{mkt} - P^{SCD}\) is therefore the gap between the price landlords actually set (via \(M^{BTL}\)) and the price a planner valuing the sustenance of the working population would set (via \(M^{SCN}\)). The two kernels are deliberately kept apart; their formal integration within a single heterogeneous-household equilibrium — in which landlord and tenant groups coexist with distinct budget sets — is the aggregation extension deferred to Section 7.3. The present paper’s results do not require that integration: every claim is either a competitive-equilibrium statement priced by \(M^{BTL}\) or a normative statement evaluated by \(M^{SCN}\), and the two never appear on the same side of an equation.

One objection should be met head-on, because it rests on a premise the SCN framework explicitly rejects. A reader trained on the welfare-economics duality between prices and quantities — that the pricing kernel must be the marginal-utility gradient of the objective that ranks allocations — will expect the rent ceiling \(s^*\) and the benchmark price \(P^{SCD}\) to descend from a single objective, and will read the linear-ranking / curved-valuation split as an inconsistency (“two planners”). But that duality is a property of a utilitarian social objective, and SCD is not utilitarian: it is, in the parent model’s own words, “structurally linear (risk-neutral) in expected \(N\),” and “can rank an allocation as dominant that a Paretian welfare function would reject, and vice versa” [Galindo 2026c, §3, Remark on SCD structure]. Severing the price–quantity duality is therefore not an oversight but a defining commitment of the framework: the welfare ranking of population paths (linear, used for \(s^*\)) and the valuation of demographic risk borne by an asset-holder (curved, used for \(P^{SCD}\)) are answers to two different questions and need not share a gradient. Collapsing them into one curved planner — as the duality would require — would reintroduce exactly the variance-penalising Paretian objective that SCD was constructed to displace, and would make this paper inconsistent with its parent rather than more rigorous.

A second separation, distinct from the kernel separation above, must also be kept in view, because it is easily mistaken for an inconsistency. The SCN framework deploys two normative objects, not one, and they have different curvature by construction. The valuation object is the curved dynastic Bellman index \(u(N)=N^{1-\sigma}/(1-\sigma)\) with \(\sigma \ge 1\), whose risk aversion is precisely what generates the demographic risk premium and hence the pricing kernel \(M^{SCN}\) used to define \(P^{SCD}\) (SCN Theorem 4.1). The ranking object is the Sustenance-Capacity Dominance criterion, which SCN states is “structurally linear (risk-neutral) in expected \(N\)” — a discounted Ramsey–Weizsäcker overtaking order over population paths, deliberately not the curved utilitarian sum (Galindo 2026c, §3, Remark on SCD structure). This is not a free choice of convenience but a defining commitment of the parent model: SCD evaluates physical sustenance capacity directly, without penalising variance in \(N\) as such. Consequently the asset price \(P^{SCD}\) (a valuation, curved kernel, \(\sigma \ge 1\)) and the rent ceiling \(s^*\) (a ranking of feasible paths, linear SCD criterion) are derived from the same parent framework using the two objects it already maintains side by side — not from two mutually inconsistent planners. The rent-ceiling derivation of §6 and Appendix A uses only the linear ranking object; the curvature parameter \(\sigma\) enters this paper solely through the pricing apparatus of the present section.

5. Two Lenses on the LHA Mechanism

The sustenance framework and the standard fiscal capture account agree on the empirical prediction: \(\beta_1 < 0\) in binding local authorities. They diverge sharply on the welfare interpretation and the policy implications.

5.1 Lens 1: Fiscal Capture (Standard Account)

Under the fiscal capture account, LHA in binding areas acts as a price floor: landlords set rents at or near the maximum LHA rate, knowing tenants’ budgets are constrained at that level. When LHA is frozen and market rents rise, the floor progressively unbinds from below — LHA can no longer support rents at the market level — and rent growth in binding areas slows relative to non-binding areas.

Welfare interpretation: LHA is distortionary. It transfers public funds from general revenues to private landlord yields rather than to tenant welfare. The DiD finding of \(\beta_1 < 0\) is evidence of that distortion: it shows that LHA was sustaining rents above what the market alone would have delivered. The policy implication is LHA reform: move away from cash subsidies into bricks-and-mortar investment, or redesign LHA to fund social housing acquisition directly, bypassing the landlord capitalization channel.

5.2 Lens 2: Demographic Compression (SCN Account)

Under the sustenance framework, LHA in binding areas acts as a sustenance subsidy: it maintains the non-housing consumption base of benefit-dependent households at a level consistent with population renewal. When LHA is frozen, the effective subsidy falls in real terms, compressing the sustenance capacity of high-intensity areas. Demand for housing — driven by household formation, which is driven by demographic growth — slows in these areas. Rent growth slows not because the yield floor recedes but because the demand base contracts.

Welfare interpretation: LHA is a necessary but insufficient response to housing market failure. The DiD finding of \(\beta_1 < 0\) is evidence that LHA was contributing to population renewal, and its freeze has had adverse demographic consequences in high-intensity areas. The policy implication is not LHA reform alone but rent cap plus LHA augmentation: the rent cap reduces \(s_t\) toward the sustenance-consistent level, while reformed LHA (decoupled from market rents) directly supplements household consumption capacity.

5.3 The Divergence and Its Significance

The two accounts are observationally equivalent at the level of the rent-growth coefficients but distinguishable through the incidence the sustenance framework predicts — first on the tenant population’s standard of living, and only slowly on its population renewal. The two-event DiD (Galindo 2026b) yields the rent-growth tests \(\beta_{\text{up}} > 0\) (April 2020 uplift) and \(\beta_{\text{fr}} < 0\) (April 2021 re-freeze), which both frameworks share. Beyond them the sustenance framework predicts a structured set of effects, ranked by how directly each reads the mechanism — rent compresses the sustenance fund \((1-s_t)Y_t\) — and how cleanly it discriminates the structural account from behavioural capitalisation:

Two points order this set. First, the discriminating tests are the sustenance ones, not the demographic ones — and the cut is sharper than “rent crowds out other spending,” which orthodox budget-constraint economics predicts equally. What separates the sustenance account is the coupling: it is rent above the provision cost \(\underline R\) — the location-rent wedge \(R^{mkt}-\underline R\) of §4.3 — forcing the binding tenant population below its historical-moral subsistence standard. (This standard-of-living reading is the deferred foundational criterion of §7.3, not the head-count SCD object the paper maintains; here it names only what the sustenance metrics detect — themselves the leading observable of the sustenance-fund compression that the head-count criterion registers only slowly.) Monopoly rent is orthodox; monopoly rent that demonstrably compresses sustenance below that standard — the after-housing residual-income and in-work-poverty dose-response to the LHA freeze, on the companion’s DiD — is the heterodox discriminator. These after-housing metrics are also the strongest tests: near-unity elasticity to \(s_t\) by construction, and measured directly in UK administrative data (DWP Households Below Average Income before- and after-housing-cost series; ONS Living Costs and Food Survey; Understanding Society), rather than inferred from a slow behavioural response.

Second, the demographic predictions — population growth and household formation — are retained but demoted to a slow, attenuated, confounded auxiliary. Fertility and household formation have low and slow income-elasticity in rich economies; households absorb a rent shock by cutting consumption, doubling up, or migrating long before they fail to form. A near-zero demographic estimate therefore does not falsify the thesis — the squeeze will already have registered in the sustenance metrics; demographic slowing (testable with ONS mid-year estimates and MHCLG data) is a corroborating channel, not the front-line test. The falsifier moves with it: it is the sustenance dose-response the thesis stakes itself on — were the after-housing residual-income and in-work-poverty responses to the freeze, coupled to the location-rent wedge, to show no compression in high-intensity binding areas, the structural account would be refuted. The rent-growth coefficients remain the companion paper’s identification object, with the uplift test (\(\beta_{\text{up}}>0\)) the primary rent-side identifier and the re-freeze test (\(\beta_{\text{fr}}<0\)) supporting evidence subject to downward nominal rigidity and tenant lock-in (so a near-zero \(\beta_{\text{fr}}\) is not on its own diagnostic).

The welfare divergence is the more fundamental difference. If LHA is merely distortionary (fiscal capture), the correct response is to eliminate the distortion: stop subsidizing private landlords, redirect funds to supply. If LHA is a sustenance subsidy that the market has colonized (sustenance account), the correct response is to protect the sustenance function while eliminating the colonization: cap rents to remove the landlord capitalization channel, and redesign LHA as direct income support uncorrelated with market rent levels.

6. The Sustenance-Consistent Rent Ceiling

6.1 The Planner’s Problem Under SCD

The Sustenance-Capacity Dominance criterion [Galindo 2026c, Section 5] ranks population paths using the Ramsey–Weizsäcker overtaking criterion: path \(\{N_t^A\}\) dominates path \(\{N_t^B\}\) if there exists \(T^*\) such that for all \(T > T^*\):

\[\sum_{t=0}^{T} \beta^t N_t^A > \sum_{t=0}^{T} \beta^t N_t^B\]

The SCN paper establishes that the CE fails SCD: incomplete markets for demographic insurance mean the CE population path is dominated by the social planner’s path.

In the housing context, the planner maximizes the SCD criterion over \(\{s_t\}\), the sequence of rent shares, subject to:

  1. Sustenance: \(N_{t+1} = Be^{\eta_t} N_t^{1-\gamma}[(1-s_t)Y_t]^\gamma\)

  2. Landlord participation: \(s_t Y_t \geq \underline{R}_t\) where \(\underline{R}_t = (\delta+m+r_f)K_h\) is the minimum rent covering depreciation, maintenance, and the risk-free opportunity cost on the produced structure (replacement cost \(K_h\), exogenous to the market asset price) — equivalently the fundamental yield \(\rho_f\) on the structure-only price \(P_t^{SCD}\) (§4.3).

  3. BGP condition: capital per effective worker \(k_t = k^*\).

6.2 The Optimality Condition

The sign-determining partial derivative with respect to \(s_t\), holding \(Y_t\) fixed and treating the sustenance function as the constraint, is:

\[\frac{\partial N_{t+1}}{\partial s_t} = -\frac{\gamma N_{t+1}}{1-s_t} < 0 \tag{13}\]

Because this partial is strictly negative throughout the interior of the feasible set, the planner’s SCD-maximizing solution lies at the lower boundary of the participation constraint — the corner \(s_t = \underline{R}_t/Y_t\). The result does not require solving the full dynamic program, and — critically — the corner is not undone by the balanced-growth capital constraint \(k_t = k^*\). The reason is structural, not merely technical. In the sustenance ontology of this paper, rent is a charge on the sustenance fund — the consumption base \((1-s_t)Y_t\) out of which the working population sustains itself — whereas the investment that maintains \(k^*\) is financed from the accumulation fund. This value-flow separability is the standing modeling choice (A6): landlords’ propensity to reinvest rental income into the productive capital stock is taken to be zero within the model’s horizon, so the rent-share and capital-accumulation margins draw on distinct value flows. Lowering \(s_t\) then raises the tenant sustenance base without drawing down the accumulation fund, so it relaxes no capital constraint and tightens none. Two consequences follow. First, because the SCD criterion ranks population paths by the linear overtaking objective \(\sum_t \beta^t N_t\) (A2) — not by the curved utility sum that governs the SCN pricing kernel — the objective is strictly increasing in \(N_t\) at every date with no dependence on the curvature parameter \(\sigma\). Second, \(N_{t+1}\) is strictly decreasing in \(s_t\) (equation 13) at every date, and no channel exists by which a higher \(s_t\) relaxes the participation, sustenance, or BGP-capital constraint. Lowering \(s_t\) therefore raises every term of the objective simultaneously: no intertemporal trade-off arises and the static sign is globally decisive. A compact Lagrangian treatment — verifying that the participation multiplier is strictly positive (the constraint binds) and that the capital and sustenance multipliers do not enter the \(s_t\) first-order condition — is given in Appendix A and delivers the same corner.

The separability (A6) is load-bearing, so its failure mode should be stated. If landlords reinvest a fraction \(\phi > 0\) of rental income into the productive capital stock, then \(k^*\) ceases to be independent of \(s_t\): a higher rent share would relax the capital constraint (iii), the multiplier \(\xi_t\) would enter the \(s_t\) first-order condition with a positive sign, and that term would partially offset the strictly negative sustenance term of equation (13). The corner \(s^* = \underline{R}_t/Y_t\) would then soften into an interior solution trading sustenance compression against accumulation, and the sign of the net effect would become a quantitative question about \(\phi\). Qualitatively, the corner survives as long as the sustenance loss from a marginal rise in \(s_t\) — the strictly negative term \(\lambda_t \gamma N_{t+1}/(1-s_t)\) — dominates the accumulation gain channelled through \(\phi\) and the capital multiplier \(\xi_t\); the corner softens to an interior optimum only once the latter overtakes the former. We do not calibrate that threshold here, because signing it requires the endogenous \(\xi_t\) from the heterogeneous-class equilibrium that is deferred to §7.3; stating the comparison, rather than a manufactured number, is the honest scope. The corner result is thus exact in the \(\phi = 0\) sectoral closure and an upper bound on the optimal rent share for \(\phi > 0\).

The SCD-optimal rent ceiling is therefore the minimum rent share consistent with landlord participation at fundamental asset prices:

\[s^* = \frac{\underline{R}_t}{Y_t} = \frac{\rho_f \cdot P_t^{SCD}}{Y_t} \tag{14}\]

The second equality is an algebraic identity, and it discharges any worry that the curved pricing kernel (Appendix B) contaminates the linear rent ceiling. By the definition of the fundamental yield, \(\rho_f \equiv \underline{R}_t/P_t^{SCD}\), so \(\rho_f \cdot P_t^{SCD} = \underline{R}_t\) whatever value the kernel assigns to \(P_t^{SCD}\). The curvature parameter \(\sigma\) enters only through the discount rate \(r\) and hence through \(P_t^{SCD}\) and \(\rho_f\) separately; their product collapses to the physical participation rent \(\underline{R}_t = (\delta + m + r_f)K_h\), which depends on no pricing object. The rent ceiling \(s^* = \underline{R}_t/Y_t\) is therefore invariant to \(\sigma\): it is fixed entirely by the physical sustenance parameters and tenant income. This is the operational content of the valuation/ranking separation of §4.5 — the financial valuation \(P_t^{SCD}\) may carry the demographic risk premium, but the policy instrument \(s^*\) does not inherit it.

where \(\rho_f\) is the fundamental yield requirement (covering maintenance, depreciation, and the risk-free rate at fundamental valuation).5

6.3 The Overvaluation–Compression Cycle

Under BTL overvaluation, \(P_t^{mkt} > P_t^{SCD}\). Highly leveraged BTL investors require debt-service payments on the overvalued asset price, which implies:

\[s_t^{mkt} = \frac{\rho_{mkt} \cdot P_t^{mkt}}{Y_t} > \frac{\rho_f \cdot P_t^{SCD}}{Y_t} = s^* \tag{15}\]

The mechanism linking BTL overvaluation to higher required rents operates through UK BTL leverage structure. With fixed loan-to-value ratio \(L\) and gross mortgage rate \(i_t\), the minimum rent required to service debt on a BTL property worth \(P^{mkt}\) satisfies:

\[R^{min}_t = \left[\rho_e + (i_t - \rho_e)L\right] P^{mkt}_t \tag{15a}\]

where \(\rho_e\) is the equity yield on the unlevered asset. This expression scales linearly with \(P^{mkt}\): a 10% increase in the market price raises the minimum required rent by exactly 10% at fixed LTV. Under overvaluation (\(P^{mkt} > P^{SCD}\)), \(R^{min}_t > \rho_f P^{SCD} = \underline{R}\), directly establishing the market rent excess of equation (11). The yield-compression objection — that overvaluation compresses \(\rho_{mkt} = R/P^{mkt}\), potentially lowering required rents — conflates the ex-post yield on the overvalued asset with the ex-ante minimum rent required to service the purchase price. At UK BTL LTV ratios of 60–75%, the leveraged minimum rent rises faster than the yield compresses.

The market rent share therefore exceeds the SCD-optimal ceiling: this within-period comparison is the formally established result. Sustenance is compressed below the planner’s path. The full dynamic characterization of the cycle — existence, uniqueness, and stability of a fixed point \(s^{cycle} > s^*\) — requires off-BGP analysis beyond the log-linearization and is deferred to the forward programme (Section 7.3); the account that follows is therefore an interpretive conjecture about the dynamics, not an established theorem.

Conjecturally, this would constitute a self-reinforcing cycle: overvalued assets require high rents to service, high rents compress sustenance, compressed sustenance reduces household formation, reduced household formation slows organic demand growth, but demographic compression also validates BTL as a demographic hedge (its worst periods coincide with poor demographic outcomes), which sustains investor demand for BTL as a hedge, potentially preventing the price from correcting to fundamental even as the physical demand for housing slows. On this reading the overvaluation would be self-perpetuating precisely because it generates the demographic conditions that justify the demographic hedge premium. Establishing whether this fixed point exists and is stable is the central task of the deferred dynamic analysis.

6.4 The CPI-Anchored Growth Ceiling as SCD Approximation

The NEF Fair Rents proposal (NEF 2026) caps annual rent increases at the lower of CPI inflation or 2%. Under the sustenance framework, this approximates the SCD-optimal path in the following sense.

On the BGP, the SCD-optimal rent share \(s^* = \underline{R}/Y\) is constant if rents and income grow at the same rate. CPI inflation is approximately equal to nominal income growth at the BGP (if real wages are roughly stable). A ceiling of CPI therefore maintains the rent-to-income ratio at its current level — neither compressing nor expanding it.

The 2% cap does more: it forces the rent-to-income ratio downward whenever CPI exceeds 2%, which has been the UK experience since 2021. Real-wage growth on top of inflation only sharpens this: the faster nominal income outpaces the 2% rent ceiling, the faster \(s_t\) falls toward \(s^*\). During periods of rent inflation above 2% (the current UK situation), the cap prevents \(s_t\) from rising further above \(s^*\).

More precisely, the CPI/2% ceiling approximates the SCD path under the following sufficient condition: current market rents are already above the SCD-optimal level \(s^*\), so any cap that holds or reduces the rent-to-income share moves the economy toward the planner’s optimum. This is satisfied whenever:

\[s_t^{mkt} > s^* \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \frac{P_t^{mkt}}{P_t^{SCD}} > 1 \tag{16}\]

which is exactly the BTL overvaluation condition of Proposition 1. The NEF ceiling is therefore not merely an emergency instrument justified by second-best welfare analysis (though it is that, as established in Galindo 2026d). It is the first-order SCD-optimal policy response to a structurally overvalued BTL market.

[!NOTE]

Proposition 2 (CPI Ceiling as SCD Approximation — within-period, conditional). Maintain the value-flow separability (A6) and transition-path feasibility (holding or reducing \(s_t\) leaves the participation floor \(\underline{R}_t\) and fundamental yields unchanged within the period). If the CE BTL market exhibits the demographic externality discount of Proposition 1 (i.e., \(P^{mkt} > P^{SCD}\) and therefore \(s_t^{mkt} > s^*\)), then any rent growth ceiling that prevents \(s_t\) from rising further moves the economy toward the SCD-optimal demographic path. A CPI-anchored growth ceiling satisfies this condition whenever actual rent growth would otherwise exceed CPI — the case throughout the UK PRS since 2021.

Proof. By Proposition 1, the overvaluation premise (condition (16)) gives \(s_t^{mkt} > s^*\). A ceiling that holds or reduces the rent-to-income share moves \(s_t\) weakly toward \(s^*\); since \(N_{t+1}\) is strictly decreasing in \(s_t\) (equation 13), every such step weakly raises each term of the SCD objective. The CPI/2% anchor enforces a non-increasing \(s_t\) precisely when counterfactual rent growth exceeds CPI. \(\square\)

The proposition is a within-period comparative-static result: it establishes the direction of movement under the ceiling, holding the participation floor \(\underline{R}_t\) and fundamental yields fixed. It assumes transition-path feasibility — that holding or reducing the rent-to-income share does not itself trigger capital destruction, abrupt landlord exit, or supply shocks that would shift \(\underline{R}_t\) and so violate the ceteris-paribus conditions of equation (13). The full off-BGP transition dynamics, including any such feedback, belong to the deferred dynamic analysis of §6.3 and §7.3.

6.5 The No-Vacancy-Decontrol Requirement

Proposition 2 applies only if the ceiling is binding at the intensive margin and there is no decontrol mechanism at tenancy turnover. Diamond, McQuade, and Qian (2019) show that the San Francisco 1994 rent control, which permitted full rent reset at vacancy, was evaded through the very mechanism the ceiling was designed to block: eviction and re-letting reset the rent-to-income share back to market levels.

Under the sustenance framework, vacancy decontrol has a specific additional cost. Each eviction displaces a household, interrupting the tenure continuity that supports household formation and stable sustenance. Frequent disruption increases \(\sigma_\eta\) (the sustenance shock variance) even if the expected rent level is controlled. This is a consistency argument: the SCN model does not formally parameterize tenure disruption as a \(\sigma_\eta\) shifter, and a full derivation of the variance effect on SCD dominance would require the heterogeneous-household extension of SCN (Section 7.3). The structural intuition — that higher shock variance raises uncertainty around the planner’s path and attenuates the SCD-dominance result in expectation — is consistent with the model’s logic, and provides a second, independent design motivation for no-vacancy-decontrol.

The NEF no-vacancy-decontrol design is therefore not merely an anti-evasion device. It is, under the sustenance framework, a variance-reduction instrument that keeps \(\sigma_\eta\) low by maintaining household tenure stability. This is a second, independent reason why no-vacancy-decontrol is the correct design from SCD first principles — supporting the design argument already made on second-best grounds in Galindo 2026d.

7. Conclusion

7.1 What This Paper Establishes

This paper provides three results that complement the second-best welfare analysis of the NEF emergency brake in the companion paper (Galindo 2026d).

First, fiscal capture is structurally necessary, not merely behaviorally contingent. Housing benefit capitalization into private rents arises because housing costs enter the sustenance function for the working population, and LHA compensates for the sustenance-compressing effect of above-subsistence rents. Landlords price to LHA in binding areas not only because they can but because LHA is the signal of effective household budget capacity in markets where tenant incomes are insufficient to sustain the sustenance-consistent rent. The DiD prediction of \(\beta_1 < 0\) follows from this structural account without invoking any behavioral assumption about rent-setting. As §5.3 sets out, the rent-growth signs alone do not discriminate the structural account from behavioral capitalization — both predict the same \(\beta_1 < 0\); the discriminating evidence is the sustenance response — after-housing residual income and in-work poverty, coupled to the location-rent wedge — with the demographic response a slower corroborating channel. Both are deferred to the companion empirical paper.

Second, conditional on the maintained empirical premise that post-2008 financialization pushed BTL market prices above their fundamental level, buy-to-let assets are overvalued relative to the SCD social optimum. The SCD-consistent price is the highest price at which landlords earn only the minimum participation rent at fundamental yields. Given that premise, market rents are forced above the SCD-optimal minimum, compressing tenant sustenance. Tenant households, excluded from BTL ownership, cannot internalize the demographic externality through market participation. The gap — the demographic externality discount — is a structural consequence of BTL overvaluation and market incompleteness, not of investor irrationality.

Third, the sustenance-consistent rent ceiling is the SCD-optimal policy instrument. The planner minimizes the rent share subject to landlord participation at fundamental (unovervalued) asset prices, under the value-flow separability (A6). BTL overvaluation forces market rents above this minimum. The within-period compression is established here; its full self-reinforcing dynamic form is advanced as a conjecture pending the off-BGP analysis (Section 6.3). Conditional on the overvaluation premise of Proposition 1 and the within-period feasibility conditions of Proposition 2, a CPI-anchored growth ceiling moves the rent share toward the SCD-optimal path whenever current market rents exceed the fundamental level — which, under that premise, they do.

7.2 Relation to Prior Work

The results formalize, within the parent theory’s sustenance-cost framework (Galindo 2026c; Huato forthcoming, B0), the insight that housing is a cost of reproducing the working population: rent as a deduction from wages finds its formal expression in the housing-augmented sustenance function (equation 2). Huato’s (forthcoming, B0) analysis of urban land rent as a capitalised claim — a claim on future income discounted to a present price — corresponds formally to the BTL capitalization formula \(P = R/(r - g_R)\), and his identification of the deduction mechanism corresponds to the demographic externality discount \(\Delta P\) of equation (11).

The SCN model is not built on that tradition’s formal apparatus (it is a stochastic dynamic optimization model with rational agents), but its welfare criterion — SCD — captures the concern with the sustenance of the working population as a social end, not merely an input to production. The SCD result that CE markets fail to achieve the planner’s demographic path is the formal counterpart of the argument that unconstrained accumulation can undermine the conditions of its own reproduction.

7.3 The Forward Empirical Programme

This paper is theoretical; no estimation is undertaken here. Its empirical counterpart is ordered by how directly each test reads the mechanism — rent compresses the sustenance fund \((1-s_t)Y_t\) — with the standard-of-living tests in front and the demographic ones, slower and more confounded, behind.

The rent-side identification (\(\beta_{\text{up}} > 0\) at the 2020 uplift, \(\beta_{\text{fr}} < 0\) at the 2021 re-freeze) is the subject of the companion empirical paper (Galindo 2026b), to which all estimation, specifications, and inference are referred. The present paper supplies the structural prediction the companion’s difference-in-differences design tests.

The front line is the sustenance programme — the most direct and least confounded reading of the compression of \((1-s_t)Y_t\), and the part that discriminates the structural account from behavioural capitalisation. Three measurable, fast tests, all on the companion’s LHA quasi-experiment and standard UK administrative data: (i) After-housing residual income and real non-housing consumption in high-intensity binding areas across the freeze — predicted to fall, with near-unity elasticity to \(s_t\) (DWP Households Below Average Income before- and after-housing-cost series; ONS Living Costs and Food Survey; Understanding Society for the panel). (ii) In-work after-housing-cost poverty — predicted to rise as rent forces the binding population below the historical-moral subsistence standard. This is the heterodox discriminator only when coupled to the location-rent wedge \(R^{mkt}-\underline R\) (§4.3): it is rent above provision cost, not housing cost as such, that the sustenance account indicts (the bare after-housing gap is shared with orthodox budget economics). (iii) Owner–renter wealth divergence via the rent-transfer channel and tenure entrenchment — the renter→owner transition suppressed, and, by the §4.3 capitalisation logic extended to owner-occupiers, the location rent plausibly capitalised into the purchase price and thence into mortgage debt (debt-to-income of mortgaged owners) rather than escaped by ownership (ONS Wealth and Assets Survey). This carries the relational axis — the perpetuation of the propertyless condition (cf. Galindo 2026e, §8) — and is conjectural in both direction and magnitude (it rests on that tenure conjecture and the §4.3 extension, not on this paper’s hand-to-mouth-tenant mechanism).

The demographic tests are an auxiliary, not the front line. Whether high-intensity areas also show differential slowing of household formation and population growth (\(\Delta n < 0\), \(\Delta HH < 0\)) can be pursued with ONS mid-year estimates and MHCLG data, and the demographic externality \(\delta_{demog}\) requires estimating \(\text{Cov}(\Delta n, R_h)\) at LA level (the ONS Price Index of Private Rents, successor to VOA PRMS, for returns; mid-year estimates for demographics). But fertility and household formation are slow and confounded in rich economies, so their non-appearance does not falsify the thesis once the sustenance tests have registered the squeeze. A calibration heuristic (optional, not a result of this paper) expresses the demographic cost per unit of BTL return as

\[\delta_{demog} \approx (1-\gamma)\Omega \cdot \frac{\text{Cov}(\Delta n, R_h)}{\sigma_{R_h}^2} \tag{17}\]

where \((1-\gamma)\Omega\) is the demographic risk-premium coefficient of SCN Theorem 4.1 (Galindo 2026c), GMM-identifiable from cohort fertility and matched asset-pricing data. That theorem was derived for the capital asset \(K\), not a housing asset; equation (17) transfers the coefficient by analogy, presuming cross-sectoral capital mobility sufficient that no housing-specific risk wedge survives — not innocuous under this paper’s incompleteness premise, which is why the transfer is confined to this note. At illustrative parameters (\(\gamma = 0.4\), \(\Omega = 0.6\), \(\text{Cov}(\Delta n, R_h) < 0\) in binding markets), \(\delta_{demog} < 0\), consistent with BTL’s demographic-hedge property (Appendix B), but its magnitude is an empirical question.

A theoretical extension completes the programme: a heterogeneous-household SCN model in which the tenant subpopulation’s sustenance shock variance \(\sigma_\eta\) responds to tenure stability and in which the SCD-dominance comparison can be computed across heterogeneous paths. It would formalise the off-BGP dynamics of the §6.3 overvaluation cycle (the existence and stability of \(s^{cycle} > s^*\)) and resolve the aggregation gap between the zero-savings tenant model (§3) and the asset-pricing setup (§4). It also marks where a deeper foundational question becomes tractable: whether the welfare object should be the standard at which the workforce is sustained — the deviation of the after-rent consumption wage from a historically-and-socially determined sustenance standard — rather than the head-count path the SCD criterion ranks. This paper takes the head-count criterion from the parent model as given; the sustenance front line above is where that question would be joined.

Together these would let the sustenance-consistent rent ceiling \(s^*\) be estimated from data rather than inferred from the SCD optimality condition alone — giving policymakers, and advocates for the NEF Fair Rents programme, not just that market rents exceed the SCD-optimal level but by how much, and what ceiling restores the path.

7.4 Closing Remarks

The standard case against rent control rests on the presumption that market prices reflect competitive conditions and that interfering with them creates deadweight loss. Both the companion paper (Galindo 2026d) and this paper show why that presumption fails in the UK PRS.

The companion paper shows it fails on institutional grounds: the UK PRS is a quadruple-object market (shelter, asset, collateral, fiscal conduit) that violates the foundational conditions of competitive welfare analysis at three simultaneous margins. This paper shows it fails on sustenance-dominance grounds: even if the market were institutionally competitive, the CE would fail to achieve the SCD-optimal demographic path because BTL pricing omits the demographic externality, and the missing externality generates a structural sustenance compression cycle that no competitive equilibrium can internalize.

The rent ceiling derived here is not a departure from economic reasoning. It is that reasoning applied consistently — inclusive of the sustenance function that standard models leave out. Conditional on the maintained overvaluation premise and the value-flow separability, it yields a clear policy conclusion: a no-vacancy-decontrol, CPI-anchored growth ceiling is the SCD-optimal instrument for a sustenance-compressing BTL market of the kind that premise describes — the market UK housing policy appears to have constructed. On those conditions, the case for protecting Britain’s renters is not only humanitarian: it follows from the strongest economic reasoning available, applied consistently.

Appendix A: Proof of the Corner Solution (§6.2)

We verify the corner solution under the value-flow separability assumption stated in (A6) and the linear SCD ranking criterion (A2); the curved pricing index of §4.2 plays no role here (see §4.5 on why the valuation and ranking objects differ in curvature by construction of the parent model).

The planner ranks rent-share sequences \(\{s_t\}_{t\ge0}\) by the SCD overtaking criterion of SCN (Galindo 2026c, §3 and Appendix A.3), which is linear in the population path: sequence \(A\) dominates \(B\) if \(\liminf_{T\to\infty}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\beta^t\big(N_t^A - N_t^B\big) \ge 0\), with strict inequality somewhere. This is the criterion (A2); crucially, it is defined directly over \(N_t\) and does not pass through the curved flow index \(u(N)=N^{1-\sigma}/(1-\sigma)\) that governs the SCN pricing kernel. The choice variables are subject, at every date \(t\), to:

    1. the sustenance technology \(N_{t+1} = Be^{\eta_t}N_t^{1-\gamma}[(1-s_t)Y_t]^{\gamma}\);
    1. the landlord participation constraint \(s_t Y_t \ge \underline{R}_t\);
    1. the balanced-growth capital condition \(k_t = k^*\).

Monotonicity in the path. Because the objective weights are \(\beta^t > 0\) and every \(N_t\) enters with a positive coefficient, the criterion is strictly increasing in each \(N_t\). By (i), raising \(N_{t+1}\) requires raising the sustenance base \((1-s_t)Y_t\) for some \(t\), i.e. lowering \(s_t\); and by the recursion in (i) a higher \(N_{t+1}\) weakly raises every \(N_{t+\tau}\), \(\tau \ge 1\). No curvature or sign restriction on \(1-\sigma\) is used: the ranking is linear in \(N\).

First-order condition in \(s_t\). Attach multipliers \(\lambda_t\) to (i), \(\mu_t \ge 0\) to (ii), and \(\xi_t\) to (iii). Only constraints (i) and (ii) contain \(s_t\); constraint (iii) and the marginal value of capital do not — by value-flow separability (A6), rent redistributes within the sustenance fund while \(k^*\) is maintained from the separate accumulation fund (§6.2). Hence

\[\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial s_t} = -\lambda_t\,\gamma\,\frac{N_{t+1}}{1-s_t} + \mu_t Y_t .\]

Sign of \(\lambda_t\). The multiplier \(\lambda_t\) is the shadow value of \(N_{t+1}\) in the overtaking sum. Since the criterion is strictly increasing in every \(N_t\) and \(N_{t+1}\) propagates into all later dates through (i), \(\lambda_t > 0\) at any candidate optimum. The first term is therefore strictly negative for every interior \(s_t \in (\underline{R}_t/Y_t,\,1)\).

Complementary slackness. With \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial s_t < 0\) throughout the interior, stationarity can hold only with \(\mu_t > 0\): the participation constraint binds, pinning

\[s_t = \frac{\underline{R}_t}{Y_t} = s^* \qquad \text{for every } t.\]

The capital multiplier \(\xi_t\) does not enter the \(s_t\) first-order condition, confirming that constraint (iii) is slack in \(s_t\): the corner is determined entirely by the sustenance objective and the participation floor, with no intertemporal trade-off — the formal counterpart of the value-flow separability (A6) argued in §6.2.

Remark (no growth-bound is required, by construction of the criterion). A reader accustomed to a curved discounted objective \(\sum_t \beta^t u(N_t)\) would here demand a boundedness condition such as \(\beta(1+g^*)^{1-\sigma} < 1\) evaluated at the growth-maximizing corner. The SCD criterion is constructed precisely to avoid that demand. As SCN states (Appendix A.3), “no spectral-radius restriction on \(\beta g_N\) is imposed … the criterion is adopted precisely to handle the case where \(N_t\) grows along the equilibrium path”: the overtaking order ranks paths by the \(\liminf\) of finite partial sums and is well defined even when \(\sum_t \beta^t N_t\) diverges. In contrast to a conventional discounted-utility objective \(\sum_t \beta^t u(N_t)\), which would require a boundedness condition evaluated at the maximised growth rate to keep the infinite sum finite, the SCD criterion needs no such restriction. The corner \(s_t = s^*\) delivers the pointwise maximal \(N_t\) at every date (each finite partial sum is maximized term-by-term), so it overtakes every feasible alternative regardless of convergence; no parameter restriction on the maximized growth rate \(g^*_{\max}\) is needed for admissibility. (The separate pricing-side boundedness condition — the SCN contraction bound on the value function used to derive the SDF, which does involve \(1-\sigma\) — applies to §4’s asset-pricing apparatus, not to this rent-share program.) The corner \(s_t = \underline{R}_t/Y_t\) thus satisfies the first-order conditions, complementary slackness (\(\mu_t > 0\)), and the overtaking dominance comparison at every date, and is the unique SCD optimum. \(\square\)

Appendix B: The SCN Valuation Kernel and BTL’s Demographic-Hedge Character

This appendix sets out, self-containedly, the stochastic valuation kernel that the main text (§4.2) uses only through its steady-state reduction \(P^{SCD}=\underline R/(r^{*}-g_R)\). It is drawn from the parent theory paper [Galindo 2026c, §2 and Appendix A.2]; nothing in it is required for the overvaluation result (Proposition 1) or the rent ceiling (§6), which rest on that reduction and the linear SCD criterion alone. It is collected here, rather than in the body, so the main argument carries its political-economy weight without the stochastic apparatus. (Equations B.1–B.4 below are the former main-text equations (7)–(10), relocated here; the body’s display equations accordingly run (1)–(6) and resume at (11).)

The dynastic kernel. The parent model’s representative fully-insured working dynasty maximises the curved sustenance-capacity index \(u(N_t)=N_t^{1-\sigma}/(1-\sigma)\), \(\sigma\ge1\), under the CRS sustenance function. Its intertemporal optimality (Euler) condition yields the stochastic discount factor

\[M_{t+1}^{SCN} = \beta \left(\frac{N_{t+1}}{N_t}\right)^{-\sigma} \frac{\Psi_k(k_{t+1}, \theta_{t+1})}{\Psi_k(k_t, \theta_t)} \tag{B.1}\]

where \(\Psi_k\) is the marginal value of capital in the intensive-form value function \(V = N^{1-\sigma}\Psi(k, \theta)\). The kernel is high when population growth is low — the marginal value of each surviving unit of sustenance capacity is high when the population is contracting. At the deterministic steady state \(M^{SCN}\) takes the constant value \(\beta\,\bar g^{-\sigma}\), giving the rate \(r^{*}=\bar g^{\sigma}/\beta-1\) used in \(P^{SCD}\) (SCN Proposition 6.2).

The housing-augmented demographic channel. Writing \(c_t \equiv Y_t/N_t\) for per-capita income, under the housing-augmented sustenance function (eq. 2) the demographic growth rate is

\[\frac{N_{t+1}}{N_t} = B e^{\eta_t}(1-s_t)^{\gamma}(c_t)^{\gamma} \tag{B.2}\]

so the kernel depends on the rent share:

\[M_{t+1}^{SCN} \propto \beta \left[B e^{\eta_t}(1-s_t)^{\gamma}(c_t)^{\gamma}\right]^{-\sigma} \tag{B.3}\]

The demographic-hedge covariance. When \(s_t\) rises (rents increase, or the benefit is frozen), \((N_{t+1}/N_t)^{-\sigma}\) rises (the kernel increases) while the BTL return \(R_{h,t+1}\) also rises, so along the rent-share channel, holding per-capita income \(c_t\) fixed,

\[\text{Cov}\!\left(M_{t+1}^{SCN},\, R_{h,t+1}\right) > 0 \tag{B.4}\]

A counteracting income channel runs the other way — high \(c_t\) raises both demographic growth and housing returns, generating the standard negative CCAPM covariance — so the net sign is calibration-dependent and is not load-bearing for any result in the body. Along the rent-share channel a positive covariance implies a negative risk premium, \(E[R_h]^{SCN}-R_f<0\): BTL housing acts as a demographic hedge, paying off precisely when demographic outcomes are poor (high \(s_t\) → high \(R_h\) → high \(M^{SCN}\)). The hedge carries a social cost — high \(R_h\) is itself the cause of poor demographics — and is the risk-character counterpart of the normative overvaluation of §4.3. The overvaluation discount \(\Delta P\) (eq. 11) is, by contrast, a capitalisation gap that does not depend on this covariance.
