Reproductive Capacity as Numéraire

Carlos Galindo Escajeda May 2026

Preliminary working paper — please do not cite, quote, or circulate without the author’s permission.

Abstract

This foundational paper supplies the welfare grammar the rest of the programme draws on, and aligns it with NEF’s commitment to an economy organised around people rather than asset wealth. Mainstream macro-finance evaluates outcomes by subjective utility and Pareto efficiency — standards under which market-generated inequality can register as “efficient.” This paper replaces that with Reproductive-Capacity Dominance (RCD): a criterion that ranks economic paths by the physical reproduction of the working population over time. Using the same dynamic-stochastic (Bellman) machinery mainstream economists use to argue for market efficiency, it shows that even under idealised, frictionless complete markets, competitive institutions can fail this criterion once initial capital is unequally held — and that a structural “demographic continuation” channel makes today’s misallocation compound into future deficits in the population’s capacity to reproduce itself. For NEF this is the heterodox anchor: a formal argument, conducted on orthodox economics’ own terms, that unregulated private finance can systematically erode human reproduction, and so calls for exactly the structural, redistributive interventions NEF advances. Its risk-premium theorem is presented as a conditional second-order local perturbation result, not a global exact claim. The paper is explicit that RCD is a normative choice, openly defended against the utilitarian default rather than smuggled in as neutral, and that its results hold within a stationary modelling frame — the honest boundary of an immanent critique.

JEL Classification: C61, D31, D50, E11, E13, G12

Keywords: reproductive-capacity dynamics, structural macro-finance, demographic continuation term, stochastic discount factor, Reproductive-Capacity Dominance, structural asset pricing, structural growth and finance

1. Introduction

Conventional economic theory takes subjective utility as its primitive. This paper takes a different ontological starting point. We posit that society’s proper objective is the maximization of an objective physical index of reproductive capacity — denoted \(N\) — governed by a reproduction technology with constant returns to scale:

\[ N_{t+1} = B e^{\eta_t} N_t^{1-\gamma} C_t^\gamma. \]

Throughout, \(C_t\) is interpreted as reproduction-effective goods and services input (rather than raw expenditure units), so the CRS map above is read as a physical transformation rule in effective units.

Reproductive capacity is the fundamental numéraire in which all economic values must ultimately be expressed. This numéraire is not an arbitrary welfare metric: it is the formal expression of the claim that the intra-temporal reproduction of the working population and its inter-temporal comparability are jointly constitutive of value, money, and capital. This joint necessity entails that asset valuation must be derived from the same reproductive process rather than appended to it as an external financial convenience.1

This paper’s approach is distinct from prior structural asset-pricing attempts — the asset-market and user-cost capitalisation of housing (Poterba 1984), dynamic housing-macro models (Piazzesi and Schneider 2016), and the long-run asset-returns literature (Jordà et al. 2019) — which treat asset prices and rent as capitalised claims on future income but operate without a formal dynamic model anchored in population reproduction. The most direct antecedent (Huato, forthcoming) advances this anchoring informally; the present paper’s contribution is to derive it. Its novelty lies in deriving asset prices as direct outputs of the structural Bellman problem — not as external accounting identities — as established in Appendix A.2, placing the reproduction of the working population, and the institutions that govern it, at the foundations of asset pricing. The demographic continuation term \((1-\gamma)\) has no counterpart in prior accounts.

The reproductive-capacity primitive also locates the framework within the classical and heterodox tradition of value theory, where value has long been anchored in the conditions of reproduction rather than in subjective preference. The classical surplus approach — from Quesnay’s produit net (1758) through Ricardo to its modern reconstruction by Sraffa (1960) and Garegnani (1984) — takes the physical surplus over the reproduction requirements of the system as the object of distribution. The reproduction technology used here — in which labour-power is itself a produced magnitude, reproduced from a subsistence input — stands in the lineage of the von Neumann (1945) expanding-economy model, where commodities, labour among them, are produced from commodities; that is the formal ancestor of the present primitive. Marx, in turn, grounds the value of labour-power in the cost of reproducing it (Marx 1867, ch. 6), making the reproduction of the labouring population the material condition presupposed by every capitalist magnitude. The present framework shares this orientation — reproduction as the anchor of value — while differing in its primitive: it takes society’s reproductive capacity, a physical magnitude, not socially necessary labour-time, as the numéraire of the normative (RCD) criterion — the decentralised market still prices in output units, so reproductive capacity is the unit of account of the value-theoretic criterion, not of the competitive equilibrium. Its contribution to that tradition is neither a restatement nor a critique but a formalisation: it renders reproductive capacity an explicit dynamic-stochastic numéraire and derives a pricing kernel and a welfare criterion from it. The factor-pricing remains marginalist by deliberate design — competitive firms paying marginal products — because the strategy is one of immanence: the orthodox apparatus is turned to show that competitive institutions violate a reproduction-based criterion on their own terrain. A caveat is owed to the surplus tradition: the lineage claimed here is to its reproduction-anchored conception of value, not to Sraffa’s Standard commodity as a measure — the latter is a multi-sector construct with no role in this one-sector model. The aggregate-capital production function is the orthodox apparatus the immanent strategy deliberately adopts, so that the violation of the reproduction criterion is demonstrated on competitive institutions’ own terms; the paper neither enters nor claims to resolve the Cambridge-capital controversy, and makes no claim to be a multi-sector surplus model.2 The framework is thus heterodox in its primitive, orthodox in its method.

Although the paper is theoretical, it already yields a concrete empirical restriction. The effective risk price is structurally constrained — not freely calibrated — because the cross-equation relation \((1-\gamma)\Omega = -\psi_x\xi_\theta/(\gamma\varphi_\theta)\) ties it to reproduction elasticity, the capital-ratio savings response, the normalized-consumption response coefficient, and Bellman-curvature objects. The natural empirical follow-up is therefore not free calibration but testing that restriction against observable profit rates, risk-free rates, wage shares, accumulation paths, cohort fertility data, investment-share dynamics, and the response of normalized consumption to identified technology shocks.

Prior formalizations of this structural framework have incorporated three internally contradictory devices:

  • Stochastic formulations that drop the \(N_t^{1-\gamma}\) term, violating constant returns to scale.
  • Welfare claims based on Pareto efficiency and the first welfare theorem.
  • Comparative statics imported from subjective utility models rather than derived from physical \(N\)-variance.

The first contradiction has the deepest structural consequences: without \(N_t^{1-\gamma}\), joint CRS fails, the value function admits no exact normalization, the Bellman perturbation yields no closed-form risk premium, and the RCD violation has no structural coefficient to sign-identify. These contradictions are terminal. This paper resolves them by correcting the model’s auxiliary structure. The formalization satisfies three requirements:

  1. Full consistency with the CRS reproduction technology, including the demographic continuation term.
  2. Internal derivation of all asset pricing results.
  3. Replacement of Pareto efficiency with Reproductive-Capacity Dominance.

This paper develops a structural macro-finance framework centered on the reproduction of the working population. Its formal strategy runs from the competitive equilibrium side: rather than starting from a planner’s benchmark and measuring distortions from the optimum, it asks what the competitive equilibrium proves about itself from its own first-order conditions. It opens a broader research agenda on growth, finance, and the reproduction of the working population without claiming to settle the full historical questions usually associated with unified growth theory in a single paper.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 develops the structural stochastic baseline — the Bellman problem with \(N\) as the primitive objective and the constant-returns reproduction technology \(N_{t+1} = Be^{\eta_t}N_t^{1-\gamma}C_t^\gamma\) — and derives the structural Euler equation with the demographic continuation term \((1-\gamma)\) explicit, together with the stochastic discount factor \(m_{t+1} = \beta\lambda_{t+1}/\lambda_t\). The closed-form reduction of the Euler equation and the derivation of \(\Omega\) are the subject of Appendix A.2. Section 3 introduces Reproductive-Capacity Dominance: allocations are ranked directly over discounted dynasty-level \(N\)-paths, and Proposition 3.1 establishes that initial capital inequality generates persistent violations, compounded by \((1-\gamma)\). Section 4 states Theorem 4.1 as a conditional second-order local perturbation result for the structural risk premium, with the demographic coefficient \(\Omega(\sigma,\gamma,\beta)\) sign-identified under two standard curvature and savings-rate conditions. Section 5 examines RCD violations under autarky, credit markets, and labor markets. Section 6 introduces incomplete markets and the precautionary saving channel through which idiosyncratic risk deepens reproductive deficits; it also shows how the derived SDF prices a yield hierarchy in which wealth takes the form of capitalised claims within the model’s asset span — the portfolio generalization across heterogeneous asset classes and endogenous credit is deferred to future work. Appendix A.2 applies the Schmitt-Grohé/Uribe second-order perturbation to derive Theorem 4.1.

2. The Structural Stochastic Baseline

Production and reproduction are subject to multiplicative shocks:

\[ Y_t = A e^{\theta_t} N_t^{1-\alpha} K_t^\alpha, \quad N_{t+1} = B e^{\eta_t} N_t^{1-\gamma} C_t^\gamma. \]

The capital accumulation equation is \(K_{t+1} = Y_t - C_t + (1-\delta)K_t\). The Bellman equation is:

\[ V(N_t, K_t) = \max_{C_t} \Bigl\{ u(N_t) + \beta \mathbb{E}_t \bigl[ V(N_{t+1}, K_{t+1}) \bigr] \Bigr\}. \]

Timing convention: The shocks \((\eta_t, \theta_t)\) are realized at the beginning of period \(t\) and are part of the period-\(t\) information set \(\mathcal{F}_t\), before the consumption choice \(C_t\) is made. Consequently \(N_{t+1}\) and \(K_{t+1}\) are \(\mathcal{F}_t\)-measurable, as is \(V_K(N_{t+1},K_{t+1})\). The shadow value \(\lambda_t \equiv \beta\mathbb{E}_t[V_K(N_{t+1},K_{t+1})] = \beta V_K(N_{t+1},K_{t+1})\) is therefore \(\mathcal{F}_t\)-measurable — it is known at \(t\). By contrast, \(\lambda_{t+1} \equiv \beta V_K(N_{t+2},K_{t+2})\) is \(\mathcal{F}_{t+1}\)-measurable and genuinely uncertain at \(t\), because \(N_{t+2}\) and \(K_{t+2}\) depend on \((\eta_{t+1},\theta_{t+1}) \notin \mathcal{F}_t\) through the next-period reproduction and capital laws of motion. The SDF \(m_{t+1} = \beta\lambda_{t+1}/\lambda_t = \beta V_K(N_{t+2},K_{t+2})/V_K(N_{t+1},K_{t+1})\) is therefore \(\mathcal{F}_{t+1}\)-measurable and genuinely stochastic from the time-\(t\) perspective, making non-zero covariance with risky returns possible. The value function \(V(N_t,K_t)\) denotes the Bellman value after absorbing period-\(t\) shocks; the dependence on \((\eta_t,\theta_t)\) is implicit.

We specify \(u(N) = \frac{N^{1-\sigma}}{1-\sigma}\) with \(\sigma > 0\). The functional form is formally identical to standard CRRA utility; the structural departure does not lie in the apparatus but in two specific places: (i) the state variable is the physical reproductive stock \(N\) rather than subjective consumption \(C\), which alters the policy function and produces distinct empirical implications for asset prices; and (ii) the demographic continuation term \((1-\gamma)\) appears in the Euler equation — a term with no counterpart in any standard Ramsey formulation — through which reproductive persistence alters risk premia. The parameter \(\sigma\) governs the planner’s attitude toward variance in aggregate reproductive capacity.3

The Bellman flow payoff \(u(N_t)\) is defined over the current-period reproductive stock \(N_t\) — the realized population at the start of period \(t\), determined by last period’s choice of \(C_{t-1}\). This timing convention reflects the interpretation that society derives period-\(t\) productive capacity from the existing population \(N_t\); the current choice \(C_t\) governs next period’s reproductive level \(N_{t+1}\).

First-Order Condition and Envelope Conditions

The first-order condition with respect to consumption \(C_t\) defines the shadow value of capital \(\lambda_t\):

\[ \lambda_t \equiv \beta \mathbb{E}_t [ V_K(N_{t+1}, K_{t+1}) ] = \beta \mathbb{E}_t \Bigl[ V_N(N_{t+1}, K_{t+1}) \frac{\partial N_{t+1}}{\partial C_t} \Bigr] = \beta \mathbb{E}_t \Bigl[ V_N(N_{t+1}, K_{t+1}) \cdot \gamma \frac{N_{t+1}}{C_t} \Bigr]. \]

Although \(\lambda_t\) is the capital costate, its dynamics are governed by the reproduction problem — through the \(V_N\) envelope below and the dependence of \(V_K\) on the reproductive state \(N_t\) — so the kernel \(m_{t+1}=\beta\lambda_{t+1}/\lambda_t\) carries the \((1-\gamma)\) reproduction recursion that Theorem 4.1 isolates as \((1-\gamma)\Omega\).

The envelope condition for capital \(K_t\) isolates the marginal product of capital, which is independent of demographic terms:

\[ V_K(N_t, K_t) = \beta \mathbb{E}_t \Bigl[ V_K(N_{t+1}, K_{t+1}) \Bigl( \alpha \frac{Y_t}{K_t} + 1 - \delta \Bigr) \Bigr] = \lambda_t R_{K,t}. \]

The envelope condition for reproductive capacity \(N_t\) accounts for both its role in production (wage \(w_t\)) and reproduction:

\[ V_N(N_t, K_t) = u'(N_t) + \lambda_t w_t + \beta \mathbb{E}_t \Bigl[ V_N(N_{t+1}, K_{t+1}) (1-\gamma) \frac{N_{t+1}}{N_t} \Bigr]. \]

To resolve dimensional consistency across the two state variables, note that \(w_t\) is the physical marginal product of labor (goods per unit of \(N\)). The shadow-price-deflated real wage — the marginal rate at which the planner can trade capital accumulation for reproductive capacity at the margin, scaled by the physical wage — is therefore \(w_t^* = w_t \cdot \frac{\lambda_t}{u'(N_t)}\).

The Structural Euler Equation (open form)

Iterating the \(V_N\) envelope condition one period forward and substituting it into the first-order condition for \(C_t\) yields the following necessary condition:

\[ \lambda_t = \beta \mathbb{E}_t \Biggl[ \gamma \frac{N_{t+1}}{C_t} \Bigl( u'(N_{t+1}) + \lambda_{t+1} w_{t+1} + \beta \mathbb{E}_{t+1} \Bigl[ V_N(N_{t+2}, K_{t+2}) \cdot (1-\gamma) \frac{N_{t+2}}{N_{t+1}} \Bigr] \Bigr) \Biggr]. \]

This structural condition is open-form: \(V_N\) appears on the right-hand side and generates an infinite-horizon recursion whose closed-form reduction is established in Appendix A.2 (through the homogeneous value function \(V = N^{1-\sigma}\Psi(K/N)\) and the Schmitt-Grohé/Uribe second-order perturbation). The closed-form Euler equation and derived risk premium are established in Theorem 4.1 and Appendix A.2. The final term \(\beta\mathbb{E}_{t+1}[\dots]\) is the demographic continuation term — it generates a structural channel through which capital misallocation today compounds non-linearly into future reproductive-capacity deficits. Unlike the dynastic-altruism terms in Barro-Becker endogenous-fertility models, it cannot be calibrated away: it is a consequence of the CRS reproductive technology parameter \(\gamma\), not of parental preference. The positive reproduction elasticity \(\gamma\) is a physical parameter governing the aggregate resource intensity of population reproduction under constant returns, not a reduced-form fertility preference parameter; observed income-fertility gradients therefore lie outside the model’s specified domain. Its magnitude is pinned by the same technology that governs \(\Omega\) in Theorem 4.1.

Stochastic Discount Factor

\[ m_{t+1} = \beta \frac{\lambda_{t+1}}{\lambda_t}. \]

This expression follows from the definition \(\lambda_t \equiv \beta\mathbb{E}_t[V_K(N_{t+1},K_{t+1})]\) and the standard pricing kernel \(\mathbb{E}_t[m_{t+1}R_{t+1}]=1\); the closed-form expression for \(m_{t+1}\) in terms of observables is established in Appendix A.2. The derivation is not merely a formality: it reveals that the SDF innovation contains a demographic continuation channel — the \(\psi_x\hat{x}^{\text{innov}}_{t+2}\) term derived in Appendix A.2 — that carries the \((1-\gamma)\) reproductive persistence into the pricing kernel and is entirely absent from any a priori declared SDF form; this channel is the structural basis of Theorem 4.1’s departure from standard CCAPM.4

3. Reproductive-Capacity Dominance — A Native Welfare Criterion

Institutionally, the criterion developed below is meant to capture the standpoint of a social planner concerned with sustaining the reproduction of the working population across dynasties. From that standpoint, variance matters insofar as it lowers expected reproductive capacity or entrenches group-differentiated deficits, not as an independent source of disutility.

Definition (Reproductive-Capacity Dominance)
Allocation \(A\) Reproductive-Capacity Dominates allocation \(B\) under a discounted variant of the Ramsey-Weizsäcker overtaking criterion — replacing undiscounted partial sums with \(\beta^t\)-weighted partial sums — if (i) for every dynasty \(i\), \(\liminf_{T \to \infty} \mathbb{E}_0\left[\sum_{t=0}^T \beta^t (N_{i,t}^A - N_{i,t}^B)\right] \ge 0\), and (ii) for at least one dynasty the inequality is strictly positive. The criterion is adopted precisely because \(N_t\) grows along the equilibrium path and the discounted absolute sum may not converge; the \(\liminf\) is taken in the extended reals, and no spectral bound is needed for the comparisons used here, where the difference sequence \(N^A_{i,t}-N^B_{i,t}\) is eventually sign-definite (Propositions 3.1, 5.1) so the dominance verdict is fixed by the sign of the leading terms whether or not \(\beta\bar g \lessgtr 1\). (A bound \(\beta\bar g<1\) would in addition make every \(\beta^t\)-weighted absolute sum finite, extending the partial order to the unrestricted class; it is not required for the results below.) The Bellman transversality condition \(\lim_{T\to\infty}\beta^T \mathbb{E}[V_K K_T] = 0\) is a separate requirement on the optimum.

Remark (RCD structure and Paretian comparison). The classical Ramsey-Weizsäcker criterion uses undiscounted partial sums; the \(\beta^t\)-weighting here preserves the overtaking structure — ranking by liminf of finite partial sums without requiring global convergence — while maintaining commensurability with the dynastic Bellman objective. The discounted overtaking criterion is a partial order: it is not transitive in general; transitivity holds on the restricted class of paths where the liminf converges, which is the relevant case for bounded stochastic equilibria. The axiomatics of RCD relative to Paretian and utilitarian orderings is deferred to future work. As a preliminary comparison: RCD is structurally linear (risk-neutral) in expected \(N_{i,t}\), whereas a standard Paretian objective over dynasty welfare is concave (risk-averse) in \(N_{i,t}\) for \(\sigma > 0\). A mean-preserving spread over \(N\)-paths therefore leaves RCD indifferent while strictly degrading Paretian welfare; RCD can rank an allocation as dominant that a Paretian welfare function would reject, and vice versa. This divergence is a feature: RCD evaluates the expected physical reproductive capacity of each dynasty directly, without penalizing variance in \(N_t\) as such, precisely because reproductive capacity is treated as an objective quantity rather than a utility-bearing argument. The criterion measures competitive failure on the market’s own terrain — the reproduction of the working population — which is precisely the terrain on which the market yield hierarchy implicitly evaluates reproductive outcomes. That this terrain excludes ecological throughput, care-work capacity, and non-wage reproductive dimensions is a precision, not a limitation: a broader structural welfare criterion incorporating those dimensions is the subject of future work, but it would require a different formal object; the present analysis is complete within its deliberately scoped domain.

Dynasty-extension model. Propositions 3.1, 5.1, and 6.1 apply to a model extension in which a finite set of dynasties \(i \in \{1, \ldots, I\}\) share the production and reproduction technology with identical parameters but heterogeneous initial capital endowments \(K_{i,0}\). Each dynasty \(i\) independently maximizes \(\mathbb{E}_0\left[\sum_{t=0}^\infty \beta^t \frac{N_{i,t}^{1-\sigma}}{1-\sigma}\right]\) taking aggregate prices as given. For the price-taking assumption to be internally consistent under persistent \(x_i\)-heterogeneity, all dynasty propositions are understood in the large-\(I\) continuum limit in which each dynasty is atomistic (\(N_{i,t}/\sum_j N_{j,t}\to 0\)) and individual decisions have no measurable effect on the aggregate rental rate \(R_{K,t}\) or wage \(w_t\); finite-\(I\) general-equilibrium feedback from persistent \(N_i\)-gaps on aggregate factor prices is left for future work. A further implication of the atomistic closure is that as \(N\)-ratios diverge multiplicatively under Proposition 3.1, the wealthiest dynasties eventually command non-atomistic shares of the aggregate population — at which point the yield hierarchy becomes strategic rather than parametric, a structural result that constitutes, within DSGE syntax, a formal derivation of an endogenous concentration tendency: competitive institutions do not merely permit but necessitate the erosion of their own behavioral foundation. The strategic analysis of the resulting non-atomistic yield hierarchy — where the wealthiest dynasties hold pricing power over factor markets — is the subject of the finite-\(I\) extension. Production is organized by a competitive representative firm that hires the aggregate factor supplies \(N_t = \sum_i N_{i,t}\) and \(K_t = \sum_i K_{i,t}\), so aggregate output is \(Y_t = Ae^{\theta_t}N_t^{1-\alpha}K_t^\alpha\) and dynasties are factor suppliers rather than dynasty-specific production units. Competitive factor markets clear at: \(w_t = (1-\alpha)Y_t/N_t\) (wage equals marginal product of labour) and \(R_{K,t} = \alpha Y_t/K_t + 1 - \delta\) (rental rate equals marginal product of capital). The aggregate resource constraint is \(\sum_i C_{i,t} + K_{t+1} = Y_t + (1-\delta)K_t\). Under complete markets, this competitive equilibrium coincides with the social planner’s optimum by the first welfare theorem applied to the \(N\)-primitive objective (the theorem requires only competitive prices and convex feasibility sets, which are satisfied here); the RCD violations established below therefore arise within the space of Pareto-efficient competitive equilibria — comparing unequal-endowment and equal-endowment CEs that the Paretian ordering cannot rank, so RCD supplies the discriminating criterion. In the complete-markets benchmark, dynasties trade the full set of state-contingent claims for idiosyncratic shocks \(\eta_{i,t}\); this is the maintained asset span for Propositions 3.1 and 5.1 unless otherwise stated. Dynasties do not trade claims on aggregate shocks \((\theta_t, \eta_t)\); aggregate risk is borne through the common rental rate \(R_{K,t}\) and is not diversifiable at the dynasty level — this rules out GE price externalities from aggregate-shock insurance that could otherwise dampen or reverse the \((1-\gamma)\) persistence channel. Incomplete markets (6) must specify the available asset span explicitly.

Definition (Competitive Equilibrium). A competitive equilibrium is a sequence of prices \(\{w_t, R_{K,t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}\) and allocations \(\{C_{i,t}, K_{i,t+1}\}_{i,t}\) such that: (i) each dynasty \(i\) solves its individual Bellman problem taking prices as given; (ii) factor markets clear: \(w_t = (1-\alpha)Y_t/N_t\) and \(R_{K,t} = \alpha Y_t/K_t + 1-\delta\); (iii) the aggregate resource constraint holds: \(\sum_i C_{i,t} + K_{t+1} = Y_t + (1-\delta)K_t\); and (iv) the transversality condition \(\lim_{T\to\infty}\beta^T\mathbb{E}[V_K(N_{i,T},K_{i,T})\,K_{i,T}] = 0\) holds for each dynasty \(i\).

Assumption (A3) (Monotone Policy Function): The optimal normalized consumption policy \(\tilde{c}(x, z)\) of each dynasty is strictly increasing in the own normalized capital ratio \(x = K/N\) for all aggregate state \(z\). The exact sufficient condition on the marginal propensity to consume — derived by requiring \(\partial h/\partial x > 0\) directly from the normalized capital law of motion \(h = (w+R_K x - \tilde{c})/(Be^\eta\tilde{c}^\gamma)\) via the quotient rule — is: \[\tilde{c}_x < \frac{\tilde{c}\,R_{K,t}}{\tilde{c} + \gamma(w_t + R_{K,t}x - \tilde{c})}\] at the competitive equilibrium. (This bound is tighter than the naive \(\tilde{c}_x < R_{K,t}\) because the denominator \(Be^\eta\tilde{c}^\gamma\) also increases in \(x\) through \(\tilde{c}\); the \(\gamma\) factor on the savings term \(w+R_K x-\tilde{c} > 0\) is the demographic dilution term absent from standard RCK normalizations. Assumption (A3) is standard in heterogeneous-agent RBC models; the monotone-policy property holds under analogous conditions in Stokey, Lucas, and Prescott (1989, Propositions 9.8–9.10) for the standard stochastic growth model and Aiyagari (1994, Lemma 1) for the precautionary-savings setting. A3 is maintained here as a standing assumption given the strict concavity of \(u(N)\) and the CRS technology.)

Proposition 3.1
In a complete-markets economy with idiosyncratic-shock insurance, the competitive equilibrium satisfies Reproductive-Capacity Dominance with respect to idiosyncratic risk. However, initial capital inequality violates Reproductive-Capacity Dominance with respect to the distribution of reproductive capacity. This violation exhibits greater persistence due to the demographic continuation term \((1-\gamma)\).

Proof (conditional on Assumption A3). Three steps.

Step (i) — Monotone capital-ratio ordering. Both dynasties share the aggregate state \(z_t = (\theta_t, \eta_t)\) and the competitive equilibrium policy function \(\tilde{c}(\cdot, z_t)\). Under competitive factor markets, dynasty \(i\) receives the common wage and rental income schedule induced by the representative firm, so the normalized capital law of motion is: \[x_{i,t+1} = h(x_{i,t}, z_t) \;\equiv\; \frac{w_t + R_{K,t}x_{i,t} \;-\; \tilde{c}(x_{i,t},z_t)}{Be^{\eta_t}\,\tilde{c}(x_{i,t},z_t)^\gamma}.\] Under (A3), differentiating \(h = N(x)/D(x)\) with \(N = w_t + R_{K,t}x - \tilde{c}\) and \(D = Be^{\eta_t}\tilde{c}^\gamma\) gives \(h_x = (R_{K,t}-\tilde{c}_x)/D - h\gamma\tilde{c}_x/\tilde{c}\); the A3 condition \(\tilde{c}_x < \tilde{c}R_{K,t}/[\tilde{c}+\gamma(w_t+R_{K,t}x-\tilde{c})]\) is precisely the condition that makes \(h_x > 0\). Consequently \(h(\cdot,z_t)\) is strictly increasing in \(x\) for all \(z_t\). By induction: \(x_{1,0} > x_{2,0} \Rightarrow x_{1,t} > x_{2,t}\) a.s. for all \(t \ge 0\).

Step (ii) — Strict reproductive-growth ordering. With idiosyncratic shocks \(\eta_{i,t}\) fully insured, dynasty \(i\)’s aggregate reproduction growth factor is \(g_{i,t} = Be^{\eta_t}\tilde{c}(x_{i,t},z_t)^\gamma\) (common aggregate shock \(\eta_t\), dynasty-specific \(\tilde{c}\)). Since \(\tilde{c}(x_{1,t},z_t) > \tilde{c}(x_{2,t},z_t)\) from Step (i) and \(\gamma > 0\): \(g_{1,t} > g_{2,t}\) a.s. for all \(t \ge 1\). With \(N_{1,0} = N_{2,0}\), the reproduction ratio \(N_{1,t}/N_{2,t} = \prod_{s=0}^{t-1}g_{1,s}/g_{2,s} > 1\) and is strictly increasing in \(t\).

Step (iii) — RCD violation via liminf. Compare allocation A (unequal: \(K_{1,0} > K_{2,0}\), common \(N_0\)) to allocation B (equal-endowment counterfactual: \(K_{i,0} = \bar{K}\), same \(N_0\)), where in the large-\(I\) atomistic closure \(\bar{K} \equiv \frac{1}{I}\sum_{i=1}^I K_{i,0}\) (equal per-dynasty share of aggregate initial capital; in the two-dynasty illustration this reduces to \((K_{1,0}+K_{2,0})/2\)), so aggregate initial capital is identical across the two allocations and \(\bar{x}_0 > x_{2,0}^A\). By Step (i), \(\bar{x}_t > x_{2,t}^A\) a.s. for all \(t \ge 0\); by Step (ii), \(\bar{g}_t > g_{2,t}^A\) a.s. for all \(t \ge 1\). The discounted cumulative \(N\)-difference for dynasty 2 is: \[S_T^2 \;=\; \sum_{t=0}^T \beta^t\bigl(N_{2,t}^A - N_{2,t}^B\bigr) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^T \beta^t N_0 \Biggl(\prod_{s=0}^{t-1} g_{2,s}^A - \prod_{s=0}^{t-1} \bar{g}_s\Biggr).\] Each term in the sum is strictly negative (since \(\prod_{s=0}^{t-1} g_{2,s}^A < \prod_{s=0}^{t-1} \bar{g}_s\) by the strict growth-rate ordering from Step (ii)). Therefore \(S_T^2 < 0\) for all \(T \ge 1\); since each finite partial sum is strictly negative a.s., linearity of expectation also gives \(\mathbb{E}_0[S_T^2] < 0\) for every finite \(T \ge 1\). Hence \(\liminf_{T\to\infty} S_T^2 \le S_1^2 = \beta(g_{2,0}^A - \bar{g}_0)N_0 < 0\): allocation A does not RCD-dominate B for dynasty 2. The \((1-\gamma)\) amplification holds because the reproduction technology \(N_{i,t+1} = Be^{\eta_t}N_{i,t}^{1-\gamma}C_{i,t}^\gamma\) exhibits N-level persistence proportional to \((1-\gamma)\): higher \((1-\gamma)\) preserves existing \(N\)-ratio gaps between dynasties more strongly each period — through the \(N_{i,t}^{1-\gamma}\) elasticity — compounding the strictly negative N-level disadvantage multiplicatively across periods. \(\square\)

4. Internal Derivation of Interest Rates and Risk Premia

The structural risk premium emerges from the stochastic discount factor \(m_{t+1} = \beta\lambda_{t+1}/\lambda_t\) once the second-order Schmitt-Grohé/Uribe perturbation described in Appendix A.2 is applied. A first-order log-linearization is insufficient: at first order the SDF carries zero conditional variance and all covariance risk premia vanish (see Appendix A.2 for the argument). As derived in Appendix A.2, the risk premium is stated as Theorem 4.1 below. Let lower-case variables denote log deviations.

Intuition. A stochastic discount factor is the pricing kernel that equates today’s asset price to tomorrow’s expected payoff adjusted for macroeconomic risk. In standard CCAPM, this kernel depends on aggregate consumption growth: assets that pay off poorly when consumption is low earn a risk premium. The structural SDF derived from \(m_{t+1} = \beta\lambda_{t+1}/\lambda_t\) adds a second risk channel: because \(\lambda_t\) is the Bellman shadow value of capital and the consumption first-order condition equates it to the marginal value of reproductive capacity weighted by the reproduction elasticity (\(\lambda_t = \beta\mathbb{E}_t[V_N\cdot\gamma N_{t+1}/C_t]\)), the pricing kernel inherits demographic risk — through the reproduction-coupled dynamics of \(V_K\) — as well as consumption risk. An asset that pays off poorly precisely when society’s reproductive capacity contracts earns an additional risk premium beyond what CCAPM would assign. This is the \((1-\gamma)\Omega\) term.

The key structural feature is that \((1-\gamma)\Omega\) is not a free parameter. It is pinned by the cross-equation restriction \((1-\gamma)\Omega = -\psi_x\xi_\theta/(\gamma\varphi_\theta)\): the same CRS technology parameter \(\gamma\) that governs reproduction also constrains the effective risk price through Bellman curvature \(\psi_x\), the capital-ratio savings response \(\xi_\theta\), and the consumption response coefficient \(\varphi_\theta\). Standard CCAPM can match any observed risk premium by adjusting the risk-aversion parameter; the structural framework cannot — which is what makes Theorem 4.1 a testable restriction rather than a calibration.

Relation to the equity-premium-puzzle literature. The contrast with CCAPM is not incidental. The consumption-based kernel’s central empirical failure — the equity premium puzzle (Mehra and Prescott 1985): matching the observed premium requires implausibly high risk aversion — has been addressed by enriching the preference specification with additional free parameters: recursive utility that separates risk aversion from the elasticity of intertemporal substitution, freeing the risk-aversion parameter to be set independently (Epstein and Zin 1989; Weil 1989); long-run consumption risk (Bansal and Yaron 2004); and habit formation (Campbell and Cochrane 1999). In each, the added parameters are disciplined by asset-market moments — among them the premium they are meant to explain. The present framework differs in two respects. First, the state variable is reproductive capacity \(N\), not consumption: the kernel is built on a different primitive, not a richer preference over the same one. Second, the demographic risk channel \((1-\gamma)\Omega\) is derived — its sign and its \((1-\gamma)\) loading fall out of the reproduction technology through the cross-equation restriction above, rather than being a free parameter introduced to fit the premium. Its magnitude still inherits the objective’s curvature \(\sigma\), a calibrated parameter as in any such model (though not one dedicated to the premium); the honest claim is therefore over-identification of the channel’s structure — calibrating \(\gamma\) to reproduction moments fixes the channel’s sign and form, turning the premium into an over-identifying test of that structure rather than a target — not a parameter-free risk price. That derived structure is what a recalibrated CCAPM, which meets the premium by adding a free preference parameter, does not possess.

Theorem 4.1 (Conditional Structural Risk Premium) Conditional on Closures (i)–(v) in Step (0) of Appendix A.2, and on:
(A1) the normalized value function \(\Psi(x)\) satisfies \(\Psi''(\bar{x}) < 0\) (strict concavity, standard SLP Ch.~4); and
(A2) \(\xi_\theta = (\bar{y} - \bar{c}\varphi_\theta)/(\bar{g}\bar{x}) - \gamma\varphi_\theta > 0\) (positive net capital-ratio savings response exceeding demographic dilution: \((\bar{y}-\bar{c}\varphi_\theta)/(\bar{g}\bar{x}) > \gamma\varphi_\theta\)).
Then the equilibrium risk premium on productive capital satisfies, to second order in the SG&U perturbation: for \(\sigma \ge 1\),

\[ \mathbb{E}_t[R_{K,t+1}] - R_{f,t} \approx \sigma \gamma \cdot \operatorname{Cov}_t(\Delta\tilde{c}_{t+1}, R_{K,t+1}) + (1-\gamma) \cdot \Omega(\sigma, \gamma, \beta) \cdot \operatorname{Cov}_t(\Delta n_{t+2}, R_{K,t+1}). \]

This statement is local: it is valid in the SG&U second-order neighborhood of the normalized stochastic steady state under Closures (i)-(v) in Appendix A.2, and is not claimed as a global exact representation away from that neighborhood.

(Here \(\Delta\tilde{c}_{t+1} \equiv \Delta\log(C_{t+1}/N_{t+1})\) is the log-growth of normalized per-capita consumption; \(\Delta n_{t+2} \equiv \log(N_{t+2}/N_{t+1})\) is the log-growth of reproductive capacity in period \(t+1\), capturing the period-\((t+1)\) reproductive shock through the SDF innovation; and \(R_{f,t}\) is the gross risk-free return. The \(\sigma\gamma\) loading on \(\Delta\tilde{c}\) is the second-order analogue of the standard CCAPM consumption-risk term; the \((1-\gamma)\Omega\) loading on \(\Delta n_{t+2}\) is the demographic continuation channel with no counterpart in any Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans formulation; the demographic terms in Barro and Becker (1989) dynastic-altruism models arise from parental preference over child utility rather than from a physical CRS reproduction technology, and are structurally distinct.)

The demographic-risk coefficient is given in closed form by the A.2 derivation: \[\Omega = -\frac{\psi_x\,\xi_\theta}{(1-\gamma)\,\gamma\,\varphi_\theta} > 0,\] where \(\psi_x \equiv \bar{x}\Psi''(\bar{x})/\Psi'(\bar{x}) < 0\) (A1), \(\xi_\theta \equiv (\bar{y}-\bar{c}\varphi_\theta)/(\bar{g}\bar{x}) - \gamma\varphi_\theta > 0\) (A2), and \(\varphi_\theta > 0\) (consumption-smoothing response to technology shock). The \(-\gamma\varphi_\theta\) term in \(\xi_\theta\) is the demographic dilution channel: higher \(\gamma\) (more consumption-dependent reproduction) directly suppresses the capital-ratio savings response to technology shocks by increasing denominator growth \(\hat{g}_t = \eta_t + \gamma\hat{\tilde{c}}_t\) — a structural damping effect absent from all standard RCK normalizations where growth is exogenous. The sign \(\Omega > 0\) follows directly: numerator \(-\psi_x\xi_\theta\) is negative times positive $= $ positive; denominator \((1-\gamma)\gamma\varphi_\theta > 0\). The parametric boundary \(\sigma \ge 1\) appears in the \(\psi_x\) curvature through the SS Bellman equations and ensures the marginal disutility of reproductive variance is non-negligible relative to the intertemporal substitution motive; it also operates through the net capital-ratio savings response \(\xi_\theta\) (A2): for \(\sigma < 1\) the reproductive objective’s curvature can reduce the investment residual \((\bar{y} - \bar{c}\varphi_\theta)\) and potentially violate A2. We impose \(\sigma \ge 1\) as a primitive parametric condition sufficient for the steady-state conditions A1 and A2 on which Theorem 4.1’s local representation rests. The effective risk price \((1-\gamma)\Omega = -\psi_x\xi_\theta/(\gamma\varphi_\theta)\) is strictly decreasing in \(\gamma\) (taking the explicit \(\gamma\)-dependence of \(\xi_\theta\) into account, \(\partial[(1-\gamma)\Omega]/\partial\gamma = \psi_x/\gamma + \psi_x\xi_\theta/(\gamma^2\varphi_\theta) < 0\) under A1–A2), so the demographic channel’s contribution to the risk premium declines as reproduction becomes more consumption-dependent. Note: \(\Omega\) itself diverges as \(\gamma\to 1\) while \((1-\gamma)\to 0\); the product \((1-\gamma)\Omega\) converges to a finite positive constant \(-\psi_x\xi_\theta/\varphi_\theta > 0\), so the full premium does not vanish at \(\gamma=1\) instead the two channels merge as \(\Delta n_{t+2}\to\Delta\tilde{c}_{t+1}+\eta_{t+1}\) when \(\gamma=1\). More generally, under the maintained orthogonality of primitive shocks (\(\operatorname{Cov}_t(\eta_{t+1}, R_{K,t+1}) = 0\)), both covariance terms are proportional to the same scalar for any \(\gamma\in(0,1)\): \(\operatorname{Cov}_t(\Delta n_{t+2}, R_{K,t+1}) = \gamma\,\operatorname{Cov}_t(\Delta\tilde{c}_{t+1}, R_{K,t+1})\) (as shown in Appendix A.2). Substituting yields the equivalent single-factor form \(\gamma[\sigma + (1-\gamma)\Omega]\cdot\operatorname{Cov}_t(\Delta\tilde{c}_{t+1}, R_{K,t+1})\), where \(\gamma[\sigma+(1-\gamma)\Omega]\) is the total effective risk-aversion coefficient. The two-term presentation in Theorem 4.1 separates the consumption-risk interpretation from the structural augmentation; the augmentation \(\gamma(1-\gamma)\Omega > 0\) raises effective risk aversion above the standard \(\sigma\gamma\) level, establishing the structural risk premium as a structural reinterpretation of the CCAPM coefficient rather than an independent additive factor.

5. Competitive Institutions and Reproductive-Capacity Inequality

Each institutional arrangement alters the mechanism but not the fundamental result: under initial capital inequality, competitive factor markets cannot restore Reproductive-Capacity Dominance.

Autarky: Dynasties consume their own production. Reproductive-Capacity Dominance is violated due to unequal marginal products of capital.

Credit Markets: Equalize marginal products and raise total reproductive capacity, but the capital-rich dynasty receives interest income, widening absolute inequality in \(N\). Credit does not abolish hierarchy; it reorganizes it. Private ownership claims are priced as deviations from the yield on the safest asset, so that the capital-rich dynasty occupies the superior position in that hierarchy even where product-market efficiency rises. The widening of dynasty-level claims under credit and labour markets is therefore not a neutral financial redistribution but — in the present model’s terms — a differentiated command over the future reproduction of the working population.

Labor Markets: Equalize wages and per-capita consumption in steady state, but the capital-rich dynasty retains a transitional advantage. The demographic continuation term amplifies permanent divergence in dynasty-level reproductive-capacity trajectories.

Proposition 5.1
Private ownership satisfies Reproductive-Capacity Dominance with respect to idiosyncratic risk but violates it with respect to initial capital inequality. The violation grows with \((1-\gamma)\).

Proof (conditional on Assumption A3). The RCD violation under private ownership parallels Proposition 3.1 Steps (i)–(iii), with \(\tilde{c}(\cdot)\) now the competitive equilibrium policy under private ownership and with the same maintained complete-markets insurance against idiosyncratic shocks as in the dynasty benchmark above. The monotonicity argument from Proposition 3.1 Step (i) carries over under the competitive factor-income law of motion \(h(\cdot,z_t)\), so the \(x\)-gap remains preserved for all \(t\); because the idiosyncratic shocks remain fully insured in this proposition’s benchmark, the common-shock growth ordering \(g_{1,t} > g_{2,t}\) then follows exactly as in Proposition 3.1 Step (ii). Step (iii) therefore carries over to yield \(\liminf_T S_T^2 < 0\).

The additional gap that Proposition 3.1’s proof does not exclude — that competitive equilibrium dynamics might generate stochastic convergence in \(x_{i,t}\) across dynasties, potentially erasing the \(N\)-gap — does not block the RCD violation. Even if \(x_{1,t} - x_{2,t} \to 0\) asymptotically, the \(N\)-ratio \(N_{1,t}/N_{2,t} = \prod_{s=0}^{t-1}g_{1,s}/g_{2,s}\) accumulates all prior growth-rate differentials multiplicatively. Any finite window \([0,t^*]\) with \(g_{1,s} > g_{2,s}\) locks in \(N_{1,t}/N_{2,t} > 1\) for all \(t > t^*\), since subsequent equal growth rates preserve but cannot erase the accumulated ratio. The discounted \(N\)-difference \(S_T^2\) therefore remains strictly negative regardless of asymptotic \(x\)-convergence, and \(\liminf_T S_T^2 < 0\) holds. \(\square\)

6. Incomplete Markets, Precautionary Saving, and Capitalised Claims

Under incomplete markets, dynasties face uninsurable idiosyncratic shocks to physical reproduction and must self-insure.

Assumption 6.A (Jensen Dominance): The direct concavity penalty on \(\mathbb{E}[N_{t+1}]\) from uninsurable idiosyncratic variance strictly dominates the general-equilibrium increase in aggregate capital accumulation induced by precautionary saving. (Assumption 6.A is a primitive of this analysis. Its parametric boundary — the \((\gamma,\sigma)\)-region in which the Jensen concavity penalty strictly dominates the GE precautionary-savings offset — constitutes the central quantitative question for the empirical companion paper, which will derive that boundary from model parameters and test it against cohort fertility and asset-pricing data. Note: this dominance condition is not monotone in \(\sigma\). Higher \(\sigma\) strengthens both the Jensen penalty and the GE precautionary-savings offset — the latter because higher risk aversion raises the precautionary demand for capital accumulation, as in Aiyagari (1994). The \((\gamma,\sigma)\)-boundary at which Jensen dominance holds therefore depends on the relative magnitudes of these two opposing effects. The \((1-\gamma)\) demographic dilution term dampens the capital-ratio savings response (by the \(g_t(\tilde{c}_t)\)-denominator dilution derived for technology shocks in Appendix A.2 Step (2), which applies identically to the idiosyncratic-shock response), making the GE offset weaker than in a standard RCK model for any given \(\sigma\); this structural damping supports the dominance condition at moderate idiosyncratic variance, but it does not constitute a monotone implication from \(\sigma\) alone.)

Proposition 6.1 (conditional on Assumptions 6.A and A3)
Under Assumption 6.A, incomplete markets violate Reproductive-Capacity Dominance with respect to idiosyncratic risk. Total expected reproductive capacity is strictly lower than under the complete-markets benchmark. The demographic continuation term amplifies this inefficiency through non-linear compounding of reproductive deficits.

Status: this is a conditional result, not a free-standing theorem. Assumption 6.A supplies the sign: under incomplete markets the general-equilibrium precautionary-saving channel can in principle raise aggregate capital and wages (Aiyagari 1994), so the net effect on aggregate reproductive capacity is signed only under 6.A, whose \((\gamma,\sigma)\) region of validity is the open quantitative question stated above.

(Proof sketch: Without state-contingent claims, dynasties face uninsurable idiosyncratic variance. For admissible internal calibrations \(0 < \gamma < 1\), the reproduction function \(N_{t+1} = B e^{\eta_t} N_t^{1-\gamma} C_t^{\gamma}\) is strictly concave with respect to \(N_t\). With mean-zero shocks independent of aggregate variables, Jensen’s Inequality applied to the cross-dynasty distribution of \(N_{i,t}\) (which carries strictly positive variance under incomplete markets due to uninsured \(\eta_{i,t}\) shocks) gives \(\frac{1}{I}\sum_i N_{i,t+1} < f\!\left(\frac{1}{I}\sum_i N_{i,t}\right)\), using strict concavity of \(N_{t+1}\) in \(N_t\) with each dynasty’s \(C_{i,t}\) held at its individually optimal path. Under Assumption 6.A, this partial-equilibrium Jensen penalty is not offset by GE precautionary capital accumulation, and the RCD violation holds. (Note: the Jensen argument establishes a one-period reduction in \(\mathbb{E}[N_{t+1}]\); the infinite-horizon RCD conclusion — that the liminf of the discounted cumulative \(N\)-difference is strictly positive — now rests on the proof argument of Propositions 3.1 and 5.1. Under Assumption (A3), the monotone-mapping and liminf steps extend to the aggregate case, replacing the inter-dynasty comparison with the incomplete-markets vs. complete-markets benchmark comparison. The Jensen step establishes the one-period reduction; the infinite-horizon RCD claim holds conditionally under Assumptions (A3) and 6.A.)

Capitalised Claims

The derivation below supplies the formal risk-pricing foundation for capitalised claims within the Bellman apparatus — the specific gap that prior structural accounts identified but could not close without a dynamic stochastic model anchored in the conditions of population reproduction. The institutional dimensions (general-equilibrium credit closure, distributional consequences of the yield hierarchy, endogenous credit-money creation) constitute the terrain for future companion work; they extend and concretize the present formal result rather than replace it.5

(Note on terminology: “capitalised claims” here denotes an asset whose price capitalises a ranked structure of claims on future income through the conditions of reproduction. The mechanism in the present model is precautionary demand for insurance against reproductive-risk variance. The distributional consequences linking the bond to between-group reproduction require the heterogeneous-agent portfolio closure deferred to future work.)
The safe bond (“money of last resort”) is priced by the corrected SDF via \(\mathbb{E}_t[m_{t+1} R_{f,t}] = 1\). This safe return is the apex of a yield hierarchy: the bond approaches the yield on the safest asset, while every subordinate ownership claim deviates from that anchor in proportion to its specific risk premium. In the present model \(R_f = 1/\mathbb{E}_t[m_{t+1}]\) is determined endogenously by demographic risk \(\sigma_\eta^2\) and the planner’s curvature parameters \((\sigma,\gamma)\): a rise in \(\sigma_\eta^2\) lowers \(R_f\) — precautionary demand for the safe asset drives up bond prices and compresses the risk-free yield.6 That pricing relation clarifies the sense in which wealth appears as a capitalised claim: what is capitalised here is not an autonomous financial substance but a ranked structure of claims on future income, discounted through the conditions of population reproduction. The Bellman structure used here is a formal representation of that reproduction constraint — a dynamic state-space that makes the claim that finance is a command over future reproductive capacity mathematically tractable. Because \(m_{t+1}\) incorporates the demographic continuation term, uncertainty about future reproductive capacity raises the price of safety and depresses investment in real capital. The demographic SDF thus converts reproductive uncertainty into a systematic demand for the safe asset: finance appears here not as an autonomous monetary sphere but as the institutional form through which the risk of future reproductive-capacity deficits is priced and redistributed across dynasties. By capitalizing future income streams at the demographic SDF, the safe bond aggregates the cost of missing insurance markets. However, translating this aggregate-level crowding out into the specific reproduction of between-group inequality requires a fully specified heterogeneous-agent portfolio closure, which is deferred to future work.

Capitalising a growing income stream. The safe bond is the zero-growth, riskless special case of a more general object. The same kernel prices any claim to a growing, risky income stream — a rent, a wage bill, or a dividend — and reduces, at the deterministic steady state, to the constant-growth capitalisation form used throughout the asset-market and house-price literatures (Poterba 1984; Jordà et al. 2019), with the difference that its discount rate is here supplied by the reproduction problem rather than imported. Let a claim pay the income flow \(D_{t+1}\) next period, with the flow growing deterministically at its own rate \(g_D\) — claim-specific, so that for an idiosyncratic claim (a particular dwelling’s rent) it need not equal aggregate growth, while for an economy-wide aggregate it is pinned to \(\bar g - 1\); transversality requires \(r^{*}>g_D\) either way, with \(g_D\) the long-run deterministic trend, not a transitory realised rate — transitory deviations are priced through the covariance premium below, not through \(g_D\) — and trade at price \(P_t\); no-arbitrage under the structural SDF prices it by the Euler recursion \(P_t = \mathbb{E}_t[m_{t+1}(D_{t+1} + P_{t+1})]\), with \(m_{t+1} = \beta\lambda_{t+1}/\lambda_t\).

Proposition 6.2 (capitalisation reduced form). At the deterministic steady state the kernel takes the constant value \(m^{\mathrm{SS}}=\beta\,\bar{g}^{-\sigma}\equiv\tilde\beta\), where \(\bar{g}=N_{t+1}/N_t\) is the gross steady-state growth factor of reproductive capacity — since \(\lambda_t=\beta N_{t+1}^{-\sigma}\Psi'(\bar{x})\) gives \(\lambda_{t+1}/\lambda_t=\bar{g}^{-\sigma}\) at the balanced path. The price–flow recursion then has the unique no-bubble solution \(P^{*}_t = D_{t+1}/(r^{*}-g_D)\), with \(r^{*}\equiv 1/\tilde\beta - 1 = \bar{g}^{\sigma}/\beta - 1\), provided \(r^{*}>g_D\).

Proof. Under \(m_{t+1}=\tilde\beta\), forward iteration with the no-bubble condition \(\lim_{T}\tilde\beta^{T}P_{t+T}=0\) gives \(P^{*}_t=\sum_{j\ge 1}\tilde\beta^{j}D_{t+1}(1+g_D)^{j-1}=\tilde\beta D_{t+1}/[1-\tilde\beta(1+g_D)]\). Since \(1-\tilde\beta(1+g_D)=\tilde\beta(r^{*}-g_D)\), this equals \(D_{t+1}/(r^{*}-g_D)\); convergence requires \(\tilde\beta(1+g_D)<1\), i.e. \(r^{*}>g_D\). The rate is \(r^{*}=R_f-1\) for the steady-state safe rate \(R_f=1/m^{\mathrm{SS}}=\bar{g}^{\sigma}/\beta\) of the preceding paragraph, and collapses to \(1/\beta-1\) only under stationary reproduction \(\bar{g}=1\). \(\square\)

Away from the deterministic steady state the constant rate \(r^{*}\) is replaced by the claim’s expected gross return, which decomposes by the same kernel into the reproduction-priced safe rate and a covariance risk premium, \(\mathbb{E}_t[R_{t+1}] = R_{f,t} - R_{f,t}\operatorname{Cov}_t(m_{t+1},R_{t+1})\), with \(R_{f,t}=1/\mathbb{E}_t[m_{t+1}]\) the safe rate of the preceding paragraph, fixed by demographic risk \(\sigma_\eta^2\) and curvature \((\sigma,\gamma)\). For a claim whose return is orthogonal to the reproduction shock the premium takes the two-component form of Theorem 4.1, \(\sigma\gamma\,\operatorname{Cov}_t(\Delta\tilde{c}_{t+1},R_{t+1}) + (1-\gamma)\Omega\,\operatorname{Cov}_t(\Delta n_{t+2},R_{t+1})\); in general it carries the same demographic channel \((1-\gamma)\Omega\) alongside a direct reproduction-covariance term. Both the rate’s components are therefore outputs of the reproduction-grounded kernel. The constant-growth form \(P=D/(r-g)\) — read off market data in the empirical asset-price literature, and posited from time-preference in the marginalist theory of capital — is thus recovered as the steady-state reduction of a kernel anchored in the conditions under which the population reproduces. What is established is the form of the capitalisation rate and the source of its two components; the calibrated magnitude of the risk premium for any specific claim — housing in particular — inherits the heterogeneous-agent portfolio closure deferred above, and is not asserted here.

Proposition 6.3 (fundamental price and the market wedge). Let \(P^{*}=D/(r^{*}-g_D)\) be the fundamental price at the reproduction-grounded rate \(r^{*}\) and \(P^{\mathrm{mkt}}=D/(r^{\mathrm{mkt}}-g_D)\) the price at the rate the market applies. Then \(P^{\mathrm{mkt}}-P^{*}=D\,(r^{*}-r^{\mathrm{mkt}})/[(r^{\mathrm{mkt}}-g_D)(r^{*}-g_D)]\), which is strictly positive if and only if \(r^{\mathrm{mkt}}<r^{*}\) (both rates exceeding \(g_D\)).

Proof. Differencing the two capitalisations over a common denominator; the denominator is positive under the transversality conditions \(r^{\mathrm{mkt}},r^{*}>g_D\), so the gap takes the sign of \(r^{*}-r^{\mathrm{mkt}}\). \(\square\)

The fundamental price \(P^{*}\) values the claim as a command over the reproduction-grounded income it conveys. As a mathematical object the gap \(P^{\mathrm{mkt}}-P^{*}\) is simply the wedge between two capitalisation rates, positive when the market discounts at a lower rate than the reproduction-grounded one. It admits a classical interpretation — fictitious capital in the surplus-theoretic sense: a capitalised command over future reproduction in excess of the income the claim is a title to, sustained by financial conditions rather than by reproduction. That reading is an interpretation of the wedge, not an additional formal result. The reproduction-grounded rate accordingly does not forecast the market price and is not refuted by failing to track it — the divergence \(P^{*}\neq P^{\mathrm{mkt}}\) is the object the framework isolates, not an error in it.

7. Conclusion

By restoring constant returns to scale to the stochastic reproduction function, replacing the subjective utility metric with the objective physical index \(N\), and deriving asset prices as conditional closed-form outputs from the Euler equation, this paper establishes four structural results. First, the structural Bellman problem produces a stochastic discount factor with a demographic continuation term \((1-\gamma)\) absent from standard Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans formulations (and structurally distinct from the dynastic-altruism terms in Barro and Becker (1989), which arise from parental preference over child utility rather than from a physical CRS reproduction technology); this term is not a calibration device but a consequence of CRS reproductive technology. Second, Theorem 4.1 is established in Appendix A.2 as a conditional second-order SG&U risk-premium formula: \(E_t[R_{K,t+1}] - R_{f,t} \approx \sigma\gamma\cdot\operatorname{Cov}_t(\Delta\tilde{c}_{t+1}, R_{K,t+1}) + (1-\gamma)\Omega\cdot\operatorname{Cov}_t(\Delta n_{t+2}, R_{K,t+1})\), with structural risk-premium coefficient \(\Omega = -\psi_x\xi_\theta/[(1-\gamma)\gamma\varphi_\theta]\) whose sign is proved positive under A1 and A2. Crucially, under \(\sigma \ge 1\) the equivalent single-factor representation \(\gamma[\sigma+(1-\gamma)\Omega]\cdot\operatorname{Cov}_t(\Delta\tilde{c}_{t+1}, R_{K,t+1})\) makes visible what distinguishes this framework from standard consumption-CAPM (Cochrane 2005): the effective risk-aversion coefficient \(\gamma[\sigma+(1-\gamma)\Omega]\) is not freely chosen but structurally constrained by reproduction technology parameters \((\gamma, \psi_x, \xi_\theta, \varphi_\theta)\) through the cross-equation restriction \((1-\gamma)\Omega = -\psi_x\xi_\theta/(\gamma\varphi_\theta)\) — a testable constraint that standard CCAPM cannot generate. Third, Reproductive-Capacity Dominance supplies the welfare criterion under which competitive institutions generate structurally necessary violations when initial capital is unequally distributed (Proposition 3.1), when those gaps persist under a monotone policy function (Proposition 5.1), and when incomplete insurance markets amplify the Jensen penalty on aggregate reproductive capacity (Proposition 6.1). Fourth, the safe bond’s risk premium and the crowding-out of real investment are grounded in the same demographic SDF that prices the risky asset, closing the capitalised-claim argument structurally rather than interpretatively; the same kernel reduces, at the deterministic steady state, to the constant-growth capitalisation \(P=D/(r-g)\) (Propositions 6.2–6.3), with its discount rate supplied by the reproduction problem and any market price decomposable into a reproduction-grounded fundamental and a market wedge — a fictitious premium in the classical interpretation — whose sign is governed by the gap between the market rate and the reproduction-grounded one.

The primary empirical frontier opened by this framework is the structural identification of the effective risk price \((1-\gamma)\Omega\), from which the demographic coefficient \(\Omega\) can be extracted. The structural SDF uses the normalized consumption measure \(\Delta\tilde{c}_{t+1} = \Delta(C/N)_{t+1}\) — consumption per unit of reproductive capacity — rather than standard aggregate consumption per capita; these differ when demographic composition shifts non-uniformly (e.g., falling fertility accompanied by rising old-age dependency), providing a second observational wedge against standard CCAPM. The cross-equation restriction \((1-\gamma)\Omega = -\psi_x\xi_\theta/(\gamma\varphi_\theta)\) connects the effective risk-aversion coefficient in the SDF to three observables: the reproduction elasticity \(\gamma\) (identifiable from cohort fertility data), the capital-ratio savings response \(\xi_\theta\) (recoverable from investment-share dynamics under the model’s state-space closure), and the technology-shock response coefficient \(\varphi_\theta\) (recoverable from the response of normalized consumption to identified productivity shocks). The remaining Bellman-curvature term \(\psi_x \equiv \bar{x}\Psi''(\bar{x})/\Psi'(\bar{x})\) is mathematically identifiable via perturbation methods, though not empirically provided here. Testing this restriction against standard CCAPM — and deriving the GMM moment conditions for \(\Omega\) from joint demographic and asset pricing data — is the subject of a companion paper.

This paper establishes a structural framework placing the reproduction of the working population at the center of long-run growth and finance. Its formal strategy is one of deliberate methodological immanence: the same Bellman apparatus that standard macro deploys to establish the efficiency of competitive institutions is here turned to demonstrate that those institutions generate structurally necessary violations of the structural welfare criterion (necessary under the maintained closures: Assumption A3, complete-markets insurance, and the atomistic large-\(I\) limit) — competitive factor markets do not fail in this model by failing to solve their own Paretian optimization problem but by solving it perfectly, thereby compounding the reproductive deficits that RCD formalizes. A further structural result, derived in §3, is an endogenous concentration tendency: the atomistic price-taking closure is self-undermining, since under Proposition 3.1 the wealthiest dynasties’ population shares grow without bound until the yield hierarchy turns strategic — so competitive institutions do not merely permit but necessitate the erosion of their own price-taking foundation (its strategic formalization is left to the finite-\(I\) extension). Throughout, the paper operates within the stochastic recursive grammar of dynamic programming — a deliberate methodological choice whose condition of possibility is stationary, ergodic structure — a constraint the paper accepts because it is precisely the condition for deriving the critique from within the apparatus that establishes competitive efficiency. The stationarity assumption is the price of admission for immanent proof, not a concession to neoclassical convention. The stationary pricing kernel of Theorem 4.1 is the formal result that any future extension incorporating crisis dynamics, qualitative regime transitions, or non-ergodic reproductive trajectories must be consistent with — not as a claim that the historical record is ergodic. The immediate extensions are: (i) the empirical companion paper, identifying \(\Omega\) and testing the cross-equation restriction using reproduction-technology moments; (ii) a group-specific Bellman extension — separating liquidity-constrained dynasties from accumulating ones — that would supply the heterogeneous-agent general equilibrium with portfolio choice needed to derive the full distributional consequences of the RCD violation in a complete factor-market closure; and (iii) the axiomatics of RCD relative to Paretian and utilitarian orderings, sketched in §3 and deferred to future work.

Appendix: Key Mathematical Derivations

A.1 Full Corrected Euler Equation

The structural Euler equation is obtained by combining the first-order condition with the iterated \(V_N\) envelope. The first-order condition gives: \[\lambda_t = \beta\mathbb{E}_t\Bigl[V_N(N_{t+1},K_{t+1})\cdot\gamma\frac{N_{t+1}}{C_t}\Bigr].\] Substituting the \(V_N\) envelope one period forward: \[V_N(N_{t+1},K_{t+1}) = u'(N_{t+1}) + \lambda_{t+1}w_{t+1} + \beta\mathbb{E}_{t+1}\Bigl[V_N(N_{t+2},K_{t+2})\cdot(1-\gamma)\frac{N_{t+2}}{N_{t+1}}\Bigr]\] yields the open-form structural Euler equation stated in 2. The key departure from Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans is the demographic continuation term \((1-\gamma)N_{t+1}/N_t\): setting \(\gamma=1\) collapses the reproduction technology to a pure consumption-to-population link, eliminating the continuation term and recovering a standard Euler equation. The closed-form reduction of this open-form condition is established by the normalization and perturbation in Appendix A.2.

A.2 Structural Risk Premium — Full Derivation

Because the production and reproduction technologies are constant returns to scale in \((N_t, K_t, C_t)\) jointly, the value function satisfies the homogeneity condition \(V(xN, xK) = x^{1-\sigma}V(N,K)\), implying the correct functional form is: \[V(N_t, K_t) = N_t^{1-\sigma}\,\Psi\!\left(\frac{K_t}{N_t}\right)\] where \(\Psi\) is a function of the capital-labor ratio alone. Existence and uniqueness of the value function satisfying this homogeneous form follow from the standard contraction-mapping argument for bounded-growth Bellman operators (Stokey, Lucas, and Prescott 1989, ch. 4); the contraction condition stated in closure (i) of step (0) is the relevant sufficient condition here. The log-linear value-function ansatz \(v(n_t,k_t) = A_N n_t + A_K k_t\) (log-deviations of raw states) is consistent with this homogeneous form only in the knife-edge \(\sigma=1\) case; for general \(\sigma \neq 1\) it is incompatible with the model’s Bellman structure and must not be used.

The derivation proceeds in five steps: (0) Maintained Assumptions: (i) To ensure the normalized policy function exists as a contraction mapping, we impose the strict contraction bound \(\beta \mathbb{E}[\left(N_{t+1}/N_t\right)^{1-\sigma}] < 1\) (note: this is an implicit condition since \(N_{t+1}/N_t = Be^{\eta}\tilde{c}_t^\gamma\) involves the policy function \(\tilde{c}\); a primitive sufficient condition on exogenous parameters requires bounding \(\mathbb{E}[e^{(1-\sigma)\eta}]\) by \(1/\beta B^{1-\sigma}\) at the planner’s steady-state consumption share, which pins the condition on \(\sigma\), \(\gamma\), \(\beta\), \(B\), and the shock variance); (ii) the normalized stochastic steady state \((\bar{x},\bar{\tilde{c}})\) is assumed unique — to be verified by confirming the linearized Bellman-operator Jacobian has all eigenvalues strictly inside the unit circle at the fixed point; (iii) the BGP growth factor \(g_N \equiv \mathbb{E}[N_{t+1}/N_t]\) is assumed finite and positive, ensuring stationarity of the normalized system after \(N_t\) is factored out. The sign conditions \(\Omega > 0\) and \(\partial\Omega/\partial\gamma < 0\) are proved conditional on all five closures and Assumptions (A1)–(A2); see Theorem 4.1 and Step (5) below. Two further closures govern the perturbation step specifically: (iv) while the contraction bound (closure i) ensures existence of the value function, local uniqueness of the normalized SS (closure ii) formally requires saddle-path stability of the resulting policy function; (v) the SG&U second-order perturbation is locally valid in a neighborhood of the SS; the maintained log-normal shock distribution bounds the probability of large realizations escaping this neighborhood, but does not guarantee global accuracy. Sign conditions are proved conditional on all five closures and Assumptions (A1)–(A2). We further assume the state and shock variables admit log-normal innovations with finite second moments. The second-order perturbation is performed directly on the fully stationary stochastically normalized variables \((x_t, \tilde{c}_t)\), with the unit-root variable \(N_t\) factored entirely out. The sign conditions \(\Omega > 0\) and \(\partial\Omega/\partial\gamma < 0\) are proved for this stochastically normalized system conditional on Assumptions (A1)–(A2); (1) Normalized Bellman. Define \(x_t \equiv K_t/N_t\), \(\tilde{c}_t \equiv C_t/N_t\), \(g_t \equiv N_{t+1}/N_t = Be^{\eta_t}\tilde{c}_t^\gamma\), and \(y_t \equiv Y_t/N_t = Ae^{\theta_t}x_t^\alpha\). The normalized capital law of motion is \(x_{t+1} = (y_t - \tilde{c}_t + (1-\delta)x_t)/g_t\). Substituting \(V(N,K) = N^{1-\sigma}\Psi(x)\) into the Bellman equation and dividing by \(N_t^{1-\sigma}\) yields the normalized fixed-point problem: \[\Psi(x_t) = \max_{\tilde{c}_t}\!\left\{\frac{1}{1-\sigma} + \beta\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[g_t^{1-\sigma}\Psi(x_{t+1})\right]\right\}.\] The first-order condition for \(\tilde{c}_t\), using \(\partial x_{t+1}/\partial\tilde{c}_t = -g_t^{-1} - \gamma x_{t+1}/\tilde{c}_t\), is: \[\beta\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[g_t^{1-\sigma}\!\left(\frac{\gamma(1-\sigma)\Psi(x_{t+1})}{\tilde{c}_t} - \Psi'(x_{t+1})\!\left(\frac{1}{g_t}+\frac{\gamma x_{t+1}}{\tilde{c}_t}\right)\!\right)\right] = 0.\] (2) Stochastic steady state and linearized policy function. At the deterministic steady state (\(\theta=\eta=0\), \(x_t=\bar{x}\), \(\tilde{c}_t=\bar{c}\)), define \(\Phi(x) \equiv (1-\sigma)\Psi(x) - x\Psi'(x)\). The three SS conditions are: \[\bar{g} = B\bar{c}^\gamma, \qquad \bar{g}\bar{x} = A\bar{x}^\alpha + (1-\delta)\bar{x} - \bar{c}, \qquad \bar{g}\gamma\Phi(\bar{x}) = \bar{c}\Psi'(\bar{x}),\] together with the capital Euler \(\beta\bar{g}^{-\sigma}\bar{R}_K = 1\) where \(\bar{R}_K = \alpha A\bar{x}^{\alpha-1} + 1-\delta\) (the marginal/Euler discount \(\beta\bar{g}^{-\sigma}\) is the value-function discount \(\beta\bar{g}^{1-\sigma}\) divided by the \(\partial x_{t+1}/\partial x_t = \bar{R}_K/\bar{g}\) Jacobian; at the deterministic steady state \(\bar{R}_K = \bar{g}^{\sigma}/\beta = R_f\), consistent with Proposition 6.2). The linearized policy function around SS takes the form \[\hat{\tilde{c}}_t = \varphi_x\hat{x}_t + \varphi_\theta\theta_t + \varphi_\eta\eta_t, \quad \hat{x}_{t+1} = \xi_x\hat{x}_t + \xi_\theta\theta_t + \xi_\eta\eta_t,\] where \(\hat{a} \equiv \log(a_t/\bar{a})\) and the coefficients \((\varphi_x,\varphi_\theta,\varphi_\eta,\xi_x,\xi_\theta,\xi_\eta)\) are determined by log-linearizing the FOC and normalized Bellman envelope around the SS. The key coefficient — derived by log-linearizing \(x_{t+1} = (y_t - \tilde{c}_t + (1-\delta)x_t)/g_t\) fully via the quotient rule, accounting for \(g_t = Be^{\eta_t}\tilde{c}_t^\gamma\)’s dependence on \(\tilde{c}_t\) in the denominator — is: \[\xi_\theta = \frac{\bar{y} - \bar{c}\varphi_\theta}{\bar{g}\bar{x}} - \gamma\varphi_\theta,\] which governs the net capital-ratio response to a technology shock. The \(-\gamma\varphi_\theta\) term is the demographic dilution correction: a positive technology shock raises \(\tilde{c}_t\) (via \(\varphi_\theta > 0\)), which in turn raises \(g_t = Be^{\eta_t}\tilde{c}_t^\gamma\) (the denominator), partially offsetting the numerator’s increase. Standard RCK normalizations omit this correction because their growth rate is exogenous and \(g_t\) does not depend on \(\tilde{c}_t\). (3) Shadow price in log-deviations. From \(V_K(N_t,K_t) = N_t^{-\sigma}\Psi'(x_t)\) and the definition \(\lambda_t \equiv \beta\mathbb{E}_t[V_K(N_{t+1},K_{t+1})] = \beta N_{t+1}^{-\sigma}\Psi'(x_{t+1})\) (the expectation collapses exactly since \(N_{t+1} = Be^{\eta_t}N_t^{1-\gamma}C_t^\gamma\) and \(x_{t+1} = K_{t+1}/N_{t+1}\) are both strictly \(\mathcal{F}_t\)-measurable, with all shocks \((\eta_t,\theta_t)\) realized before the period-\(t\) choice \(C_t\) is made): \[\hat{\lambda}_t = -\sigma\hat{n}_{t+1} + \psi_x\hat{x}_{t+1},\] where \(\psi_x \equiv \bar{x}\Psi''(\bar{x})/\Psi'(\bar{x})\) is the curvature elasticity of \(\Psi'\) at the steady state. This expression implicitly incorporates the recursive \((1-\gamma)\) demographic continuation through \(\Psi'\), since \(\Psi\) is the Bellman fixed point that has absorbed the full infinite-horizon reproduction structure. (4) Second-order perturbation and SDF innovation. From \(m_{t+1} = \beta\lambda_{t+1}/\lambda_t\): \[\log m_{t+1} \approx \log\beta - \sigma(\hat{n}_{t+2} - \hat{n}_{t+1}) + \psi_x(\hat{x}_{t+2} - \hat{x}_{t+1}).\] The innovation to \(\log m_{t+1}\) driven by period-\((t+1)\) shocks \((\eta_{t+1},\theta_{t+1})\) is: \[\widehat{m}_{t+1} \equiv \log m_{t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t[\log m_{t+1}] = -\sigma\hat{n}_{t+2}^{\text{innov}} + \psi_x\hat{x}_{t+2}^{\text{innov}}\] with \(\hat{n}_{t+2}^{\text{innov}} = (1+\gamma\varphi_\eta)\eta_{t+1} + \gamma\varphi_\theta\theta_{t+1}\) and \(\hat{x}_{t+2}^{\text{innov}} = \xi_\eta\eta_{t+1} + \xi_\theta\theta_{t+1}\). Decomposing \(\hat{n}_{t+2}^{\text{innov}} = \eta_{t+1} + \gamma\Delta\tilde{c}_{t+1}^{\text{innov}}\) makes explicit that \(\widehat{m}_{t+1} = -\sigma\gamma\Delta\tilde{c}_{t+1}^{\text{innov}} - \sigma\eta_{t+1} + \psi_x\hat{x}_{t+2}^{\text{innov}}\). At first order in the Schmitt-Groh’{e}–Uribe sense (linear policy function with \(\Psi^{(1)'}\) constant), \(\psi_x = 0\) and \(\hat{m}_{t+1}\) carries zero conditional variance with respect to \(r_{K,t+1}\) (since \(R_{K,t+1} = \alpha Ae^{\theta_{t+1}}x_{t+1}^{\alpha-1}+1-\delta\) and \(x_{t+1}\) is \(\mathcal{F}_t\)-measurable). The \(\psi_x\) term requires \(\Psi''(\bar{x})\neq 0\) — equivalently, a second-order Taylor expansion of \(\Psi\) around the SS — and this is the non-degenerate channel that generates a non-zero risk premium. (5) Risk premium and the structural coefficient \(\Omega\). Because \(R_{K,t+1}\) depends on period-\((t+1)\) shocks only through \(\theta_{t+1}\) (with \(x_{t+1}\) predetermined at \(t\)), the stochastic component is \(r_{K,t+1}^{\text{innov}} = \rho_\theta\theta_{t+1}\) where \(\rho_\theta \equiv \alpha A\bar{x}^{\alpha-1}/\bar{R}_K\). Applying \(\mathbb{E}_t[R_{K,t+1}] - R_{f,t} \approx -\operatorname{Cov}_t(\log m_{t+1}, r_{K,t+1})\): \[\mathbb{E}_t[R_{K,t+1}] - R_{f,t} = \sigma\gamma\operatorname{Cov}_t(\Delta\tilde{c}_{t+1}, R_{K,t+1}) + \left(-\frac{\psi_x\xi_\theta}{\gamma\varphi_\theta}\right)\operatorname{Cov}_t(\Delta n_{t+2}, R_{K,t+1}),\] using \(\operatorname{Cov}_t(\Delta\tilde{c}_{t+1},R_{K,t+1}) = \varphi_\theta\rho_\theta\sigma_\theta^2\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}_t(\Delta n_{t+2},R_{K,t+1}) = \gamma\varphi_\theta\rho_\theta\sigma_\theta^2\) (where \(\operatorname{Cov}_t(\eta_{t+1}, R_{K,t+1}) = 0\) because \(R_{K,t+1}\) depends only on \(\theta_{t+1}\) and the predetermined \(x_{t+1}\), and the primitive shocks \(\theta_{t+1}\perp\eta_{t+1}\) are maintained orthogonal throughout — a standard assumption stated in Step (0)). Matching with the Theorem 4.1 formula \((1-\gamma)\Omega = -\psi_x\xi_\theta/(\gamma\varphi_\theta)\) identifies:

If aggregate productivity and reproductive shocks are correlated in observed data, this baseline covariance mapping is contaminated and empirical identification of \(\Omega\) must jointly estimate that correlation structure. \[\Omega = -\frac{\psi_x\,\xi_\theta}{(1-\gamma)\,\gamma\,\varphi_\theta}.\] Sign of \(\Omega\). Under Assumptions (A1)–(A2) stated in Theorem 4.1: (A1) implies \(\psi_x < 0\); (A2) implies \(\xi_\theta > 0\); \(\varphi_\theta > 0\) because a positive technology shock raises permanent income and consumption responds positively at the SS linearization — formally, log-linearizing the Euler equation at SS gives \(\hat{\tilde{c}}_t = \varphi_x\hat{x}_t + \varphi_\theta\theta_t\) with \(\varphi_\theta > 0\) (see Stokey, Lucas, and Prescott 1989, chs.~4–5 for the policy-function sign in the standard stochastic growth model); \(\gamma(1-\gamma)>0\). Hence \(\Omega = -(\text{neg})(\text{pos})/((\text{pos})^3) > 0\). Declining demographic risk price. \((1-\gamma)\Omega = -\psi_x\xi_\theta/(\gamma\varphi_\theta)\) is strictly decreasing in \(\gamma\) (partial derivative \(\psi_x/\gamma + \psi_x\xi_\theta/(\gamma^2\varphi_\theta) < 0\), taking the explicit \(\gamma\)-dependence of \(\xi_\theta\) into account), so the demographic channel’s contribution to the effective risk price falls as \(\gamma\) rises. At \(\gamma=1\) the factorization through \((1-\gamma)\) and \(\Omega\) separately becomes singular, but the product converges to the finite positive constant \(-\psi_x\xi_\theta/\varphi_\theta > 0\); the full demographic premium does not vanish at this boundary.

A.3 Reproductive-Capacity Dominance (Formal) Allocation \(A\) Reproductive-Capacity Dominates allocation \(B\) under a discounted variant of the Ramsey-Weizsäcker overtaking criterion — replacing undiscounted partial sums with \(\beta^t\)-weighted partial sums — if (i) for every dynasty \(i\), \(\liminf_{T \to \infty} \mathbb{E}_0\left[\sum_{t=0}^T \beta^t (N_{i,t}^A - N_{i,t}^B)\right] \ge 0\), and (ii) for at least one dynasty the inequality is strictly positive. No spectral radius restriction on \(\beta g_N\) is imposed as part of the RCD definition; the criterion is adopted precisely to handle the case where \(N_t\) grows along the equilibrium path. The classical Ramsey-Weizsäcker criterion uses undiscounted partial sums; the \(\beta^t\)-weighting preserves the overtaking structure while maintaining commensurability with the dynastic Bellman objective. The Bellman transversality condition \(\lim_{T\to\infty}\beta^T \mathbb{E}[V_K K_T] = 0\) is a separate optimality requirement.
