Research Built to Change Things

Carlos Galindo Escajeda · June 2026

There is a version of academic economics that ends at the journal page. It builds the model, proves the theorem, posts the paper, and moves on. The discipline reproduced; the world unchanged.

This is not that version.

Every paper in this programme was built backwards from a specific question: what would you actually need to know — rigorously, defensibly, with precision that survives a parliamentary committee — to justify a specific intervention in Britain’s housing market? Not to describe the problem. Not to contribute to the literature. To change something real, now, in a market that has been failing working households for forty years.

The answer turned out to require five papers.

The Problem Is Not Ignorance. It Is a Bad Argument That Has Never Been Properly Answered.

The obstacle to rent regulation in Britain is not a lack of political will. It is a single economic argument, endlessly repeated, that shuts down the conversation before the evidence is examined: rent controls choke supply, therefore they harm the people they protect. The argument sounds like science. It is deployed as a veto.

The case for inaction rests on a model of a market that Britain does not have. The textbook conclusion holds inside a competitive rental market — elastic supply, many landlords, prices that track the real cost of shelter. Britain’s private rented sector has not been that market since the 1988 Act. Planning has throttled new build for decades. The 1988 deregulation stripped out tenant protections. Buy-to-let lending turned homes into leveraged financial instruments whose prices now track credit conditions, not the cost of housing people. A home in this market is four things at once: somewhere to live, an investment asset, collateral for a loan, and a pipe carrying housing benefit into landlord yields.

You cannot use a competitive-market veto on a market that is not competitive. The standard objection cuts both ways: once you establish that competitive conditions have failed across multiple margins simultaneously — which is what the second-best theorem says — deregulation loses its claim to be the safe default exactly as much as regulation loses any claim to be automatically efficient. Doing nothing is not a return to a healthy market. It is a choice to leave a broken one in place.

That argument is the core of Breaking the Benchmark. It was not written to sit on a shelf. It was written to survive — and to disarm — the veto.

Theory Is Not the Opposite of Action. It Is What Makes Action Defensible.

There are two welfare-theory papers in this programme. Readers who see “welfare theory” and expect a retreat from the concrete will be surprised.

The first — Rent as Reproduction Cost — treats housing expenditure as an input to the reproduction of labour power, not consumption. This is not an ideological position. It is a diagnostic move with precise empirical implications. If you measure welfare correctly — not as the satisfaction of preferences but as the capacity of households to sustain and reproduce themselves across generations — you can derive a specific, testable upper bound on welfare-consistent rent. You can ask, quantitatively, whether market rents exceed that bound. Under the post-2008 buy-to-let conditions, they do. The CPI-anchored brake is not a patch; under this framework, it is the efficient correction.

The second — Labor Power as Numéraire — provides the deeper foundation. It replaces the standard welfare criterion of Pareto efficiency with one built on Labour-Power Dominance: competitive markets systematically fail this criterion when initial wealth is unequal, even with perfect competition and full information. This is not a radical claim dressed as theory. It is a formal result, derived within a Bellman framework that orthodox economists use every day, with testable implications for asset pricing and macrofinance.

The point of both papers is the same: the policy case is not merely political. It is structurally sound. It follows from first principles that do not bend under scrutiny. When a hostile economist asks what is your welfare criterion for this intervention? — these papers provide the answer. When a Treasury official asks why isn’t the market solving this itself? — these papers explain, formally, why it cannot.

Theory becomes actionable when it is sharp enough to answer the adversary’s best argument. Both papers were sharpened for exactly that purpose.

Evidence That Survives the Strongest Objection

Fiscal Capture provides the empirical load-bearing beam. Using two directionally-opposite natural policy events — the April 2020 LHA uplift and the April 2021 re-freeze — across 290 English local authorities between 2019 and 2024, it tests one of the most important and least examined questions in UK housing policy: does housing benefit reach tenants, or do landlords absorb it?

The answer is consistent across 26 specifications with zero sign reversals: there is evidence of landlord capture. Every coefficient is negative. The headline figure is reported as a conservative lower bound. The research design was built to be robust to the objection that a single policy event could be confounded; using two opposite events that should produce opposite effects, and finding the same directional result, is the kind of robustness that survives hostile review.

The finding matters not only on its own terms. It links directly to the policy argument in Breaking the Benchmark: if benefits are being captured by landlords rather than reaching tenants, then benefits and rent regulation are not alternatives. They are complements. You cannot get the money to the people who need it without also controlling the price. That is not a political preference. It is an empirical result.

Built for Scrutiny, Not for Comfort

The fifth paper — A Progressing Problemshift — does something unusual. It audits the other four using Imre Lakatos’s framework for evaluating whether a research programme is genuinely developing or merely patching over its own failures with ad hoc fixes.

The verdict is split, and it is honest. The programme is theoretically progressive: each successive paper adds testable content rather than retreating from it. The empirical record is progressing — moving in the right direction — but not yet progressive in the strict Lakatosian sense, because the distinguishing empirical test has been named but not yet run. The paper does not claim otherwise.

That kind of self-audit is rare. It is there because the purpose of this programme is to persuade people who will look for the weakest point. If you find the weakness first and name it clearly, you control the conversation. If you pretend the weakness does not exist, someone else finds it at the worst possible moment.

The Aim Is Specific

This is not a contribution to the literature on housing economics. It is a case file for a specific intervention in the UK private rented sector — an emergency rent brake with statutory exit criteria and a ban on vacancy decontrol — and the conditions under which that intervention should be sustained, expanded, or ended.

The papers are the argument. The argument is about a place where people live, a market that is extracting from the working population rather than serving it, and a policy lever that can be pulled now.

The point is to change it.
