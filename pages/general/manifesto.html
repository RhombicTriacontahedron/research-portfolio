Research Built to Change Things (Manifesto)

Carlos Galindo Escajeda · June 2026

The push for rent regulation routinely collides with a specific structural obstacle: the textbook claim that price controls inevitably reduce housing supply, deployed to close down discussion before the evidence is examined. Rebutting that claim on its own terms requires showing, with the same dynamic tools mainstream economists use, that the competitive conditions the objection assumes do not hold in Britain’s private rented sector. That is what the papers here do.

Most economic research ends at the journal page — it describes housing and other crises without helping to fix them. The point of this work is to help change or alleviate them, for good.

The case here was built backwards from a single question: what would you actually need — proven, verifiable, and resistant to the strongest attack that can be mounted against it — to justify a specific intervention in Britain’s housing market? The goal is not to describe the problem but to help fix it.

Five papers turned out to be required. Four are built backwards from that question. The fifth — Reproductive Capacity as Numéraire — runs the other way: it is the value theory the others rest on, developed in its own right and prior to the housing question, not bent to fit the conclusion.

The Orthodox Veto Is Not Decisive. The Market It Assumes Does Not Exist.

The obstacle to rent regulation in Britain is not merely political. It is a single economic argument deployed as a conversation-stopper: rent controls choke supply and harm the people they protect. The argument sounds like settled science. It is not.

The textbook conclusion holds inside a competitive rental market defined by elastic supply, many landlords, and prices that track the real cost of shelter. Britain’s private rented sector (PRS) has systematically failed these conditions since the 1988 Housing Act. Supply is highly inelastic — planning restrictions have throttled new build for decades. The 1988 deregulation stripped tenant security and bargaining power. Buy-to-let (BTL) lending turned dwellings into leveraged financial instruments priced not by what it costs to house someone but by what investors expect to earn — a formula that pumps values far above any shelter-cost anchor whenever interest rates fall, and leaves rents chasing those inflated asset values long after the credit conditions that created them have passed. A home in this market operates simultaneously as shelter, an investment asset, credit collateral, and a fiscal conduit routing housing benefit into landlord yields.

When competitive conditions fail across multiple margins simultaneously, the Lipsey-Lancaster general theorem of the second best establishes that removing one distortion — or relying on supply deregulation alone — does not guarantee a welfare improvement. Neither deregulation nor regulation carries a presumptive welfare warrant.

What the result actually establishes: the competitive-market veto is not decisive. It does not prove the brake is automatically optimal; it removes the argument that the brake is presumptively wrong. The affirmative case for the brake is then made by structural evidence and structural welfare theory — which is what the remaining papers do.

Doing nothing is not a neutral return to a healthy market. It is a political choice to leave a deliberately engineered, broken system in place.

Theory on the Orthodoxy’s Own Ground

Two of the five papers are welfare theory. Neither retreats from the concrete.

Rent as Reproduction Cost treats housing expenditure not as generic consumption, but as a necessary input to the reproduction of the working population. It is a framework choice with precise structural consequences: welfare is measured not by the satisfaction of subjective preferences, but by the capacity of households to physically sustain and reproduce themselves across generations. That measurement yields a derivable, testable upper bound on welfare-consistent rent. Under plausible post-2008 BTL overvaluation conditions, the paper argues that market rents exceed that bound. The CPI-anchored brake, under this framework, is not an arbitrary patch — it is the directionally correct response to reduce the demographic externality.

Reproductive Capacity as Numéraire provides the deep foundation through a strategy of deliberate methodological immanence: it adopts the Bellman apparatus that standard macroeconomics uses to prove market efficiency, and turns it against the orthodox conclusion. It replaces Pareto efficiency with Reproductive-Capacity Dominance (RCD) and asks whether competitive factor markets can be presumed to satisfy it. They cannot — even under benchmark conditions of perfect competition and full information — when initial wealth is unequal. Competitive markets here do not fail by failing to solve their own optimization problem; they fail by solving it cleanly and, in doing so, compounding reproductive deficits the Paretian welfare criterion was never built to register. This non-presumption theorem yields a closed-form cross-equation restriction for asset pricing, tying the effective risk-aversion coefficient to physical reproduction parameters and reducing the modeler’s degrees of freedom.

Together these papers answer the two questions a hostile audience will ask. What is your welfare criterion for this intervention? — the reproduction-cost framework answers this with a derivable bound. Why isn’t the market self-correcting? — that result answers this: the market-as-solver presumption fails its own theoretical conditions.

Theory is useful when it is sharp enough to answer the adversary’s strongest argument. Both papers were sharpened for that purpose.

Opposite Events, the Same Direction

Fiscal Capture carries the empirical weight. It uses a difference-in-differences design across 290 English local authorities (2019–2024), exploiting two directionally opposite natural policy events: the April 2020 Local Housing Allowance (LHA) uplift and the April 2021 re-freeze.

The logic of the design is severe: opposite events should produce opposite effects if the mechanism runs through tenants. The paper looks for sign reversals across more than twenty distinct specifications. There are none. Every point estimate is in the direction consistent with landlord capture. The magnitude and precision are reported in the paper; the headline coefficient is stated as a conservative lower bound, attenuated by documented downward nominal rigidity and tenant lock-in.

Sign-consistency across opposite events is not the same as a precisely sized effect. But it closes off the “single-event confound” objection — the most common first-line attack on DiD designs in policy-adjacent research. The directional finding is as clean as this design can produce.

It connects directly to the case made in Why the Rent-Control Veto Fails: if housing benefit is absorbed by landlords rather than reaching tenants, then subsidies and rent regulation are complements, not alternatives.

The Evidence Does Not Interpret Itself

Both sides can agree on what Fiscal Capture establishes — that landlords absorb much of the LHA — and still draw opposite policies from it, because the same fact means different things in different theories.

Read housing as an ordinary commodity, and the LHA is a distortion propping up a price floor. Capture is the leak; rent growth stalling at the freeze looks like the market healing once the leak stops. The implied policy is to stop subsidising demand and build supply — bypass the landlord rather than regulate the price.

Read housing as a constraint on the reproduction of the working population — the framework of Rent as Reproduction Cost and Reproductive Capacity as Numéraire — and the LHA is not a distortion but a defence of the consumption base of the lowest-paid. Capture is then the proof that the price, not only the subsidy, has to be controlled; and under this reading a deceleration driven by households that can no longer sustain the rent is not the market healing but demand being destroyed.

The regression cannot referee between these readings; the one test that would — differential household formation under sustained PRS pressure — is named in the fifth paper and has not been run. That is why the theory is load-bearing, not ornamental. The orthodox “build, don’t regulate” conclusion is not the neutral reading of the data it presents itself as: it is one ontology’s reading, no better licensed by the evidence than the other, and with the weaker account of why the price moved. Choosing between them is a judgment about what housing is — and the papers make that judgment explicit, defend it, and accept the test that could refute it.

Theoretically Progressive, Empirically Unfinished

A Progressing Problemshift applies Imre Lakatos’s framework directly to the programme itself, examining whether the research is genuinely developing testable content or merely patching failures ad hoc.

The verdict is split, and it is stated plainly. The programme is theoretically progressive: it converted an unproven demographic-risk conjecture into a closed-form restriction that reduces the modeler’s degrees of freedom. The empirical record is progressing — accumulating in the expected direction with robust sign-consistency — but it is not yet empirically progressive in the strict Lakatosian sense. The distinguishing empirical test (differential household formation and population dynamics under sustained PRS pressure) has been specified and named. It has not yet been run.

This kind of self-appraisal is rare in policy research, for obvious reasons. It is here because the purpose of this programme is to persuade reviewers who actively look for the weakest point. Name the open frontier first, and you control the conversation. Leave it unnamed, and someone else weaponises it at the worst possible moment.

A Case File for an Emergency Rent Brake

This is not a contribution to the literature. It is a rigorous case file for a specific intervention in the UK private rented sector: an emergency rent brake, with the analysis bearing on the design of pre-committed statutory exit criteria and protection against vacancy decontrol as supported policy implications. The papers identify when the brake is warranted, under what conditions it should be sustained or wound down, and what evidence would settle the remaining open questions.

The papers are the argument. The argument concerns a market that extracts from the working population rather than serving it, and identifies the lever that can be pulled now.

The point is to change it.

Ready to Be Tested, Not Dismissed

  • Hard core: reproductive capacity as the numéraire of value; Reproductive-Capacity Dominance as the welfare criterion (replaces, rather than refines, Pareto efficiency).
  • Theoretical status: progressive. Each successive paper adds testable content without retreating from prior commitments.
  • Empirical status: progressing. Directional findings are robust across specifications; the single discriminating test that would separate the programme from its orthodox rival has been named but not yet run.
  • Non-degenerating: under sustained attack on the core itself, the programme has responded with belt revision and principled refusal — not with ad hoc rescue.

This is not a finished theory. It is a developing research programme that has reached the point where its claims can be tested rather than dismissed. The brake is the correct emergency response to a market that demonstrably fails the competitive conditions the standard objections assume. It should be introduced now, paired with supply expansion and land-value capture, and bound to transparent, pre-committed exit criteria.

The point is to change it.
