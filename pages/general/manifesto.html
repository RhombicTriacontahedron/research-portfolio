Research Built to Change Things (Manifesto)

Carlos Galindo Escajeda · June 2026

“The philosophers have only interpreted the world in various ways; the point is to change it.” — KHM, Theses on Feuerbach (Thesis XI), 1845.

Most economic research ends at the journal page. At best, it merely interprets housing and other crises in various ways; the point of this programme is to help change or alleviate them—the point is to help change the material world, for good!

Every paper here was built backwards from a single question: what would you actually need — proven, verifiable, and resistant to the strongest attack that can be mounted against it — to justify a specific intervention in Britain’s housing market? The goal is not to describe the problem. The point is to change it.

Five papers turned out to be required.

The Orthodox Veto Is Not Decisive. The Market It Assumes Does Not Exist.

The obstacle to rent regulation in Britain is not merely political. It is a single economic argument deployed as a conversation-stopper: rent controls choke supply and harm the people they protect. The argument sounds like settled science. It is not.

The textbook conclusion holds inside a competitive rental market defined by elastic supply, many landlords, and prices that track the real cost of shelter. Britain’s private rented sector (PRS) has systematically failed these conditions since the 1988 Housing Act. Supply is highly inelastic — planning restrictions have throttled new build for decades. The 1988 deregulation stripped tenant security and bargaining power. Buy-to-let (BTL) lending turned dwellings into leveraged financial instruments priced not by what it costs to house someone but by what investors expect to earn — a formula that pumps values far above any shelter-cost anchor whenever interest rates fall, and leaves rents chasing those inflated asset values long after the credit conditions that created them have passed. A home in this market operates simultaneously as shelter, an investment asset, credit collateral, and a fiscal conduit routing housing benefit into landlord yields.

When competitive conditions fail across multiple margins simultaneously, the Lipsey-Lancaster general theorem of the second best establishes that removing one distortion — or relying on supply deregulation alone — does not guarantee a welfare improvement. Neither deregulation nor regulation carries a presumptive welfare warrant.

What the result actually establishes: the competitive-market veto is not decisive. It does not prove the brake is automatically optimal; it removes the argument that the brake is presumptively wrong. The affirmative case for the brake is then made by structural evidence and materialist welfare theory — which is what the remaining papers do.

Doing nothing is not a neutral return to a healthy market. It is a political choice to leave a deliberately engineered, broken system in place.

Theory Is a Precision Instrument

Two welfare-theory papers in this programme. Neither retreats from the concrete.

Rent as Reproduction Cost treats housing expenditure not as generic consumption, but as a necessary input to the reproduction of labour power. It is a framework choice with precise structural consequences: welfare is measured not by the satisfaction of subjective preferences, but by the capacity of households to physically sustain and reproduce themselves across generations. That measurement yields a derivable, testable upper bound on welfare-consistent rent. Under plausible post-2008 BTL overvaluation conditions, the paper argues that market rents exceed that bound. The CPI-anchored brake, under this framework, is not an arbitrary patch — it is the directionally correct response to compress the demographic externality.

Labor Power as Numéraire provides the deep foundation through a strategy of deliberate methodological immanence: it adopts the Bellman apparatus that standard macroeconomics uses to prove market efficiency, and turns it against the orthodox conclusion. It replaces Pareto efficiency with Labour-Power Dominance (LPD) and asks whether competitive factor markets can be presumed to satisfy it. They cannot — even under benchmark conditions of perfect competition and full information — when initial wealth is unequal. Competitive markets here do not fail by failing to solve their own optimization problem; they fail by solving it cleanly and, in doing so, compounding reproductive deficits the Paretian welfare criterion was never built to register. This non-presumption theorem yields a closed-form cross-equation restriction for asset pricing, tying the effective risk-aversion coefficient to physical reproduction parameters and reducing the modeler’s degrees of freedom.

Together these papers answer the two questions a hostile audience will ask. What is your welfare criterion for this intervention? — the reproduction-cost framework answers this with a derivable bound. Why isn’t the market self-correcting? — the LPN result answers this: the market-as-solver presumption fails its own theoretical conditions.

Theory is useful when it is sharp enough to answer the adversary’s strongest argument. Both papers were sharpened for that purpose.

Evidence Designed for Hostile Scrutiny

Fiscal Capture carries the empirical weight. It uses a difference-in-differences design across 290 English local authorities (2019–2024), exploiting two directionally opposite natural policy events: the April 2020 Local Housing Allowance (LHA) uplift and the April 2021 re-freeze.

The logic of the design is severe: opposite events should produce opposite effects if the mechanism runs through tenants. The paper looks for sign reversals across 26 distinct specifications. There are none. Every point estimate is in the direction consistent with landlord capture. The magnitude and precision are reported in the paper; the headline coefficient is stated as a conservative lower bound, attenuated by documented downward nominal rigidity and tenant lock-in.

Sign-consistency across opposite events is not the same as a precisely sized effect. But it closes off the “single-event confound” objection — the most common first-line attack on DiD designs in policy-adjacent research. The directional finding is as clean as this design can produce.

It connects directly to Breaking the Benchmark: if housing benefit is absorbed by landlords rather than reaching tenants, then subsidies and rent regulation are not alternatives — they are structural complements. The price must be controlled before the money can reach the people it was meant for. That is not a political preference. It is what the data say.

Honest About What Is Not Yet Done

A Progressing Problemshift applies Imre Lakatos’s framework directly to the programme itself, auditing whether the research is genuinely developing testable content or merely patching failures ad hoc.

The verdict is split, and it is stated plainly. The programme is theoretically progressive: it converted an unproven demographic-risk conjecture into a closed-form restriction that reduces the modeler’s degrees of freedom. The empirical record is progressing — accumulating in the expected direction with robust sign-consistency — but it is not yet empirically progressive in the strict Lakatosian sense. The distinguishing empirical test (differential household formation and population dynamics under sustained PRS pressure) has been specified and named. It has not yet been run.

This kind of self-audit is rare in policy research, for obvious reasons. It is here because the purpose of this programme is to persuade reviewers who actively look for the weakest point. Name the open frontier first, and you control the conversation. Leave it unnamed, and someone else weaponises it at the worst possible moment.

The Aim Is Specific

This is not a contribution to the literature. It is a rigorous case file for a specific intervention in the UK private rented sector: an emergency rent brake, with the programme’s analysis bearing on the design of pre-committed statutory exit criteria and protection against vacancy decontrol as supported policy implications. The papers identify when the brake is warranted, under what conditions it should be sustained or wound down, and what evidence would settle the remaining open questions.

The papers are the argument. The argument concerns a market that extracts from the working population rather than serving it, and identifies the lever that can be pulled now.

The point is to change it.

Programme Status (Lakatosian Summary)

  • Hard core: labour power as the numéraire of value; Labour-Power Dominance as the welfare criterion (replaces, rather than refines, Pareto efficiency).
  • Theoretical status: progressive. Each successive paper adds testable content without retreating from prior commitments.
  • Empirical status: progressing. Directional findings are robust across specifications; the single discriminating test that would separate the programme from its orthodox rival has been named but not yet run.
  • Non-degenerating: under sustained attack on the core itself, the programme has responded with belt revision and principled refusal — not with ad hoc rescue.

This is not a finished theory. It is a developing research programme that has reached the point where its claims can be tested rather than dismissed. The brake is the correct emergency response to a market that demonstrably fails the competitive conditions the standard objections assume. It should be introduced now, paired with supply expansion and land-value capture, and bound to transparent, pre-committed exit criteria.

The point is to change it.
