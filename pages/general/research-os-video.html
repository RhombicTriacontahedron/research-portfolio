The Work Behind the Screen

Carlos Galindo Escajeda · June 2026

A word before you press play, because this page is easy to misread. AI did not author this work; a person did — one who has built models from scratch for decades and knew exactly what he was looking for. The machine fetched and typed; the thinking, the argument, and the judgement are his. If seeing AI anywhere near research makes you wary, keep that instinct — it is the right one, and it is the whole reason none of this was left to the machine.

The video on this page shows a machine producing text. That is the least interesting part of the process.

Producing text costs nothing now. Anyone can generate a draft in seconds. But a draft is not research. Left to itself, a machine pulls a sharp argument back toward the conventional. The real work is not generating the output. It is designing the machinery that produces it and auditing what comes back — the code and the mathematics alike — a powerful but volatile tool kept firmly under the hand.

In a quixotesque way, the machine is tireless and genuinely useful — it will fetch a figure, carry an argument across three counties, and never complain. But it stays a squire. It requires a Quixote to envision and to use his imagination to lead the quest. Sancho keeps no quest of its own, and it cannot tell a giant from a windmill. Quixote supplies the vision and holds the reins; the squire does the legwork. What follows is what it takes to keep the Quixote and Sancho honest.

What you are looking at is the surface of a whole body of work. Beneath it sit five linked papers. One builds a theory of why housing costs decide whether working families can hold together across generations. Another tests that theory against real UK government data — rent indices, benefit caseloads, housing stock. A third works out what rent would have to be to leave families that room. A fourth defends a rent cap using the same mathematics mainstream economists use to oppose one. The fifth audits the whole programme and says plainly where the evidence stops.

Every figure ties to a public source you can check. Every draft was attacked on purpose — from the evidence, the logic, the framing, and the strongest case the other side could make — to find where it broke. Each draft was cross-examined by competing models, so no model marked its own homework. The machine was not careful on its own. The person running it was.

Work like this means moving between very different kinds of thinking. One problem is working out why two government datasets give clashing numbers for the same local authority; another is checking by hand whether a line of stochastic calculus actually follows from the Bellman equation above it. In between is everything else — cleaning bad data, holding an argument to a proof, and knowing which results are real and which only look it.

That range does not come from software. It comes from a career measured in decades: building models from scratch, teaching people to use data so messy it had been abandoned, and, above all, being wrong often enough that failure starts to look familiar. That experience is what lets a person read a result the way a doctor reads a scan. It catches the machine claiming more than it has shown.

The work leaves a trail.

When two official sources conflicted, the numbers were reconciled, not quietly averaged away. When a central theorem could only prove a narrow case, it was scoped back to a first-order perturbation rather than dressed up as a general law. And when the machine tried to soften a sharp idea — “KKT distortion mechanisms” — into something blander, the change was caught and reversed. Three different errors, caught by the same judgment.

That judgment also decides what to give up. When a test on migration data came back weak, it was not made to look decisive. It went into a footnote, and stronger evidence took its place: the same finding — that landlords capture much of the housing subsidy — holding across more than twenty different specifications, with no reversals.

AI tools are everywhere now. What stays rare is the judgment to know what is real, and the discipline to prove it to a reader who assumes it is not.

The knight charged windmills, certain they were giants. Here it is the other way round: the giant is real — the UK housing market — and the world has agreed to call it a windmill. Seeing that takes imagination. Proving it takes everything above. The squire never asks whether the giant is real; it charges on command and calls the day done. The asking is mine — and so is being wrong.

A note on the video. What you are watching is deliberately partial — the tip of an iceberg, not the method itself. The point is not that a machine can produce text; it is that the system behind it had to be designed, stress-tested, and brought to the point where its output holds up — and that engineering was the author’s, built over years before any of it ran. The system is unique and it is effective; the research gathered on this site is the evidence. None of it was handed down by a god, or by a robot god. It was built.
