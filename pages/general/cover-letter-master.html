Cover Letter in Full Glory

Carlos Galindo Escajeda · cgalindito@gmail.com · London, UK · +44 (0) 737-677-1146 Eligible to work in the UK

Dear Recruitment Team,

NEF’s A Modern System for Fair Rents set me reading, then mulling, then following one idea into the next — and that is the honest origin of this application for the Social Policy Economist / Quantitative Researcher position. I did not begin with a grand design, least of all a plan to “correct” NEF. It was closer to self-discovery under a hard time limit: the questions I kept pulling on organically coalesced into five linked papers, and the deeper I dug, the more independent lines of inquiry converged — not on whether to intervene in the UK private rented sector, but on a single genuinely contentious question that I did not go looking for: the durability of the intervention, and how far the case for an emergency brake actually extends toward a permanent fair-rents regime. What follows is who built that, how, and the spirit in which I would want to do it from inside your team.

I have worked this way since long before AI existed — starting from scratch, in unfamiliar territory, into the unknown, and against the clock — and I have real confidence that my ingenuity, curiosity, and diligence will get me there on any such occasion. My first serious research ran exactly this pattern. I was working on poverty and demographic risk in a context very different from the UK — Mexico’s low-income household sector — but the policy question was structurally similar to what NEF works on: how do we distribute the burden and benefit of social risk across the income distribution?

That puzzle led me to microsimulate poverty-reduction policy, and doing it meant teaching myself three unfamiliar things at once, from a standing start: how relational databases actually work, well outside the clean tabular data of an econometrics course; how to handle the complex sampling design and processing gymnastics of SEDESOL’s household survey microdata, until I could manipulate and explain it fluidly; and how the Ravallion microsimulation literature operated — estimating a household consumption model, then simulating a policy change to measure how much it shifts each subgroup’s probability of being poor. I learned to program in STATA for that work specifically, and did all of it in under six months — while finishing a master’s thesis on an entirely separate subject: a two-sector overlapping-generations model of household heterogeneity, growth, and convergence between rich and poor. That poverty work became peer-reviewed publications on poverty measurement and policy simulation.

Then I did the part I am proudest of: I packaged the hard-won processing knowledge and gave it away — more than 700 hours of STATA training so that undergraduates could finally mine survey microdata that had gone under-exploited precisely because it was so hard to process, feeding a stream of undergraduate theses. Multiplying the people around me, not only shipping my own output, is the disposition I would bring to your team.

From there I spent the better part of a decade building quantitative models in macro-finance — emerging-market forecasting, Bayesian time-series and DSGE work, even reverse-engineering parts of the Federal Reserve’s FRB/US model to surface scenarios the consensus had missed — at Deutsche Bank, Bank of America, and a global macro fund. Applying to your social-policy team is, for me, a deliberate return to where I began, now with a macro modeller’s toolkit behind it.

So I would ask you to read my recent arrival in UK housing policy the right way. Until I saw this posting I was a tourist in it — but “tourist” is how nearly every one of my best projects has begun; it names how I work, not a ceiling on where I reach. Two honest caveats follow.

First: do not mistake my theoretical papers for a desire to live in abstraction. I am driven to transform material reality — that is what drew me to policy in the first place. But theory and empirics are complements, not substitutes: theory disciplines what we ask, empirics answers it, and the two together correct each other.

You will find these papers are about hardening the welfare case for policies NEF already advocates, grounding them in structural arguments that survive hostile scrutiny. I am not here to replace your policy expertise but to arm it with a particular rigour. The arguments that decide housing policy are fought in public against economists trained in the orthodoxy and trusted by government — and they are won on that ground, with those tools. I can argue there: Breaking the Benchmark uses a mainstream second-best result to take apart the standard case against rent control. That is not the only thing I bring, but it is the hardest to find.

I have laid all this out in the Manifesto — what the five papers are actually for, why each contributes to a specific policy intervention rather than the academic literature, and why theory and action are never in tension in this work.

Second: do not expect me to recite every line flawlessly across an interview table. Across work this broad and this deep, recall matters less than whether it holds up on the page. What matters is that the work is verifiable, genuinely original, and non-trivial by orders of magnitude — not a repackaging of the familiar. I can rebuild any part of it on request, and the ability to reconstruct from first principles, rather than to recite, is the real capability I bring. Plainly: the one thing I find genuinely difficult is real-time speech — partly because I am autistic and dyslexic. What comes with it is the opposite strength: I can stay with a complex problem far longer than is typical, and with the right tools carrying the mechanical load, that sustained focus is what produced everything here. It is not a limitation I work around; this package is that capability on the page, not a description of it.

A brief note on method. Every load-bearing claim in these papers is verified against a primary source, survives adversarial challenge, or is flagged open — the discipline I work by. The best evidence that this is real and not rhetorical is the fifth paper, A Progressing Problemshift: it applies mainstream philosophy-of-science standards to my own programme and, against my own immediate advantage, downgrades the strongest-looking verdict from “empirically progressive” to “empirically progressing” because the discriminating test is named but still unrun. That is the reflex I would bring under external scrutiny: ambitious claims, strict boundaries, and transparent uncertainty where evidence has not yet closed the question.

The papers map directly onto your specification.

Empirical rigour on large UK datasets. Fiscal Capture in the UK Private Rented Sector is independent, critical panel-data work: a difference-in-differences design across 290 English local authorities (2019–2024), exploiting the April 2020 LHA uplift and April 2021 re-freeze as directionally opposite policy events. Across more than twenty specifications the signature is consistent with landlord capture, with zero sign reversals — and I report the headline coefficient as a deliberate conservative lower bound, naming the attenuation channels (downward nominal rigidity, tenant lock-in) rather than inflating it.

Challenging orthodoxy without overreach. Breaking the Benchmark dismantles the “price controls choke off supply” veto by modelling the UK PRS as a structurally distorted quadruple-object market — shelter, capitalised asset, credit collateral, and fiscal conduit at once. Lipsey–Lancaster second-best logic then cuts symmetrically: once competitive conditions are absent, deregulation forfeits its automatic-efficiency claim exactly as much as regulation does. The paper defends your emergency brake and hardens it — the design is NEF’s own Fair Rents brake — a CPI-or-2% cap, no vacancy decontrol, a new-build exemption — and the paper shows it approximates the welfare-optimal path, bound to pre-committed, self-terminating exit criteria (sustained ~300,000 net completions and a bottom-quintile cost-burden threshold) rather than open-ended discretion.

A welfare foundation aligned with your mission. Building on the value theory of Reproductive Capacity as Numéraire (whose conceptual seed is due to Julio Huato), I derived a companion — Rent as Reproduction Cost — that grounds the argument in the physical reproduction of the working population rather than subjective utility. It reframes high rents as a deduction from households’ capacity to sustain and form themselves across generations, turning a CPI-anchored cap from an affordability patch into a structural correction with a coherent welfare rationale.

The five abstracts — one for each paper — are collected in the Research Portfolio. If you only wanted the fastest route through the research, I would suggest starting with Breaking the Benchmark, then Fiscal Capture, then Rent as Reproduction Cost. Nothing in this letter depends on your opening them; they are there only if you would like the shape of each argument in a minute rather than a full manuscript.

The one place this process led me into genuinely contentious territory is exactly that durability question. The second-best argument disarms the orthodox veto robustly for the temporary emergency brake — but extending the case to a permanent fair-rents regime is the harder, more contested step, because the emergency rationale lapses as the underlying distortions are addressed, and a durable regime therefore needs a different and more demanding foundation than the brake itself supplies. I did not set out to find that fault line; multiple independent roads led me to it. I flag it not to lecture but because locating precisely where an argument becomes contestable is the most useful thing a defender can do — and because it is the first of many nuances on which I expect your experience to correct and deepen mine.

That is the spirit in which I apply: confident in the analytical contribution, but without illusions about how much I still have to learn. That is precisely why I am applying for a relatively junior role — to learn, over time and from seasoned colleagues, the institutional, political, and practical nuances my outside-in work almost certainly underestimates. I agree with your position and want to help strengthen it from within, not argue with it — to supply the technical half of NEF’s own method: arming from the top the case your organising builds from below.

My toolkit (MATLAB/DYNARE, STATA, Python, R, EViews) and my capacity to deliver work that holds up under scrutiny1 are ready to support NEF’s research priorities immediately. I have already identified the next empirical gaps — economic-rent-share estimation and transaction-level search-friction markups — that NEF’s proposed PRS-database expansion is positioned to close. What I bring is rigour that lowers institutional risk and a housing programme that already shows I can deliver — and a genuine hunger to learn the rest from you.

If you prefer a role-mapped profile snapshot, use the Short CV (and Full Glory CV for complete detail).

I would welcome the chance to discuss how I can support NEF’s mission.

Sincerely,

Carlos Galindo Escajeda
